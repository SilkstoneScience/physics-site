The speed of sound from an echo

A lab with Vernier sensors: snap your fingers at the open end of a long tube, and a microphone records the snap and its echo from the closed far end, a few milliseconds later. C.2 Wave model Vernier

Aim: to measure the time between a snap and its echo for at least five tube lengths; to determine the speed of sound in air, with its uncertainty, from the gradient of a graph of echo time against round-trip distance; and to compare your value with the value expected at the room temperature, evaluating the random and systematic effects in the experiment.

1. Skills you will practise

  • Using a sensor at a high sampling rate, with triggering, to time an event that lasts only a few milliseconds.
  • Reading times from a trace consistently, and judging a realistic uncertainty that is larger than the sampling interval.
  • Using repeated trials and the half-range method, (max − min)/2, to estimate uncertainty.
  • Plotting a graph with uncertainty bars, and using its gradient (with the steepest and shallowest lines) and its intercept to find a result and reveal a systematic offset.
  • Comparing an experimental result with an expected value, and evaluating the systematic effects that repeated trials cannot reveal.

Tools and inquiry: measuring length, time and temperature (Tool 1); collecting data with sensors, and processing it with graphs (Tool 2); collecting and processing data, and concluding and evaluating (Inquiry 2 and 3).

2. Background

Sound is a longitudinal mechanical wave. A source pushes and pulls on the air next to it, making regions of higher pressure (compressions) and lower pressure (rarefactions) that travel outwards.

Each air molecule only oscillates back and forth about its average position, parallel to the direction the wave travels. So the wave transfers energy without carrying the air along with it. Sound needs a medium, so it cannot travel through a vacuum. (See C.2 Wave model.)

Sound travels at about $340\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ in air, so over a few metres the travel time is only a few thousandths of a second: far too short for a stopwatch. A microphone and a computer sampling ten thousand times a second can measure it.

In this lab you make a sharp sound (a snap of the fingers) at the open end of a long tube, a few centimetres wide, whose far end is closed. The microphone at the open end records the snap as it happens. The pulse then travels down the tube, reflects from the closed end and comes back as an echo.

In the time $t$ between the snap and its echo, the pulse travels to the end and back: a distance $2L$, where $L$ is the length of the tube. So

$$v = \frac{2L}{t}$$

which rearranges to

$$t = \frac{1}{v} \times 2L$$

A graph of echo time $t$ against the round-trip distance $2L$ should be a straight line through the origin with gradient = $1/v$. Using several tube lengths, rather than one, lets the graph separate the speed (the gradient) from any constant timing offset (the intercept).

A snap is a short pulse, not a single note, but it is made of many frequencies. Each of them obeys the data-booklet wave equation

$$v = f\lambda$$

and all frequencies of sound travel at the same speed in air.

The speed of sound in air depends on the temperature. A useful approximation is $v \approx 331 + 0.6\theta$, where $v$ is in $\text{m s}^{-1}$ and $\theta$ is the air temperature in °C. This is not in the syllabus or the data booklet: it is given here only so that you have an expected value. At 20 °C it gives about $343\ \text{m s}^{-1}$.

3. Equipment and safety

  • Vernier interface (or LabQuest) and a laptop with Logger Pro 3
  • Vernier Microphone, on a small clamp or ring stand
  • A tube 3–10 cm wide and about 3 m long, closed at the far end: for example plastic drain or PVC pipe in 1 m sections with push-fit couplers, or cardboard postal tubes joined with tape
  • A close-fitting plug (a foam or rubber disc on a long dowel rod) or an end cap. To reach every length with the plug alone, the rod must be about 2.2 m long; a shorter rod (about 0.7 m) works if you also remove a section for each metre.
  • Scissors and tape
  • Tape measure or metre rules with millimetre divisions
  • Thermometer or Vernier Temperature Probe (for the room temperature)

Safety:

  • Support the pipe sections on the bench so that they cannot roll off, and keep the open end of the pipe and the end of the dowel rod away from faces.
  • Join and separate the pipe sections gently: cut cardboard and plastic edges can be sharp.
  • A 3 m tube along the bench can catch on bags and sleeves. Keep it on the bench and away from walkways, and keep the microphone cable tidy.
  • Make the sound with a finger snap or a gentle click. Do not clap or bang objects right next to anyone's ear.

4. Before the lab

Uncertainties

Tube length $L$: a tape measure has millimetre divisions, but the tube may not be perfectly straight, and you must judge where the face of the plug is inside the tube (mark the dowel rod to help). A realistic uncertainty is ± 0.5 cm, so the round-trip distance $2L$ is uncertain by ± 1 cm. Measure $L$ for each set-up, rather than adding up the section lengths: couplers and overlaps change it.

Echo time $t$: at 10 000 samples per second the points are 0.1 ms apart, so no time can be read better than ± 0.1 ms. In practice, deciding exactly where each pulse begins is harder than that, especially for the echo, which is smaller and more spread out than the snap. Repeat each length five times and use the half range, $\frac{\text{max} - \text{min}}{2}$, of the five echo times. If that comes out smaller than ± 0.2 ms, use ± 0.2 ms.

Temperature $\theta$: read the room temperature at the start and the end of the lab. The expected value of $v$ changes by only $0.6\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ per °C, so a ± 0.5 °C uncertainty in $\theta$ hardly matters.

Quote each uncertainty to 1 significant figure (usual), or 2 when the uncertainty itself is known precisely, and round each value to the same decimal place. See Physics skills.

5. Setting up

The tube

  1. Join the pipe sections end to end to make a tube about 3 m long, pushing each joint fully home so that it is airtight (wrap cardboard joints with tape). Lay the tube straight along the bench on its supports (see the diagram).
  2. Close the far end: push the plug on its dowel rod in from the far end until it is about 3.0 m from the open end (or fit an end cap). Mark the rod where it leaves the tube, so that you can set the plug at other lengths later.
  3. Measure L from the open end to the face of the plug (or the inside of the end cap), and record it in the first column of the readings table (section 6). Record the room temperature below.

Before you start

Logger Pro 3

  1. Connect the Vernier Microphone to CH 1 of the interface before you start Logger Pro 3 (the sensor's settings only appear once it is connected). Logger Pro should show a graph of sound pressure (arbitrary units) against time.
  2. Choose Experiment ▸ Data Collection. On the Collection tab set the Mode to Time Based, the Duration to 0.05 s and the sampling rate to 10 000 samples/second.
  3. Click Collect without making a sound, to see the background noise. Note how far the trace wanders from zero.
  4. Choose Experiment ▸ Data Collection again and open the Triggering tab. Tick the box to enable triggering, and set it to start collecting when the sound pressure is increasing across a level just above the background noise (for example 0.1, if the noise stays within ± 0.05). Set about 20 points to be collected before the trigger, so that the very start of the snap is recorded.
  5. Clamp the microphone so that its head is about 1 cm from the open end of the tube and points at the opening (see the diagram). Keep the microphone in this position for the whole experiment.

6. Collecting data

  1. Click Collect. When Logger Pro shows it is waiting for the trigger, snap your fingers right beside the opening of the tube. The trace should show a large, short burst (the snap), then quiet, then a smaller burst (the echo), as in the sketch below. If you see no clear echo, check the joints and the plug for leaks, and snap closer to the opening.
  2. Zoom in on the snap and the echo (drag a box around them and click the Zoom In button). Choose Analyze ▸ Examine and move the cursor to the point where the snap first rises clearly above the background noise; note that time. Do the same for the echo. The echo time is $t = t_\text{echo} - t_\text{snap}$: record it in the readings table, in ms. Use the same rule for both pulses every time.
  3. Repeat steps 9–10 until you have five good trials for this length. Reject a trial only if there is a clear reason (another noise in the room, an echo you cannot pick out), and say so in your notes.
  4. Move the plug about 50 cm closer to the open end by pulling the dowel rod back (or remove a pipe section and refit the end cap). Measure the new length L, and repeat steps 9–11. Continue until you have at least five lengths between about 3.0 m and 1.0 m. Do not move the microphone.
  5. Record the room temperature again at the end, and save your Logger Pro file.

At the end

7. Your graph

The graph updates as you type. It starts with the mean echo time $t$ on the vertical axis and the round-trip distance $2L$ on the horizontal axis, so the gradient of the line of best fit is $1/v$. The tool does not force the line through the origin: the intercept is part of your result.

The graph tool uses one uncertainty for every point on each axis. Type your largest Δt in the vertical axis's uncertainty box (a cautious choice) and 0.01 (m) in the horizontal axis's box. The horizontal bars will be tiny: say so in your report rather than leaving them out. Then tick Steepest and shallowest lines: they pass through every uncertainty bar. The tool gives the gradient ± its uncertainty, which is half the difference between the gradients of the steepest and shallowest lines, and the y-intercept ± its uncertainty. Press Add this graph to my report to keep it.

The gradient is in ms m⁻¹. The speed is $v = 1/\text{gradient}$, so

$$v\ (\text{in m s}^{-1}) = \frac{1000}{\text{gradient}}$$

with the same percentage uncertainty: $\dfrac{\Delta v}{v} = \dfrac{\Delta\text{gradient}}{\text{gradient}}$. For example, a gradient of (2.95 ± 0.07) ms m⁻¹ gives $v = 339\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ with a 2.4 % uncertainty, so $v = (339 \pm 8)\ \text{m s}^{-1}$.
