Single-slit diffraction HL

A lab with Vernier sensors: a light sensor scans the pattern a laser makes after passing through a narrow slit, and the positions of the dark fringes give you the width of the slit. C.3 Wave phenomena Vernier

HL only: single-slit diffraction is in the additional higher level content of C.3. The first part (comparing single- and double-slit patterns) is useful for SL students too.

Aim: to test the theory of single-slit diffraction, and to use it to measure the width of a slit.

1. Skills you will practise

  • Judging realistic uncertainties, not just an instrument's resolution.
  • Rearranging a relationship to get a straight-line graph, and explaining its gradient and intercept.
  • Finding the uncertainty in a gradient from the steepest and shallowest lines.
  • Propagating uncertainties, and comparing a result with an accepted value.

Tools and inquiry: measuring length and light intensity, and working safely with a laser (Tool 1); collecting data with sensors, and processing it with graphs (Tool 2); collecting and processing data, and concluding and evaluating (Inquiry 2 and 3).

2. Theory: where are the dark fringes?

Treat every point across the slit as a source of wavelets (Huygens' principle). At some angles, the wavelets from the top half of the slit arrive exactly out of step with those from the bottom half and cancel: a dark fringe. For a slit of width $b$, the first dark fringe is at an angle $\theta$ given by

$$\theta = \frac{\lambda}{b}$$

(for small angles, in radians: see C.3 Wave phenomena). The other dark fringes follow the same pattern: the $n$th dark fringe is at $\theta_n \approx n\lambda/b$. On a sensor a distance $D$ from the slit, the $n$th dark fringe is a distance $y_n \approx D\theta_n$ from the centre, so

$$y_n = \frac{\lambda D}{b}\,n$$

The dark fringes should be equally spaced. The sensor measures positions $x_n$ from wherever it started, not from the centre, so

$$x_n = \frac{\lambda D}{b}\,n + x_0$$

A graph of $x_n$ against $n$ should be a straight line with gradient $\lambda D/b$. Its intercept $x_0$ is the position of the centre of the pattern, so you never need to find the centre yourself.

3. Equipment and laser safety

  • Vernier interface and a laptop with Logger Pro 3
  • Vernier Optics Expansion Kit: High Sensitivity Light Sensor and Linear Position Sensor
  • Vernier Diffraction Apparatus (laser and slit plate) on the Vernier Dynamics Track
  • A metre rule, and a green laser if one is available

Laser safety: use only the school's low-power (Class 2) laser. Never look into the beam or point it at anyone, however weak it is, and keep your eyes above the height of the beam: don't bend down to look along it. Switch the laser on only when everything is in place, and watch for reflections from shiny surfaces, including the slit plate, watches and jewellery.

4. Before the lab

Uncertainties

  • Distance $D$ from the slit to the sensor: judge how precisely you can line up the slit and the sensor's opening with the track's scale. This is usually more than the scale's smallest division.
  • Wavelength $\lambda$: read it, with its tolerance, from the laser's label, or ask your teacher.
  • Position $x_n$ of each dark fringe: the minima are broad. Move the cursor across each one and note the range of positions where the intensity looks as low as it gets. Half of that range is a sensible uncertainty.

Give uncertainties to 1 significant figure (2 if the uncertainty itself is known precisely), and round each value to the same decimal place. See Physics skills.

5. Observing the patterns

  1. Fix the laser at one end of the track, facing along it. Connect its power supply, but leave it switched off.
  2. Turn the slit plate to a single slit of width 0.08 mm. Fix it to the track about 10 cm from the laser, with its shiny side towards the laser.
  3. Fix the light sensor and the linear position sensor at the other end of the track, with the light sensor facing the slit.
  4. Switch the laser on. Adjust it sideways until the pattern is brightest, and up or down until the pattern is level with the light sensor's opening. Do this again every time you change the slit.
  5. Slide the sensor so that the pattern falls on the white screen beside its opening, and describe the pattern below.
  6. Change to a double slit (slit width 0.08 mm, separation 0.25 mm). Line up the laser again and describe the pattern. Switch the laser off.

7. Part 2: scanning the single-slit pattern

  1. Connect the light sensor and the linear position sensor to the interface and open Logger Pro 3.
  2. Choose the single slit of width 0.08 mm and line up the laser as before.
  3. Measure $D$, the distance from the slit to the light sensor, and record it with $\lambda$ and the slit width the plate states, each with its uncertainty.
  4. Fit the 0.5 mm opening to the light sensor. Set its sensitivity so that the top of the central maximum is not flat (a flat top means the sensor is overloaded). Your teacher may suggest a setting.
  5. Slide the sensor all the way to the right (as seen from the laser) and choose Experiment ▸ Zero for both sensors.
  6. Click Collect, then slide the sensor smoothly to the other end, taking at least 20 seconds and moving more slowly across the bright parts. Click Stop when it reaches the end.
  7. Choose Experiment ▸ Store Latest Run, then collect a second run, sliding the other way. The two traces should lie on top of each other; if they don't, repeat more smoothly.
  8. Zoom in, and use Analyze ▸ Examine to find the position $x_n$ of the middle of the first three dark fringes on each side, $n = -3$ to $+3$. Move the cursor either side of each minimum to judge its uncertainty. Record them in the table.
  9. Save the file. Repeat steps 2–8 with a second slit width, changing the sensor's opening if you need to see more detail. Switch the laser off when you finish.

Note: light reflected from the front of the slit plate can make a sharp dip at the very top of the central maximum. It doesn't change where the dark fringes are.
