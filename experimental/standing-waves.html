Standing waves on a string

A lab with a signal generator and a vibration generator: a string stretched over a pulley by a hanging mass vibrates in loops when the driving frequency is just right. C.4 Standing waves and resonance

Aim: to set up the first harmonic and at least the next five harmonics on a string and observe their nodes and antinodes; to test the model for the harmonics with a graph of resonant frequency against harmonic number and find the wave speed $v$ with its uncertainty; to check $v$ a second way by changing the vibrating length; and to see qualitatively how the tension affects the wave speed.

1. Skills you will practise

  • Judging a resonance by eye, and turning that judgement into a realistic uncertainty: half the range of frequencies over which the pattern looks largest.
  • Measuring a length to points that are not perfectly defined (nodes), and deciding how large its uncertainty really is.
  • Using a straight-line graph through the origin to test a model, and finding a physical quantity from its gradient.
  • Finding the uncertainty in a gradient with the steepest and shallowest lines, and combining percentage uncertainties.
  • Checking a result in two independent ways and comparing them.

Tools and inquiry: measuring length, mass and frequency (Tool 1); processing data with graphs (Tool 2); propagating uncertainties and finding the uncertainty in a gradient (Tool 3); collecting and processing data, and concluding and evaluating (Inquiry 2 and 3).

2. Background

When a wave travels along a string and reflects from a fixed end, the reflected wave travels back through the oncoming wave. Two identical waves travelling in opposite directions superpose to give a standing wave.

Some points, the nodes, never move. Halfway between neighbouring nodes are the antinodes, which oscillate with the largest amplitude. Unlike a travelling wave, a standing wave does not carry energy along the string. (See C.4 Nodes, antinodes, amplitude and phase.)

In this lab one end of the string is tied to the spike of a vibration generator, and the other passes over a pulley to a hanging mass, which keeps the tension constant. Both the spike and the pulley act (almost) as fixed ends, so both are nodes.

A standing wave can only form if a whole number of loops fits into the vibrating length $L$. Each loop is half a wavelength, so for the $n$th harmonic ($n$ loops):

$$L = \frac{n\lambda}{2} \qquad\text{so}\qquad \lambda_n = \frac{2L}{n}$$

The lowest-frequency mode, with one loop, is the first harmonic ($n = 1$). Using the wave equation from the data booklet, $v = f\lambda$, the frequency of the $n$th harmonic is

$$f_n = \frac{v}{\lambda_n} = n\,\frac{v}{2L}$$

The wave speed $v$ depends on the string and its tension, not on the frequency. So with $L$ and the tension fixed, a graph of $f_n$ against $n$ should be a straight line through the origin with gradient $= \dfrac{v}{2L}$. So $v = 2L \times \text{gradient}$.

In Part 2 you keep the tension fixed and change $L$, always using the first harmonic. Then $f_1 = \dfrac{v}{2} \times \dfrac{1}{L}$, so a graph of $f_1$ against $1/L$ should be a straight line through the origin with gradient $= \dfrac{v}{2}$.

The string only shows a large standing wave when the driving frequency matches one of these natural frequencies: this is resonance. At other frequencies the string barely moves. Energy is lost to the air and at the pulley (damping), so each resonance is spread over a small range of frequencies rather than one exact value, and you will need to judge where the amplitude is largest. (See C.4 Natural frequency and resonance.)

3. Equipment and safety

  • Signal generator (sine wave output, with a frequency display) and two connecting leads
  • Mechanical vibration generator
  • About 2 m of thin, non-stretchy string (braided string or thin cord); elastic cord also works for Parts 1 and 2
  • Pulley on a bench clamp
  • Mass hanger and slotted masses (100 g and 200 g in total for Part 3)
  • Metre rule (and a second metre rule or a tape measure if L is longer than 1 m)
  • Foam cushion, or a box of crumpled paper, under the hanging mass
  • Optional: a small folded paper rider, to find nodes; a phone with a sound spectrum (frequency analyser) app, or a multimeter with a frequency (Hz) setting

Safety:

  • The hanging mass is over the edge of the bench: put a cushion under it, keep feet and bags clear, and clamp the pulley firmly. Use no more than the masses your teacher allows.
  • A stretched string can snap or slip off and whip back. Keep your face out of line with the string and do not lean over it to look along it. Add or remove masses gently, with the generator switched off.
  • Start with the signal generator's amplitude low and turn it up only as far as you need. A large amplitude makes the vibration generator hot and can make the string jump off the pulley. Switch the output off between readings.
  • The signal generator is mains-powered: keep water and drinks away, and check its leads are undamaged.

4. Before the lab

Uncertainties

Resonant frequency $f$: the display may read to 0.1 Hz (or 1 Hz), but you cannot judge "largest amplitude" that precisely, so the judgement, not the display, sets the uncertainty.

For each harmonic, find the lowest and highest frequencies at which the antinodes look as large as they get. Take the middle as $f$ and half the range as $\Delta f$. For example, if the antinodes look largest from 59.4 Hz to 60.6 Hz, $f = (60.0 \pm 0.6)$ Hz. Approach each resonance from below and from above.

Frequency display: the number on the display is not guaranteed to be the true frequency. Some generators are only accurate to a few percent, and an analogue dial is worse. This is a systematic effect: every reading is out by the same factor, so it changes the gradient but not the scatter. You can check it (see Extension E1).

Vibrating length $L$: with a metre rule this needs two readings, so ± 0.1 cm by the usual rule. But the ends are not perfect nodes: the spike moves a little (it is driving the string), and it is hard to see exactly where the string first touches the pulley. A realistic uncertainty is about ± 0.5 cm. Say in your report why you chose your value.

Harmonic number $n$ is a counted whole number, so it has no uncertainty: the $f$–$n$ graph needs vertical uncertainty bars only.

Quote each uncertainty to 1 significant figure (2 if the uncertainty itself is known precisely), with the value rounded to the same decimal place. Every point on your graphs gets uncertainty bars. See Physics skills.

5. Setting up

  1. Clamp the pulley to the edge of the bench. Stand the vibration generator on the bench about 0.9 m from the pulley, with its spike pointing up, in line with the pulley. Tie one end of the string to the spike and pass the other end over the pulley.
  2. Tie the mass hanger to the free end and load it to 100 g in total. Check that the hanger hangs freely, well above the cushion, and that the string between spike and pulley is horizontal and does not touch anything.
  3. Measure the vibrating length L from the point where the string leaves the spike to the point where it first touches the top of the pulley. If you use elastic cord, measure L only after the mass is hung, because the cord stretches. Record L and the hanging mass below, and work out the tension.
  4. Connect the vibration generator to the low-impedance (power) output of the signal generator. Choose a sine wave, set the frequency to about 5 Hz and the amplitude to its lowest setting, then switch on and turn the amplitude up until the spike visibly vibrates.

Fixed quantities for Part 1 (g = 9.8 m s⁻²)

6. Part 1: the harmonics at fixed length and tension

  1. Increase the frequency slowly until the string forms one large loop: the first harmonic. Wait a few seconds after each small change for the pattern to settle. Find the lowest and highest frequencies at which the antinode looks as large as it gets, and record both in the table.
  2. If the loop is so large that the string slaps or jumps, turn the amplitude down a little, then leave the amplitude setting alone for the rest of Part 1, so that resonances are compared fairly.
  3. Predict the frequency of the 2nd harmonic from your first result. Increase the frequency to near your prediction and find its frequency range in the same way. Sketch the pattern and count the loops to confirm n.
  4. Repeat for the 3rd, 4th, 5th and 6th harmonics (more if you can still see clear loops). Each time, check that the number of loops matches n. If you see a large vibration at a frequency that does not fit the pattern, note it and look closely: it may be the vibration generator's own resonance, or a sideways (horizontal) vibration of the string.
  5. For the highest harmonic you reached, measure the distance between two interior nodes that are several loops apart (well away from the spike), and divide by the number of loops between them to find λ/2. A light paper rider slides to rest at a node and jumps at an antinode, which helps. Record this below the table.
  6. Go back to the 3rd harmonic and watch the string closely (or film it in slow motion with a phone). Note how points in the same loop move compared with points in neighbouring loops.

Fill in the last four columns for each harmonic: $f$ is the middle of your range, $\Delta f$ is half the range, $\lambda_n = 2L/n$ and $v = f_n\lambda_n$.

7. Part 2: changing the length at fixed tension

  1. Keep the 100 g load. Slide the vibration generator towards the pulley to make L about 0.80 m. The hanger drops as you do this: if it gets near the cushion, switch off, lift the hanger, and retie the string or wind the spare string around the hanger's hook. Measure the new L.
  2. Find the frequency range of the first harmonic for this length and record it in the table.
  3. Repeat for at least five lengths between about 0.90 m and 0.40 m.

  1. Return to a length of about 0.80 m. Find the first harmonic with 100 g on the hanger. Then switch off, gently add masses to make 200 g, switch on and find the first harmonic again. Record both below, and note whether the frequency rose or fell.

9. Your graphs

Each graph updates as you type. The graph tool uses one uncertainty for every point on each axis, so type the largest one (a cautious choice), or a percentage with a % sign when every point has the same percentage uncertainty. Then tick Steepest and shallowest lines: they pass through every uncertainty bar, and the tool gives the gradient ± its uncertainty and the y-intercept ± its uncertainty. Press Add this graph to my report to keep each graph.

Graph 1: f against n (Part 1)

The graph starts with resonant frequency on the vertical axis and harmonic number on the horizontal axis. Type your largest Δf in the vertical axis's uncertainty box. Leave the horizontal axis's box empty: n is counted, so it has no uncertainty. The line is not forced through the origin: whether the y-intercept is zero within its uncertainty is part of the test of the model.

The gradient is $v/2L$, so $v = 2L \times \text{gradient}$. Its percentage uncertainty is the percentage uncertainty in the gradient plus the percentage uncertainty in $L$.

Graph 2: f1 against 1/L (Part 2)

The graph starts with first-harmonic frequency on the vertical axis and 1/L on the horizontal axis. Type your largest Δf1 in the vertical axis's box. Here both quantities have uncertainties: $\Delta(1/L) = \dfrac{\Delta L}{L} \times \dfrac{1}{L}$, so 1/L has the same percentage uncertainty as L. Type the largest percentage uncertainty in L (at your shortest length) in the horizontal axis's box, with a % sign, for example 1.3%. The steepest and shallowest lines then pass through every uncertainty box.

The gradient is $v/2$, so $v = 2 \times \text{gradient}$ and $\Delta v = 2 \times \Delta(\text{gradient})$. (The tool writes the gradient's unit as Hz / m⁻¹, which is the same as $\text{m s}^{-1}$.)
