The force on a current-carrying wire

A lab with a current balance: a stiff wire is clamped between the poles of a magnet that stands on a top-pan balance, and the change in the balance reading measures the tiny magnetic force on the wire. D.3 Motion in electromagnetic fields

Aim: to measure the force on a current-carrying wire in a magnetic field for a range of currents, using a top-pan balance; to use a graph of force $F$ against current $I$ to test $F \propto I$ and to determine the magnetic field strength $B$ between the poles, with its uncertainty; to check that reversing the current reverses the force, using the left-hand rule to predict its direction; and to evaluate what the "length of wire in the field" really is, and how heating affects the readings.

1. Skills you will practise

  • Measuring a very small force indirectly, as a change in a balance reading, and converting it with $F = \Delta m\, g$.
  • Using reversed readings to cancel a systematic effect (drift caused by heating).
  • Deciding a realistic uncertainty for a length that is not sharply defined (the length of wire that is really in the field).
  • Using a straight-line graph through the origin to find a physical quantity from its gradient.
  • Finding the uncertainty in a gradient with steepest and shallowest lines, and combining percentage uncertainties.

Tools and inquiry: measuring mass, length and current (Tool 1); processing data with a graph (Tool 2); propagating uncertainties and finding the uncertainty in a gradient (Tool 3); collecting and processing data, and concluding and evaluating (Inquiry 2 and 3).

2. Background

A wire carrying a current in a magnetic field experiences a force. The data booklet gives its size as

$$F = BIL\sin\theta$$

where $B$ is the magnetic field strength (in tesla, T), $I$ is the current, $L$ is the length of wire inside the field and $\theta$ is the angle between the wire and the field. In this lab the wire is perpendicular to the field, so $\theta = 90°$, $\sin\theta = 1$ and

$$F = BIL$$

The force is perpendicular to both the wire and the field. Its direction is given by the left-hand rule: first finger along the field, second finger along the current, thumb along the force. Reversing the current reverses the force. (See D.3 The force on a current-carrying wire.)

This force is far too small to see directly, so we use a current balance. The magnet sits on a top-pan balance, and the wire is clamped rigidly so that it passes between the poles without touching the magnet.

By Newton's third law, if the field pushes the wire with a force $F$, the wire pushes the magnet with an equal and opposite force. The clamp stops the wire from moving, but the magnet is free to press harder on (or lift off) the balance, so the balance reading changes by $\Delta m$. The force is

$$F = \Delta m\, g$$

with $\Delta m$ in kg and $g = 9.8\ \text{m s}^{-2}$.

If $B$ and $L$ stay constant, $F$ is directly proportional to $I$. A graph of $F$ (vertical) against $I$ (horizontal) should be a straight line through the origin with gradient = $BL$, so

$$B = \frac{\text{gradient}}{L}$$

The forces are tiny. A school magnet has a field of a few hundredths of a tesla between its poles, and its pole faces are a few centimetres long. With $B = 0.04$ T and $L = 0.05$ m, a current of 1 A gives $F = 2$ mN, which changes the balance reading by only about 0.2 g. A balance that reads to 0.01 g is essential.

3. Equipment and safety

  • Top-pan digital balance reading to 0.01 g
  • U-shaped magnet: a horseshoe magnet, or a pair of Magnadur magnets on a steel yoke
  • Wooden block or upturned plastic beaker, to raise the magnet a few centimetres above the pan
  • Stiff straight copper wire or rigid copper rod (bare, about 1.5–3 mm diameter, about 30 cm long)
  • Clamp stand(s) with an insulated or wooden clamp; an aluminium stand is best (keep steel away from the magnet)
  • Low-voltage d.c. supply able to give at least 4 A (with a current limit if it has one)
  • Rheostat (variable resistor): check its rating before planning 4 A, since many school rheostats take only 1–2.5 A; if so, stop at its rated current
  • Ammeter, or a digital multimeter on its 10 A range (check the meter's time limit on this range: many allow only 10–30 s, then need a rest)
  • Switch (or a reversing switch, if available) and thick connecting leads with crocodile clips
  • Ruler or vernier calipers, to measure the pole faces
  • Optional (Extension): a second identical magnet; a compass; Vernier Magnetic Field Sensor with the interface and Logger Pro 3

Safety:

  • The rod has a very small resistance, so the rheostat must always be in the circuit to limit the current. Without it the supply is almost short-circuited.
  • At several amperes the rod, the leads and especially the rheostat get hot. Close the switch only for the few seconds needed to take a reading, and do not touch the rod or the rheostat's coil straight after a reading. Keep the current below the largest value the rheostat is made for, and within the ammeter's range and time limit.
  • Strong magnets can pinch fingers and snap onto steel objects. Keep phones, bank cards and watches away from them. Anyone with a pacemaker or other medical implant must not handle the magnets.
  • Use only a low-voltage supply. Switch off before changing any connection.

4. Before the lab

Uncertainties

Change in balance reading $\Delta m$: a single reading from the balance is ± 0.01 g (the smallest digit). But the reading also flickers and drifts, because the rod warms and heats the air around the magnet.

At each current you will take four readings (two with the current one way, two reversed). Use the half range of their sizes, $\frac{\text{max} - \text{min}}{2}$, as the uncertainty, and never less than 0.01 g. The uncertainty in the force is then $\Delta F = g \times \Delta(\Delta m)$; 0.01 g corresponds to about 0.1 mN.

Current $I$: a digital ammeter is ± its smallest digit (often 0.01 A on the 10 A range). The current also falls slightly while the switch is closed, as the rod and rheostat heat up and their resistance rises. If the reading changes while you watch, use the half range of what you see as the uncertainty.

Length $L$: measure the length of the pole faces along the direction of the wire with a ruler (± 0.1 cm for two readings, or better with calipers). But the field does not stop sharply at the edges of the poles: it spreads out a little beyond them, so the wire feels a force over a slightly longer length. This effect is much larger than the ruler's resolution.

A realistic uncertainty is about ± 10 % of the pole length (for example $L = (5.0 \pm 0.5)$ cm). For a horseshoe magnet with narrow, rounded poles, the effective length is even less certain.

Quote each uncertainty to 1 significant figure (the usual choice), or to 2 significant figures when the uncertainty itself is known precisely, and round the value to the same decimal place. Every point on your graph gets uncertainty bars. See Physics skills.

5. Setting up

  1. Place the wooden block on the balance pan and the magnet on top of it, poles upwards. (Raising the magnet stops its field from disturbing the balance's own mechanism.) Make sure the magnet is in the middle of the pan and cannot slide.
  2. Measure the length L of the pole faces along the direction the wire will run, and the width of the gap between the poles. Record them below.
  3. Clamp the rod horizontally so that it passes through the middle of the gap, perpendicular to the field (along the gap, parallel to the pole faces), with its middle section centred over the pole faces. It must not touch the magnet, the block or the balance at any point. Check by gently tapping the bench: the balance reading should settle back to the same value.
  4. Connect the circuit in series: supply, switch, rheostat, ammeter and the rod. Run the leads straight away from the magnet so that they are not in its field. Set the rheostat to its maximum resistance and the supply to about 6 V. Ask your teacher to check the circuit.
  5. With the switch open, press Tare (or Zero) so that the balance reads 0.00 g with the magnet and block on it.
  6. Use the left-hand rule to predict whether the balance reading should go up or down for your current direction. Close the switch briefly at a small current and check your prediction. Record the direction of the current (for example "left to right") as the forward direction.

The magnet and the balance (g = 9.8 m s⁻²)

6. Collecting data

  1. Adjust the rheostat to give a current of about 0.5 A. Close the switch, wait until the reading settles (a few seconds), then read the ammeter and the change in balance reading at the same moment (one partner reads each). Open the switch. Record both in the table, with the sign of Δm.
  2. Wait about 20 s with the switch open. Check that the balance has returned to 0.00 g; if it has not, Tare it again before the next reading. Repeat step 7 for a second forward reading.
  3. Reverse the current: switch off and swap the two leads at the supply terminals (or use the reversing switch). Take two readings in the reversed direction in the same way, adjusting the rheostat so that the ammeter shows the same current as before. Note that Δm now changes the other way.
  4. Repeat steps 7–9 for at least six currents spread evenly from about 0.5 A to about 4 A (or the largest current your rheostat and supply are rated for). Take the currents in a mixed order, not always increasing, so that the slow warming of the apparatus does not quietly bend your line.
  5. At the end, switch off and leave everything to cool. Do not touch the rod or the rheostat until they have cooled.

After the lab, fill in the rest of each row:

  • the four sizes |Δm|: the same four numbers without any minus signs (use only these from now on);
  • the mean |Δm| of the four sizes, and its uncertainty Δ(Δm) = (max − min)/2 of the sizes, but at least 0.01 g;
  • the force in mN: $F$ = mean |Δm| (in g) × 9.8 gives mN directly, because $1\ \text{g} \times 9.8\ \text{m s}^{-2} = 9.8 \times 10^{-3}$ N;
  • in the same way, ΔF = Δ(Δm) × 9.8.

7. Your graph

The graph updates as you type. It starts with force F on the vertical axis and current I on the horizontal axis, so the gradient of the line is $BL$. Fill in the F column first: the graph uses your calculated forces, not the raw readings.

The graph tool uses one uncertainty for every point on each axis, so type the largest value from your ΔF column in the vertical axis's uncertainty box and the largest from your ΔI column in the horizontal axis's box (a cautious choice). Keep Start the axes at zero ticked, so you can see whether the line passes through the origin. The line is not forced through the origin: whether the y-intercept is zero within its uncertainty is part of the test of $F \propto I$. Then tick Steepest and shallowest lines: they pass through every uncertainty bar, and the tool gives the gradient ± its uncertainty and the y-intercept ± its uncertainty. Press Add this graph to my report to keep it.

The tool gives the gradient in mN / A (the same as mN A⁻¹). Convert it to $\text{N A}^{-1}$ (divide by 1000), then calculate $B = \text{gradient}/L$ with $L$ in metres. The percentage uncertainty in $B$ is the percentage uncertainty in the gradient plus the percentage uncertainty in $L$.
