Snell's law and the critical angle

A lab with a ray box: trace a narrow ray of light through a rectangular glass or Perspex block to test Snell's law, then find the critical angle of a semicircular block. C.3 Wave phenomena

Aim: to test Snell's law and determine the refractive index $n$ of a block, with its uncertainty, from a graph of $\sin\theta_{\text{air}}$ against $\sin\theta_{\text{block}}$; to measure the critical angle $\theta_c$ of a semicircular block and use $\sin\theta_c = 1/n$ to find $n$ a second, independent way; and to compare the two values and evaluate the main sources of uncertainty in each method.

2. Theory: Snell's law and the critical angle

When light reaches the boundary between two transparent media, part of it is reflected and the rest is transmitted. If it meets the boundary at an angle, the transmitted ray changes direction: this is refraction. It happens because light travels at a different speed in each medium.

All angles are measured between the ray and the normal, the line at 90° to the boundary.

The data booklet gives Snell's law as

$$\frac{n_1}{n_2} = \frac{\sin\theta_2}{\sin\theta_1} = \frac{v_2}{v_1}$$

where $n$ is the refractive index of each medium, $\theta$ is the angle between the ray and the normal in that medium, and $v$ is the speed of light in it. The same law, written without fractions, is $n_1 \sin\theta_1 = n_2 \sin\theta_2$. (See C.3 Refraction and Snell's law.)

In this lab one medium is air ($n = 1.00$) and the other is a block of refractive index $n$. Calling the angle in air $\theta_{\text{air}}$ and the angle in the block $\theta_{\text{block}}$, Snell's law becomes

$$\sin\theta_{\text{air}} = n \sin\theta_{\text{block}}$$

This is true whichever way the light travels. So a graph of $\sin\theta_{\text{air}}$ (vertical) against $\sin\theta_{\text{block}}$ (horizontal) should be a straight line through the origin with gradient $n$.

When light travels from the block towards air, it bends away from the normal. As the angle in the block increases, the ray in air gets closer and closer to the boundary. At the critical angle $\theta_c$, the angle in air reaches 90° and the ray just skims along the surface.

At any larger angle no light leaves: all of it is reflected back into the block. This is total internal reflection. Putting $\theta_{\text{block}} = \theta_c$ and $\theta_{\text{air}} = 90°$ into Snell's law ($\sin 90° = 1$) gives

$$\sin\theta_c = \frac{1}{n}$$

In Part 1 you will find $n$ from a graph. In Part 2 you will measure $\theta_c$ and use it to find $n$ a second, independent way. (See C.3 Critical angle and total internal reflection.)

3. Equipment and safety

  • Ray box with a single-slit plate (to make one narrow ray), and its power supply (usually 12 V: check the label on the ray box)
  • Rectangular glass or Perspex block (Part 1)
  • Semicircular glass or Perspex block (Part 2; it can also be used for Part 1, see the note in Part 1)
  • 180° or 360° protractor, 30 cm ruler, sharp pencil, set square
  • Several sheets of white A4 paper

Safety:

  • The ray box lamp and its casing get hot after a few minutes. Hold the box by its base, and switch it off between parts. Let it cool before packing away.
  • Glass blocks are heavy and their corners chip easily; a chipped edge is sharp. Keep the block in the middle of the bench, and don't slide it over other blocks. Perspex scratches: don't put it face down on a dirty bench.
  • Keep the power supply and cables away from any water (Extension E1).

4. Before the lab

Uncertainties

Protractor: a protractor with 1° divisions read once gives ± 0.5°. But that is not the real limit here. The ray is 1–2 mm wide, so you must judge where its centre is; the normal you drew may be slightly off 90°; and you mark the ray with pencil crosses. A realistic uncertainty for an angle you set by lining the ray up with a drawn line is about ± 1°.

Refracted angle in the rectangular block: you can't see this ray clearly enough to measure it directly. You find it by joining the entry point A to the exit point B, and each of these is only known to about ± 1 mm (the width of the ray). Over a path of about 6 cm, an error of 1 mm at each end in opposite directions turns the line by about 2° (2 mm across 60 mm). So ± 2° is realistic for $\theta_{\text{block}}$. Decide whether your own tracing deserves a smaller or larger value, and say why.

Uncertainty in a sine: $\sin\theta$ does not change evenly with $\theta$, so find $\Delta(\sin\theta)$ as half the difference between the sines of the largest and smallest angles:

$$\Delta(\sin\theta) = \frac{\sin(\theta + \Delta\theta) - \sin(\theta - \Delta\theta)}{2}$$

Use a calculator in degree mode. For example, $\theta = (30 \pm 2)°$ gives $\Delta(\sin\theta) = (\sin 32° - \sin 28°)/2 = 0.03$, so $\sin\theta = 0.50 \pm 0.03$.

Critical angle: the refracted ray does not vanish suddenly; it fades and turns into colours. Your repeated readings will show how well you can judge it. Use half the range, $\frac{\text{max} - \text{min}}{2}$, or ± 1° if that is larger.

Quote each uncertainty to 1 significant figure (2 if the uncertainty itself is known precisely), with the value rounded to the same decimal place. See Physics skills.

5. Part 1: refraction through a rectangular block

  1. Place the rectangular block in the middle of a sheet of paper and draw round it with a sharp pencil. Mark a point A on one long side, about 3 cm from the left end.
  2. Remove the block. Using the protractor (or set square), draw the normal through A at 90° to the side, long enough to cross the whole outline and extend 5 cm outside it.
  3. With the protractor centred on A, draw lines from A outside the block at 10°, 20°, 30°, 40°, 50°, 60° and 70° to the normal, all on the same side. Label each with its angle. These are the incident rays.
  4. Put the block back exactly on its outline. Connect the ray box to the power supply and darken the room if you can. Set up a single narrow ray.
  5. Aim the ray along the 10° line so that it hits the block exactly at A. Look at the ray on the far side of the block: mark two crosses on its centre, at least 5 cm apart, and mark the exit point B where it leaves the block. Label them with the angle.
  6. Repeat for each of the other incident lines. Watch the reflected ray at A and the faint reflections inside the block: note how their brightness changes as the angle increases.
  7. Switch off the ray box and remove the block. For each angle, join the two crosses and extend the line back to the block outline to find B accurately. Then draw the straight line from A to B: this is the refracted ray inside the block.
  8. Measure θblock, the angle between AB and the normal at A, for each angle of incidence. Record both angles in the table. Check: each emerging ray should be parallel to its incident ray. If one is clearly not, repeat that angle.

Only a semicircular block for Part 1? Use the set-up of Part 2 (light enters the curved face aimed at the centre O, so it doesn't bend there). Set θblock to 5°, 10°, 15° … 40° and measure the angle at which the ray leaves the flat face into the air. Both angles are then measured directly, but keep θblock below about 40°, or the ray won't leave the flat face at all.

6. Part 2: critical angle with a semicircular block

  1. On a fresh sheet, place the semicircular block with its flat face along a straight line. Draw round it and mark the centre O of the flat face. Remove the block and draw the normal through O, extended well beyond the curved side. Replace the block.
  2. Shine the ray at the curved face so that it travels along a radius and reaches the flat face exactly at O. Check: with the ray along the normal it should pass straight through without bending. (If the ray doesn't hit O, it bends at the curved face and the method fails.)
  3. Slowly move the ray box round the curved side, keeping the ray aimed at O, so that the angle with the normal increases. Watch the ray leaving the flat face: it bends further from the normal, grows fainter and breaks into colours, while the reflected ray inside the block gets brighter.
  4. Stop when the last of the refracted light just disappears (the reflected ray suddenly becomes much brighter). Mark two crosses on the incident ray, well apart, outside the curved face, between the ray box and the block, and label them L1.
  5. Repeat on the other side of the normal, approaching the critical angle from the opposite direction, and label the crosses R1.
  6. Repeat steps 3–5 twice more (L2, R2, L3, R3). Each time, move the ray box away first and approach the critical angle again from smaller angles.
  7. Remove the block. Join each set of crosses to O. For each trial, measure the angle between the L and R lines (this is 2θc) and also each angle to the normal. Record them in the table, and halve the angle between L and R to get θc.
