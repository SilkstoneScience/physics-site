The mass–spring oscillator

Time a mass bouncing on a spring with a stopwatch, find the spring constant from the period, and check it with a static Hooke's-law test of the same spring. C.1 Simple harmonic motion

Aim: to measure the period of a mass–spring oscillator for at least six masses, to determine the spring constant $k$ from the gradient of a graph of $T^2$ against $m$, to determine $k$ independently from a static stretch test, and to compare the two values and explain the intercept of the $T^2$–$m$ graph.

1. Skills you will practise

  • Timing many oscillations to reduce the effect of human reaction time, and starting the timing at a reference (fiducial) mark.
  • Using repeated readings and the half-range method, (max − min)/2, to estimate uncertainty, and carrying it through to $T$ and $T^2$.
  • Linearising a square-root relationship so that a straight-line graph can test a theory.
  • Finding the uncertainty in a gradient from the steepest and shallowest lines, and interpreting the gradient and the intercept physically.
  • Comparing a quantity measured by two independent methods (dynamic and static), and explaining any difference.

Tools and inquiry: measuring mass, length and time (Tool 1); processing data with graphs (Tool 2); linearising a relationship and propagating uncertainties (Tool 3); collecting and processing data, and concluding and evaluating (Inquiry 2 and 3).

2. Theory: Hooke's law and SHM

A mass hanging from a spring sits at rest at its equilibrium position, where the upward spring force balances its weight. Pull it down a little and let go: it bounces up and down about that position.

If you displace the mass by $x$ from equilibrium, the spring force changes by an amount proportional to $x$ and in the opposite direction. This is Hooke's law (see A.2 Forces and momentum):

$$F = -kx$$

Here $k$ is the spring constant in $\text{N m}^{-1}$. The weight does not change during the motion, so it only sets where the equilibrium position is: the resultant force is still $-kx$.

A restoring force proportional to the displacement and directed towards equilibrium is exactly the condition for simple harmonic motion (SHM). Newton's second law then gives $a = -\frac{k}{m}x$. This has the form of the defining equation of SHM,

$$a = -\omega^2 x$$

with $\omega^2 = k/m$. Using $T = 2\pi/\omega$, the data booklet gives the time period of a mass–spring system (see C.1 Simple harmonic motion):

$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}$$

The period does not depend on the amplitude, or on $g$. Squaring both sides gives

$$T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{k}\,m$$

So a graph of $T^2$ against $m$ should be a straight line with gradient = $4\pi^2/k$. From the gradient you can find $k$. You will compare it with the value from a static stretch test of the same spring, where $k$ is the gradient of a graph of the hanging weight against the position of the hanger.

The equation assumes an ideal spring with no mass. Look carefully at the intercept of your $T^2$–$m$ graph: you will be asked to explain it.

3. Equipment and safety

  • Retort stand, two bosses and clamps, and a G-clamp (or a heavy mass) to fix the base to the bench
  • Helical steel spring (spring constant roughly 20–30 $\text{N m}^{-1}$) with a hook or loop at each end
  • Mass hanger and slotted masses (about 600 g in total, in steps of 100 g)
  • Stopwatch reading to 0.01 s (or a phone stopwatch)
  • Metre rule with millimetre divisions, and a set square
  • Light pointer (a pin or a narrow strip of card) and sticky tape; adhesive putty or a coloured tab for the fiducial mark
  • Electronic balance
  • Foam cushion or folded towel; safety glasses

Safety:

  • Fix the stand base to the bench with a G-clamp (or a heavy mass) so the loaded spring cannot pull it over. Put a cushion underneath in case a mass falls, and keep feet clear.
  • Wear safety glasses: a stretched spring that slips off its hook can flick upwards. Check that the spring's top loop is fully on the rod and the hanger is fully on the bottom loop.
  • Stagger the slots of the slotted masses so they cannot slide off together. Do not load the spring beyond 600 g or pull it down by more than a few centimetres: an overstretched spring is permanently damaged and its k changes.

4. Before the lab

Uncertainties

  • Mass $m$: weigh the hanger and each slotted mass on the balance (a "100 g" mass may really be 98 g or 102 g). The balance uncertainty is ± the last digit, so the mass uncertainty is tiny compared with the timing uncertainty, and you may leave out horizontal uncertainty bars on your graphs (say why in your report). Remember that $m$ is the total hanging mass: hanger + slotted masses + pointer.
  • Time for 20 oscillations $t$: the stopwatch shows 0.01 s, but your reaction time at the start and at the stop is much larger than this, so the resolution is not a realistic uncertainty. Use half the range of your repeated timings, $\frac{\text{max} - \text{min}}{2}$, or 0.1 s if your repeats happen to agree more closely than that (your reactions are not more precise than about a tenth of a second). Then $\Delta T = \Delta t/20$.
  • Pointer position $y$: each position is a single reading of the metre rule, ± 0.5 mm (half a division). You plot the positions themselves, so the gradient of your graph automatically uses the differences between readings. Parallax, or a pointer that is still moving slightly, can make a reading worse than ± 0.5 mm: read at eye level and decide whether ± 1 mm is more realistic.

Quote each uncertainty to 1 significant figure (usual) and round the value to the same decimal place, for example $t = (13.5 \pm 0.1)$ s and $T = (0.675 \pm 0.005)$ s. See Physics skills.

5. Setting up

  1. Fix the stand to the bench and hang the spring from the rod (see the diagram). Weigh the hanger with its pointer, and each slotted mass, and record the masses below.
  2. Clamp the metre rule vertically beside the spring, close to the pointer but not touching it. Check that it is vertical with the set square.
  3. With no hanger on the spring, record the position of the bottom of the spring on the rule, y0. You will use it only at the end, to check that the spring has not been overstretched. Then hang the hanger alone (no slotted masses).

Before you start

6. Collecting data

For each mass: static reading, then oscillations

  1. Add slotted masses to make the first total mass (start with about 100 g on the hanger). Let the mass come completely to rest.
  2. Static reading: with your eye level with the pointer, record the pointer position y (in the static table below). Move the fiducial mark (putty or tab) on the rule so it is exactly level with the pointer: this is the equilibrium position for this mass.
  3. Pull the mass straight down by about 2 cm and release it gently, without pushing it sideways. Let it settle into a steady up-and-down motion. If it starts to swing like a pendulum or twist, stop it and start again.
  4. Watch the pointer pass the fiducial mark. Start the stopwatch as it passes the mark moving downwards, counting "zero". Count each later downward pass ("one, two, …") and stop the watch on "twenty". Record t.
  5. Repeat the timing two more times (five if time allows), restarting the oscillation each time. If one timing differs from the others by about one period (around 0.5 s or more), you probably miscounted: repeat it.
  6. Check that the amplitude stays small and that the coils of the spring never touch at the top of the motion. Then add the next mass, about 100 g more, and repeat steps 4–8. Collect at least six different masses, up to 600 g in total.
  7. Finally, remove all the masses and check that the bottom of the spring returns to y0. If it does not, the spring has been overstretched: tell your teacher.

At the end

Timing table

Fill in one row for each mass. If you took more than three timings, use them all for the mean and the half range. Then calculate the other columns:

  • the mean time $t$ and its uncertainty $\Delta t = \frac{\text{max} - \text{min}}{2}$ (at least 0.1 s);
  • the period $T = t/20$ and $\Delta T = \Delta t/20$;
  • $T^2$ and $\Delta(T^2) = 2T \times \Delta T$ (the percentage uncertainty doubles when you square).

Static (Hooke's-law) table

Use the same total masses. Calculate the hanging weight $F = mg$ with $g = 9.8$ $\text{m s}^{-2}$, and write each pointer position in metres.

7. Your graphs

Each table has its own graph, which updates as you type. The axes are labelled with the quantities and units from the table's headings. The graph tool uses one uncertainty for every point on each axis, so where the uncertainties differ, type the largest one (a cautious choice). Press Add this graph to my report to keep each graph.

Graph 1: T2 against m

  1. The graph starts with $T^2$ on the vertical axis and total mass $m$ on the horizontal axis. Keep Start the axes at zero ticked, so you can see where the line meets the vertical axis. The line of best fit is not forced through the origin: the intercept is part of your result.
  2. Type your largest Δ($T^2$) in the vertical axis's uncertainty box. Leave the horizontal box empty: the mass uncertainty is far too small to see. Say why in your report.
  3. Tick Steepest and shallowest lines. They pass through every uncertainty bar. The tool then gives the gradient ± its uncertainty (half the difference between the gradients of the steepest and shallowest lines) and the y-intercept ± its uncertainty (see uncertainty in the gradient).
  4. Calculate $k = 4\pi^2/\text{gradient}$. Because $k$ is $4\pi^2$ divided by the gradient, its percentage uncertainty is the same as the gradient's: $\Delta k = k \times \frac{\Delta(\text{gradient})}{\text{gradient}}$. Record your results below.

Graph 2: F against y

  1. The graph starts with weight $F$ on the vertical axis and pointer position $y$ on the horizontal axis. Untick Start the axes at zero, so the graph shows just your range of positions.
  2. Type your Δ$y$ (in metres, for example 0.0005) in the horizontal axis's uncertainty box. Leave the vertical box empty: the uncertainty in $F$ comes from the balance and is too small to see.
  3. Tick Steepest and shallowest lines. The gradient $\Delta F/\Delta y$ is the static spring constant, with its uncertainty, in $\text{N m}^{-1}$ (the tool writes the unit as N / m). The intercept has no special meaning here, because $y$ is measured from the top of the rule, not from where the pointer would be with no load.
