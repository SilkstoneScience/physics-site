Free-fall acceleration with a picket fence

A lab with Vernier sensors: a striped plastic strip, the picket fence, falls through a photogate, which times each black band as it cuts the beam. A.1 Kinematics Vernier

Aim: to determine the acceleration of free fall, g, from the gradient of a velocity–time graph, with its uncertainty from repeated drops; to check the software's velocities for one drop by calculating them from the raw blocking times; and to compare your value with $9.8\ \text{m s}^{-2}$, evaluating the random and systematic effects in the experiment.

1. Skills you will practise

  • Using a sensor (a photogate) to collect precise timing data, and checking what the software calculates by doing one calculation by hand.
  • Using repeated trials and the half-range method, (max − min)/2, to estimate uncertainty.
  • Measuring a length that needs two readings, and reducing its percentage uncertainty by measuring over several spacings.
  • Interpreting the gradient and the intercept of a velocity–time graph as physical quantities, with the uncertainty in the gradient from the steepest and shallowest lines.
  • Telling random uncertainty apart from systematic effects that repeated trials cannot reveal.

Tools and inquiry: measuring length and time (Tool 1); collecting data with sensors, and processing it with graphs (Tool 2); collecting and processing data, and concluding and evaluating (Inquiry 2 and 3).

2. Background

An object is in free fall when the only force acting on it is its weight. Close to the Earth's surface the gravitational field is almost uniform, so a freely falling object has a constant downward acceleration, the acceleration of free fall, $g$.

In practice the air always pushes back a little (fluid resistance). For a thin, smooth object falling a short distance at a few metres per second, this effect is small.

For uniformly accelerated motion the data booklet gives, with $a = g$ and downwards taken as positive:

$$v = u + at$$ $$s = ut + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$$

So a graph of velocity $v$ against time $t$ should be a straight line with gradient = $g$ and vertical intercept = $u$, the velocity at the moment you chose as $t = 0$. A graph of displacement against time should be a parabola. (See A.1 Kinematics.)

The picket fence is a clear plastic strip with evenly spaced black bands. The photogate sends an infrared beam across its opening and records the time whenever the beam becomes blocked. As the fence falls, each black band blocks the beam in turn.

Between one band's leading (lower) edge and the next band's leading edge, the fence falls one band spacing $d$ (5.0 cm on the standard fence). The average velocity over that gap is $d/\Delta t$, where $\Delta t$ is the time between the two blocking events.

For uniform acceleration, the average velocity over an interval equals the instantaneous velocity at the middle of that interval (you will show this in P1). So in your hand check, each average velocity is plotted at the mid-time of its gap. Logger Pro calculates its own velocities from the band times; you will compare its results with your hand check of one drop.

3. Equipment and safety

  • Vernier interface (or LabQuest) and a laptop with Logger Pro 3
  • Vernier Photogate and Vernier Picket Fence
  • Ring stand with a clamp for the photogate (and a G-clamp or heavy mass to steady the base)
  • Metre rule (or 50 cm rule) with millimetre divisions (the measurement across all seven gaps is about 35 cm)
  • Foam cushion or folded towel to land on

Safety:

  • Clamp the ring stand firmly (G-clamp or a heavy mass on its base) so it cannot tip over the bench edge.
  • The fence falls onto a cushion on the floor: keep feet and bags out of the way, and pick the fence up only when no-one is about to drop it. Do not stand on stools or benches to drop it from higher up.
  • Hold the fence only by its top edge and keep fingers clear of the gate. The plastic scratches and cracks easily; a cracked fence has sharp edges.

4. Before the lab

Uncertainties

Band spacing $d$: each measurement with a ruler needs two readings (one at each edge), so a single gap is known to ± 0.1 cm, which is ± 2 % of 5.0 cm. Measuring across all seven gaps at once (from the leading edge of the first band to the leading edge of the last) still gives ± 0.1 cm, but now on about 35 cm: under 0.3 %. Use this to check the 5.00 cm that Logger Pro assumes.

Times: the photogate timer is far more precise than any other measurement here; the band edges and the beam width limit it more than the clock does. The timing uncertainty is much smaller than the uncertainty in $d$, so you may leave out horizontal uncertainty bars, but say why in your report.

$g$ from repeated drops: the spread between drops shows the random uncertainty. Use half the range, $\frac{\text{max} - \text{min}}{2}$, quoted to 1 significant figure (usual) or 2 significant figures when the uncertainty itself is known precisely (for example from many readings), with the mean rounded to the same decimal place. See Physics skills.

Remember that a small spread does not mean the result is accurate: an error in the band spacing, a tilted fence or air resistance shifts every drop in the same direction.

5. Setting up

  1. Clamp the photogate to the ring stand with its arms horizontal and its opening beyond the edge of the bench (see the diagram). Place the cushion on the floor below. The gate must be high enough for the whole fence to pass through it before the fence lands.
  2. Lay the fence flat and measure each of the seven band spacings, always from the lower edge of one band to the lower edge of the next (the edges that enter the beam first). Then measure across all seven gaps at once. Record them below.
  3. Connect the photogate to DIG 1 on the interface before you start Logger Pro 3 (the photogate settings only appear once it is connected). The quickest set-up is Vernier's ready-made file: choose File ▸ Open and look in the Experiments folder for the Picket Fence Free Fall file (in the Physics with Vernier or Physics with Computers folder). To set it up by hand instead, choose Experiment ▸ Set Up Sensors ▸ Show All Interfaces, click the picture of the photogate, choose Set Distance or Length, and select the Picket Fence with a band spacing of 0.050 m. Either way, Experiment ▸ Data Collection… should now show a photogate timing mode rather than Time Based.
  4. Block the beam with your hand: the gate state in the status bar should change to Blocked, and the gate's red LED should light. Unblock it again.

6. Collecting data

  1. Hold the fence by its top edge, vertical, with its lowest band about 2 cm above the beam and centred in the opening. A partner checks from the side that it is vertical and will not touch the arms.
  2. Click Collect. Open your fingers to release the fence without pushing it, and let it fall onto the cushion. Click Stop if collection has not already stopped.
  3. Check the run: there should be eight blocking times. Logger Pro calculates a velocity from the band times; note how many velocity points it gives. The points should lie close to a straight line. Reject any drop where the fence touched the gate or twisted (points well off the line), and repeat it.
  4. On the velocity–time graph choose Analyze ▸ Linear Fit. Record the gradient and intercept in the table. On the acceleration–time graph choose Analyze ▸ Statistics and record the mean acceleration. On the distance–time graph choose Analyze ▸ Curve Fit, then Quadratic (At² + Bt + C) and record A. (If a graph isn't showing, click its y-axis label and choose Acceleration, or Distance.)
  5. Choose Experiment ▸ Store Latest Run, so that the next drop does not overwrite this one. Repeat steps 5–9 (storing each run before the next drop) until you have at least six good drops, all from the same release height.
  6. Choose one good drop for the hand check. Copy its eight blocking times from the Logger Pro data table into the hand-check table (section 7). Save your Logger Pro file.

7. Hand check of one drop

Use the eight blocking times of the drop you chose. They give seven gaps: gap 1 runs from the 1st blocking time to the 2nd, gap 2 from the 2nd to the 3rd, and so on. For each gap, fill in:

  • $\Delta t$ = (time at end of gap) − (time at start of gap);
  • the mid-time, the mean of the start and end times;
  • $v = d/\Delta t$, with $d$ your mean band spacing in metres;
  • $\Delta v = v \times \Delta d/d$.

Each speed is calculated from one gap, so it has the same percentage uncertainty as one band spacing: $\Delta v/v = \Delta d/d$ = 0.1 cm / 5.0 cm, about 2 %. (The much smaller 0.3 % from measuring across all seven gaps tells you how well the average spacing is known, so it only checks Logger Pro's 5.00 cm; it is not the uncertainty of an individual gap.)

8. Your graph

The graph updates as you type. It starts with velocity $v$ on the vertical axis and mid-time on the horizontal axis, so the gradient of the line of best fit is your hand-check value of $g$ and the y-intercept is $u$.

The graph tool uses one uncertainty for every point on each axis. Every speed has the same percentage uncertainty, so type it as a percentage in the vertical axis's uncertainty box (for example 2%): each point then gets its own bar, Δv = v × Δd/d. (Typing your largest Δv instead is a cautious choice.) Leave the horizontal axis's box empty, and say in your report why the time uncertainty bars are left out. Then tick Steepest and shallowest lines: they pass through every uncertainty bar, and Δg = (gmax − gmin)/2 is half the difference between their gradients.
