Static and dynamic friction

A lab with Vernier sensors: pull a wooden block with a force sensor to measure friction, then check your result a second way with a motion detector. A.2 Forces and momentum Vernier

2. Background

Push gently on a heavy box and it doesn't move: static friction matches your push. Push harder and static friction grows to match, but only up to a limit:

$F_f \le \mu_s F_N$

where $\mu_s$ is the coefficient of static friction and $F_N$ is the normal force. Once your push is bigger than $\mu_s F_N$, the box starts to slide. On a level surface, the normal force on the block is equal in size to its weight.

While the box slides, dynamic friction acts against its motion:

$$F_f = \mu_d F_N$$

where $\mu_d$ is the coefficient of dynamic friction. Both coefficients depend on the two surfaces in contact. (See A.2 Forces and momentum.)

3. Equipment and safety

  • Vernier interface and a laptop with Logger Pro 3
  • Vernier Dual-Range Force Sensor and Vernier Motion Detector
  • A wooden block with a hook, string, and slotted masses (four of 250 g, or similar)
  • A balance, and a long, smooth, level bench or track

Safety: fix the masses to the block (with tape or a rubber band) so they can't slide off. Keep the floor along the pulling path clear of bags and feet, and don't let the block or masses fall off the end of the bench.

4. Before the lab

Uncertainties

Before Part 1, find the uncertainty of the balance (for a digital balance, ± the smallest division) and the resolution of the force sensor on the range you use. For three repeated readings, use half the range, $\frac{\text{max} - \text{min}}{2}$, or the instrument's uncertainty if that is bigger. Give uncertainties to 1 significant figure (2 if the uncertainty itself is known precisely), and round each value to the same decimal place, e.g. $(4.8 \pm 0.2)$ N. See Physics skills.

5. Part 1: starting friction

Mass of the block

  1. Weigh the block and record its mass above.
  2. Plug the force sensor into CH 1 and set its switch to ±50 N. Open Logger Pro 3. Choose Experiment ▸ Data Collection… and set the length to 5 seconds.
  3. Tie the block's hook to the force sensor's hook with string. Put 1 kg on the block and fix it in place.
  4. Practise the pull without collecting data. Pull horizontally and gently, and increase the force slowly, so that the block starts to slide after about one second. Then keep it sliding at a steady speed for another second.
  5. Hold the sensor ready to pull, with the string slack, and choose Experiment ▸ Zero.
  6. Click Collect and pull as you practised. Repeat until the graph shows the block at rest, starting to move, and sliding at a steady speed. Keep this graph for the analysis (for example with File ▸ Save As, or a screenshot).

6. Part 2: friction and the normal force

  1. Take all the masses off the block. Zero the sensor, click Collect and pull as before.
  2. Peak static friction: choose Analyze ▸ Statistics for the whole graph and read the maximum force.
  3. Dynamic friction: drag across the part where the block slid at a steady speed, choose Analyze ▸ Statistics again and read the mean force.
  4. Record both values. Repeat twice more, so you have three trials.
  5. Add 250 g to the block and repeat. Then repeat with 500 g, 750 g and 1000 g added.
  6. Work out the normal force for each total mass ($F_N = mg$, with $g = 9.8\ \text{m s}^{-2}$) and its uncertainty.

Graphs of your Part 2 readings

The graph starts with mean peak static friction against normal force. Change the vertical axis to mean dynamic friction for the second graph. The tool uses one uncertainty for every point on each axis, so type your largest ΔF (static or dynamic, to match the graph) in the vertical axis's uncertainty box and your largest ΔFN in the horizontal axis's box, then tick Steepest and shallowest lines. Press Add this graph to my report for each graph.

7. Part 3: dynamic friction a second way

Now measure $\mu_d$ independently, with no string. Once the block leaves your hand, the only horizontal force on it is friction, so its acceleration gives the friction force through Newton's second law.

  1. Plug the motion detector into DIG 1. Put it on the bench 2–3 m from the block, facing it. Logger Pro should now show position and velocity graphs.
  2. Practise giving the block a short push towards the detector, so that it leaves your hand and slides about 1 m before stopping, without turning. It must stop at least 0.4 m from the detector.
  3. Click Collect and push the block. The velocity–time graph should have a straight, sloping part while the block slides freely. Repeat if it doesn't.
  4. Drag across that straight part and choose Analyze ▸ Linear Fit. The gradient is the block's acceleration. Record it. (The block moves towards the detector, so Logger Pro gives its velocity a negative sign, and the gradient comes out positive even though the block is slowing down. Use its size, |a|.)
  5. Do five slides in all. Then fix 500 g to the block and do five more.
