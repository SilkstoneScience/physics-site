Specific heat capacity of a metal block

A lab with a Vernier Temperature Probe: an electric heater warms a metal block, two multimeters measure the heater's current and potential difference, and Logger Pro records how the block's temperature rises. B.1 Thermal energy transfers Vernier

Aim: to find the specific heat capacity of a metal (aluminium, steel or brass) from a graph of energy supplied against temperature, and to compare it with the accepted value.

2. Background

Give 1 kg of water and 1 kg of aluminium exactly the same amount of energy, and the aluminium's temperature rises more than four times as much as the water's. Different materials need different amounts of energy to raise the temperature of each kilogram by one kelvin. This property is the specific heat capacity, $c$, of the material.

The energy $Q$ needed to change the temperature of a mass $m$ by $\Delta T$ is given in the data booklet as

$$Q = mc\Delta T$$

(see B.1 Thermal energy transfers). In this lab you supply the energy electrically. An immersion heater sits in a hole in a metal block, and a Temperature Probe sits in a second hole. The power delivered to the heater is found from the potential difference across it and the current through it, $P = IV$ (data booklet, B.5), so the energy supplied after a time $t$ is

$$E = Pt = IVt$$

If all of this energy stayed in the block, then

$$E = mc(\theta - \theta_0)$$

where $\theta$ is the block's temperature and $\theta_0$ its starting temperature. A graph of energy supplied $E$ (vertical axis) against temperature $\theta$ (horizontal axis) should then be a straight line with

$$\text{gradient} = mc$$

so $c = \dfrac{\text{gradient}}{m}$. A change of 1 °C is the same as a change of 1 K, so you can find the gradient with temperatures in °C and quote it in $\text{J K}^{-1}$.

In a real lab, energy is also used to warm the heater itself, and some leaks out of the block into the air and the bench. You will look closely at where your graph is straight and where it isn't, and use that to judge how reliable your value of $c$ is.

3. Equipment and safety

  • Vernier interface (or LabQuest) and a laptop with Logger Pro 3
  • Vernier Temperature Probe
  • Calorimetry block (about 1 kg) of aluminium, steel or brass, with holes for the heater and the probe
  • A 12 V immersion heater to fit the block, and a low-voltage power supply that can give about 5 A
  • Two digital multimeters (one used as an ammeter on its 10 A range, one as a voltmeter), or an ammeter and a voltmeter
  • Connecting leads
  • Top-pan balance
  • A few drops of oil (or water) for the probe hole, and a dropper
  • Insulation for the block (foam, felt or cloth) and a heat-proof mat

Safety:

  • Only switch the heater on when it is fully inside the block. A heater switched on in air overheats within seconds and can burn you or damage itself.
  • The block and heater become hot (up to about 50 °C, the heater much hotter). Let them cool, or handle them with care, at the end of the lab.
  • Never go above the heater's rated voltage. Connect the ammeter to its 10 A socket before switching on. The 0.6 A Vernier Current Probe must not be used in this circuit. Many multimeters allow 10 A for only 10–30 s at a time: check that your meter's continuous rating covers the heater current for the whole run, or use an analogue ammeter rated 5 A or more.
  • Wipe up any oil spills. Keep the leads away from the hot block, and keep water away from the power supply.

4. Before the lab

Uncertainties

  • Mass of the block, $m$: a digital balance reading has an uncertainty of ± the smallest division (for example ± 0.001 kg).
  • Current $I$ and pd $V$: a steady digital reading is ± the last digit, but the readings drift a little as the heater warms up. You will read both meters every minute. These are repeated readings, so use the mean, with an uncertainty of half the range, (max − min)/2, or ± the last digit, whichever is larger (see recording uncertainties).
  • Power $P = IV$: add the percentage uncertainties, $\dfrac{\Delta P}{P} = \dfrac{\Delta I}{I} + \dfrac{\Delta V}{V}$ (see propagating uncertainties).
  • Energy $E = Pt$: Logger Pro times to a small fraction of a second, so $\dfrac{\Delta E}{E} \approx \dfrac{\Delta P}{P}$. Each point on your graph has its own $\Delta E$.
  • Temperature $\theta$: the probe displays to 0.1 °C or better, but while the block is heating, its temperature isn't the same everywhere. A realistic uncertainty is ± 0.2 °C, larger than the display's resolution.

Quote each uncertainty to 1 significant figure, with the best estimate rounded to the same decimal place, for example $P = (47.6 \pm 0.9)$ W. See Physics skills.

5. Setting up

  1. Measure the mass of the block, $m$, on the balance. Record it below, with its uncertainty.
  2. Put the block on the heat-proof mat. Place the heater in its hole. Put a few drops of oil into the probe hole, then insert the Temperature Probe.
  3. Wrap insulation around the sides of the block, leaving the tops of the heater and the probe clear.
  4. Build the circuit in the diagram: the power supply, the ammeter (on its 10 A range) and the heater in series, and the voltmeter across the heater terminals. Keep the power supply off.
  5. Plug the Temperature Probe into the interface and open Logger Pro 3. Choose Experiment ▸ Data Collection and set the length to 900 s at 1 sample per second.
  6. Wait until the probe reading is steady (the block is at room temperature). Record this starting temperature, $\theta_0$, below.

6. Collecting data

  1. Click Collect. As close as possible to $t = 0$, switch the power supply on at the voltage your teacher gives (about 12 V).
  2. Every 60 s, read the ammeter and the voltmeter and record them in the table below. You can read the temperature off the graph afterwards.
  3. After 600 s, or when the block is about 25 °C above $\theta_0$ (whichever comes first), switch the power supply off. Note the exact time you switched off. (With a 12 V heater of about 50 W, a 1 kg block usually reaches the 25 °C limit first: after about 8 minutes for aluminium, 4 for steel and 3 for brass.)
  4. Let Logger Pro keep recording until 900 s. Watch what the temperature does after the heater is switched off.

Now find the mean current and the mean pd. Their uncertainties are half the range, (max − min)/2, or ± the last digit, whichever is larger. Then work out the mean power, $P = IV$, and its uncertainty from $\dfrac{\Delta P}{P} = \dfrac{\Delta I}{I} + \dfrac{\Delta V}{V}$.

Mean current, pd and power

Energy supplied

Fill in one row for each 60 s mark, up to the time you switched off. Type the time and the temperature $\theta$ you read in Logger Pro. Then use your mean power to work out the last two columns: the energy supplied, $E = Pt$ (0 J at $t = 0$), and its uncertainty, $\Delta E = E \times \dfrac{\Delta P}{P}$.

7. Your graph

The graph updates as you type. It starts with energy supplied on the vertical axis and temperature on the horizontal axis, so the gradient is $mc$. Untick Start the axes at zero, so that the graph shows just your temperature range.

  1. Look at the shape of the graph. Decide which points lie on the straight part (the first one or two minutes are often curved). Open Choose which data points to use and untick the others, so the line of best fit uses only the straight part.
  2. Add uncertainty bars: every point gets them. The graph tool uses one uncertainty for each axis. For the energy, every point has the same percentage uncertainty, so type your percentage uncertainty in $P$ (for example 2%) in the vertical axis's uncertainty box: each point then gets its own bar, matching your ΔE column. (Typing your largest ΔE instead is a cautious choice.) For the temperature, type 0.2 (or your own value) in the horizontal axis's box.
  3. Tick Steepest and shallowest lines. They pass through every uncertainty bar of the points you use. The uncertainty in the gradient is half the difference between their gradients, $\Delta(\text{gradient}) = \dfrac{\text{max} - \text{min}}{2}$ (see uncertainty in the gradient).
  4. The tool writes the gradient's unit as J / °C. A change of 1 °C is the same as a change of 1 K, so this is the same as $\text{J K}^{-1}$.
  5. Press Add this graph to my report to keep it. The axes are labelled with the quantities and units from your table's headings.
