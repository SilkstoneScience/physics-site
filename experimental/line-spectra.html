2. Theory: line spectra and energy levels

A hot solid, such as the filament of an old-style light bulb, gives a continuous spectrum: seen through a diffraction grating, it spreads into an unbroken band of all the colours.

A gas at low pressure behaves very differently. When a high voltage makes it glow, it emits only a few sharp colours, each at its own wavelength. Seen through a grating, the light from the narrow tube appears as a set of separate coloured copies of the tube: an emission line spectrum. Every element has its own set of lines, like a fingerprint. (See E.1: Emission and absorption spectra.)

Line spectra are evidence that an atom can only have certain discrete energy levels. When an electron drops from a higher level to a lower one, the atom emits one photon carrying the energy difference. The data booklet (E.1) gives the photon energy as

$$E = hf \quad\text{and, with } c = f\lambda,\quad E = \frac{hc}{\lambda}$$

where $h$ is the Planck constant and $c$ is the speed of light. The data booklet gives $hc = 1.99 \times 10^{-25}$ J m $= 1.24 \times 10^{-6}$ eV m, so with $\lambda$ in metres, $E = \dfrac{1.24 \times 10^{-6}}{\lambda}$ gives the energy directly in electronvolts.

Only a few energy differences are possible, so only a few wavelengths are emitted. Hydrogen, the simplest atom, has three bright lines in the visible range: red, blue-green and violet. (See E.1: The hydrogen spectrum.)

Measuring a wavelength with a diffraction grating

A diffraction grating is a sheet with a very large number of equally spaced lines. Its label gives the number of lines per millimetre, $N$, so the grating spacing is $d = 1/N$. For example, 600 lines per mm gives $d = \frac{1}{600}$ mm $= 1.67 \times 10^{-6}$ m.

Light of wavelength $\lambda$ that passes through the grating at right angles leaves strongly only at certain angles $\theta$ to the straight-through direction, given in the data booklet (C.3) by

$$n\lambda = d\sin\theta$$

where $n$ is the order ($n = 0$ straight through, $n = 1$ the first set of lines on each side). In this lab you use only the first order, $n = 1$, so $\lambda = d\sin\theta$. (See C.3: Multiple slits and diffraction gratings.)

SL and HL: the grating equation $n\lambda = d\sin\theta$ is in the HL part of C.3. SL students: you don't need to explain where it comes from. Treat it as a relation you are given, like a formula on a calibration sheet, and use it to turn your angles into wavelengths. Everything else in this lab is SL content from E.1.

You will look at the tube through the grating with your eye close behind it. A metre rule is fixed horizontally just below the glowing part of the tube, at right angles to your line of sight, at a distance $D$ from the grating. A partner slides a pointer along the rule until it lines up with each coloured line that you see. If the line appears a distance $x$ from the tube (see the diagrams in Part 2), then

$$\tan\theta = \frac{x}{D}$$

With a 600 lines per mm grating the angles are about 15–23°, too large for the small-angle approximation ($\sin\theta \approx \tan\theta$): $\sin\theta$ and $\tan\theta$ differ by up to about 9 %. With 300 lines per mm the angles are only about 7.5–11.4° and the difference is just 1–2 %, but it is still a systematic error. So in both cases, work out $\theta$ from $\tan\theta$ and then use $\sin\theta$.

If you measure $x$ at several values of $D$, a graph of $x$ against $D$ is a straight line through the origin with gradient $= \tan\theta$, one line for each colour.

3. Equipment and safety

  • Spectrum tube power supply with tube holder (high voltage, a few kV): operated by the teacher
  • Hydrogen spectrum tube; a helium tube (or another gas the school has), and one tube with its label covered for Part 3
  • Transmission diffraction grating in a slide mount; check its label for the number of lines per mm (for example 300 or 600 lines per mm; 600 is better if you have it). Stand and clamp to hold it at eye height
  • Two metre rules (one as the scale in front of the tube, one for measuring $D$), or a metre rule and a tape measure. The scale rule must be wooden or plastic with no metal end caps
  • Stand and clamp (or two wooden blocks) to hold the scale rule horizontal just below the glowing capillary of the tube
  • Two pointers: narrow strips of white card folded over the rule so they slide along it
  • Masking tape and a calculator
  • Optional: a filament lamp (clear bulb) for comparison with a continuous spectrum

Safety: high voltage, hot glass and a dim room

  • The power supply produces several thousand volts at the tube terminals. Never touch the tube, its metal ends or the holder's contacts while the supply is on. Only the teacher switches the supply on and off and changes tubes, after switching off and letting the tube cool.
  • Spectrum tubes become hot during use, especially near the ends: don't touch a tube even after it has been switched off until your teacher says it is cool. Tubes are thin glass: report a cracked or broken tube immediately and don't pick up the pieces.
  • Some tubes give out a small amount of ultraviolet as well as visible light. Don't stare at the tube from close up for long periods: look away between readings.
  • Duty cycle: the tube is on only while readings are being taken, roughly 30 s on and 30 s off (follow the instructions for your tubes). Hydrogen tubes in particular degrade quickly if left on, so plan each reading before the teacher switches on.
  • The scale rule sits close to the high-voltage terminals: use a wooden or plastic rule with no metal end caps. Keep water bottles and metal objects (stands, clamps, metal rules) clear of the tube terminals. Keep the scale rule and pointers on the observer's side of the tube.
  • When the room is dimmed, keep enough light to see the floor and your notes. Keep bags and stools out of the walkway and move carefully. Handle the grating by its mount; don't touch its surface.

4. Before the lab

Uncertainties

Line positions $x_\text{L}$ and $x_\text{R}$: the rule can be read to ± 0.05 cm, but this is not the limit. Each coloured line is a few millimetres wide, the violet line is dim, and the observer has to judge when the pointer lines up with its centre in a darkened room. A realistic uncertainty in each pointer position is about ± 0.3 cm (more for the violet line).

At one distance, both partners take a turn as observer: use the half range, $\frac{\text{max} - \text{min}}{2}$, of your two values if it is larger.

Distance $x = (x_\text{R} - x_\text{L})/2$: the difference of two readings adds their uncertainties (± 0.6 cm), and halving it halves the uncertainty, so $\Delta x \approx$ ± 0.3 cm. Reading both sides and averaging also cancels most of the error if the grating is turned slightly away from facing the tube.

Grating-to-rule distance $D$: measure from the grating surface to the front edge of the scale rule. The grating is inside a mount and the rules may not be exactly straight, so ± 1 cm is realistic: much larger than the rule's resolution.

Grating spacing $d$: this comes from the label. Inexpensive replica gratings are not always exactly what the label says: use the tolerance your teacher gives you. Because $d$ is the same for every point, its uncertainty doesn't show up on the graph: you add its percentage uncertainty at the end.

Quote uncertainties to 1 significant figure (usual), or 2 if the uncertainty itself is known precisely, with each value rounded to the same decimal place. See Physics skills.

6. Part 2: measuring hydrogen's lines

  1. Clamp the scale rule horizontally just below the glowing capillary of the tube and as close to the tube as the holder safely allows, with its 50 cm mark directly in front of the tube. Don't let the rule touch the tube or its terminals.
  2. Clamp the grating at the height of the capillary, about 0.40 m from the rule (0.60 m if your grating has 300 lines per mm), with its lines vertical and its surface facing the tube squarely. Put a pointer on each side of the tube. Record the grating's lines per mm in the panel below.
  3. Measure $D$ from the grating to the front edge of the rule, keeping the measuring rule level and to one side of the line of sight. Type $D$ in a new row of each colour's table.
  4. The observer puts one eye close behind the grating and keeps their head still. The partner slides the right-hand pointer until the observer says it is directly below the centre of the red line, then does the same on the left. Leave the pointers where they are, switch a light on briefly (a desk lamp or the room lights; never touch the tube), and read $x_\text{L}$ and $x_\text{R}$ from the rule. Record them in the red table.
  5. Repeat step 6 for the blue-green line and the violet line. (The violet line is the faintest: let your eyes adapt to the dark for a minute first.)
  6. At $D$ = 0.80 m only, swap roles: the second partner takes a full set of readings without looking at the first set. Record both partners' values of $x$ in the repeat panel below.
  7. Move the grating back by 0.10 m and repeat steps 5–7 until you have six distances (for example 0.40 m to 0.90 m). With a 300 lines per mm grating, use about 0.60 m to 1.10 m. Check each time that the grating still faces the tube squarely: $x_\text{R}$ − 50 cm and 50 cm − $x_\text{L}$ should be nearly equal.
  8. Record the number of lines per mm printed on the grating's label, and the grating spacing $d$ with its uncertainty, in the panel below.

Each x = (xR − xL)/2, in cm. Half range = (max − min)/2. Δx used is the larger of your realistic estimate (about ± 0.3 cm) and the half range.

8. Your graphs and results

Each graph updates as you type. It starts with line position $x$ on the vertical axis and grating-to-rule distance $D$ on the horizontal axis. Both are in metres, so the gradient is a pure number: gradient $= \tan\theta$.

The graph tool uses one uncertainty for every point on each axis. Type 0.01 (your ΔD in metres) in the horizontal axis's uncertainty box, and that colour's Δx used, in metres, in the vertical axis's box (for example 0.003 for ± 0.3 cm; if your Δx varied, type the largest). Then tick Steepest and shallowest lines: they pass through every uncertainty bar, and the tool gives the gradient ± its uncertainty and the y-intercept ± its uncertainty. From gradient to wavelength

The gradient $m$ is $\tan\theta$, so $\theta = \tan^{-1} m$ and

$$\lambda = d\sin\theta = \frac{d\,m}{\sqrt{1 + m^2}}$$

For each colour, with $d$ in metres:

  1. Calculate $\theta$ and $\lambda$ from the best gradient $m$, and convert $\lambda$ to nm (multiply by $10^9$).
  2. The results under the graph give the gradients of the steepest and shallowest lines. Calculate $\lambda_\text{max}$ from the steepest and $\lambda_\text{min}$ from the shallowest. Then $\Delta\lambda$ (from the graph) $= \dfrac{\lambda_\text{max} - \lambda_\text{min}}{2}$.
  3. Add the percentage uncertainty of $d$ to the percentage uncertainty from the graph to get the total $\Delta\lambda$ (Analysis question 2).
  4. Calculate the photon energy $E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$ in eV and its uncertainty (Analysis question 4).

Fill in the results table as you go: the line number, $m$, $\Delta m$, $\theta$, $\lambda$, $\Delta\lambda$, $E$ and $\Delta E$, and the upper level $n$ in Analysis question 5.
