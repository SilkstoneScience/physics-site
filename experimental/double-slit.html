Young's double slit: the wavelength of a laser

A laser shines through two narrow slits very close together, and the row of bright and dark fringes on a screen tells you the wavelength of its light. C.3 Wave phenomena

Aim: to observe the double-slit interference pattern of a laser and relate it to coherence and path difference; to measure the fringe separation $s$ at six or more slit-to-screen distances $D$ between 1 m and 3 m; to find the wavelength of the laser light, with its uncertainty, from the gradient of a graph of $s$ against $D$; and to compare it with the wavelength printed on the laser, evaluating the main sources of uncertainty and systematic error.

1. Skills you will practise

  • Measuring a very small length (the fringe separation) precisely, by measuring across many fringes and dividing.
  • Judging realistic uncertainties where human judgement (finding the centre of a fuzzy fringe, or where a slit sits inside its holder) matters more than the resolution of the ruler.
  • Choosing a straight-line graph so that the gradient gives the quantity you want, and using the intercept to spot a systematic error.
  • Finding the uncertainty in a gradient from the steepest and shallowest lines, and combining it with the uncertainty in a manufacturer's value.
  • Working safely with a laser.

Tools and inquiry: measuring length, and working safely with a laser (Tool 1); processing data with graphs (Tool 2); collecting and processing data, and concluding and evaluating (Inquiry 2 and 3).

2. Theory: why fringes?

In 1801 Thomas Young shone light through two narrow slits very close together and saw a row of bright and dark bands, or fringes, on a screen. Particles of light going straight through two slits would give just two bright lines. A set of evenly spaced fringes is what waves do when they overlap, so the experiment became one of the strongest pieces of evidence for the wave model of light.

Each slit diffracts the light, so the waves from the two slits spread out and overlap. Both slits are lit by the same laser beam, so the two sources are coherent: they have the same frequency and a constant phase difference.

At a point on the screen, the waves from the two slits have travelled different distances. Where the path difference is a whole number of wavelengths, $n\lambda$, they arrive in phase and interfere constructively: a bright fringe. Where it is $(n + \tfrac{1}{2})\lambda$, they arrive in antiphase and interfere destructively: a dark fringe. (See C.3: Young's double-slit experiment.)

When the screen is far from the slits compared with the slit separation, the bright fringes are equally spaced. The data booklet gives the fringe separation $s$ (from the centre of one bright fringe to the centre of the next) as

$$s = \frac{\lambda D}{d}$$

where $\lambda$ is the wavelength, $D$ is the distance from the slits to the screen and $d$ is the separation of the slits (centre to centre). So $s$ is directly proportional to $D$: a graph of $s$ against $D$ should be a straight line through the origin with gradient = $\lambda/d$. If $d$ is known, then

$$\lambda = d \times \text{gradient}$$

The fringes are only a few millimetres apart, so a single spacing can't be measured precisely with a ruler. Instead you will measure across many fringes, for example from the 5th bright fringe on the left of the centre to the 5th on the right, and divide by the number of spacings.

3. Equipment and laser safety

  • Class 2 laser (red; its label states the wavelength) with its power supply
  • Double-slit plate (or slide) in its holder, for example the slit plate of the Vernier Diffraction Apparatus. Use a double slit of known separation, ideally $d$ = 0.25 mm with slit width $b$ = 0.04 mm.
  • Ring stands and clamps (or the optics bench) to hold the laser and the slit plate steady
  • White screen on a stand (or a smooth wall), several sheets of plain white paper, masking tape and a sharp pencil
  • Three metre rules (or a tape measure) for $D$
  • 30 cm ruler with millimetre divisions for the fringes
  • Calculator

Laser safety:

  • Class 2 lasers are low-power, but the beam can still damage the eye. Never look into the beam or at its reflection, and never point the laser at anyone. Your blink reflex is your only protection, so don't stare at the beam.
  • When you look at or mark the pattern, look at the screen from the side, never back along the beam towards the slits and the laser.
  • Keep the beam horizontal and below eye level: clamp the laser low on the bench and stay standing. Don't sit or bend down so that your eyes are at beam height. Switch the laser off whenever you move it or change slits.
  • Watch for reflections: remove watches, rings and bracelets, and keep shiny objects (phone screens, the metal of the slit holder, glassware) out of the beam. The beam must end on the screen, and never pass through a doorway or out of a window.
  • When the room is dimmed, keep enough light to see the floor and your notes. Keep bags and stools out of the walkway, and don't move around quickly.

4. Before the lab

Uncertainties

Width $W$ across the fringes: the ruler alone gives ± 1 mm, because a length needs two readings of ± 0.5 mm each. But the fringes have fuzzy edges, so judging where the centre of each end fringe lies adds roughly another ± 0.5 mm at each end. A realistic uncertainty is about ± 2 mm, which is larger than the ruler's resolution.

Two partners marking the same pattern independently gives a check: use the half range, $\frac{\text{max} - \text{min}}{2}$, of their two values of $W$ if it is larger.

Fringe separation $s = W/N$: $N$, the number of spacings, is an exact count, so $\Delta s = \Delta W/N$. Measuring across ten spacings makes $\Delta s$ ten times smaller than measuring one spacing with the same $\Delta W$.

Slit-to-screen distance $D$: the slits are inside a holder, so you can't put the end of the rule exactly on them, and joining metre rules end to end and keeping them straight adds more. ± 1 cm is realistic: much larger than the rule's ± 0.5 mm.

Slit separation $d$: this comes from the manufacturer, so use the tolerance your teacher gives you. Because $d$ is the same for every point, its uncertainty doesn't show up as scatter on the graph: you add its percentage uncertainty to the gradient's at the end.

Quote uncertainties to 1 significant figure (usual), or 2 if the uncertainty itself is known precisely, with each value rounded to the same decimal place. See Physics skills.

5. Part 1: setting up and observing the pattern

  1. Clamp the laser low on the bench, pointing along the bench towards the screen, with the beam horizontal. Place the screen about 2 m away, perpendicular to the beam. Tape a fresh sheet of white paper to the screen where the beam lands.
  2. With the laser off, fix the slit plate in its holder about 5–10 cm in front of the laser, with the plate perpendicular to the beam. Select the double slit your teacher tells you (ideally $d$ = 0.25 mm, $b$ = 0.04 mm). Record $d$ and $b$ below.
  3. Switch the laser on. Slide or turn the slit plate until the spot falls centrally across both slits: the pattern on the screen becomes a row of evenly spaced, equally sharp fringes. Dim the room lights (safely: see the safety box).
  4. Look carefully at the pattern. Sketch it on paper, numbering the bright fringes 0 (the central fringe), 1, 2, 3… on each side. Note any fringes that are dimmer than their neighbours, or seem to be missing, and how the brightness changes as you move away from the centre. (You will describe this in Analysis question 8.)

Slit plate and laser

6. Part 2: measuring s at different distances

  1. Measure $D$ from the plane of the slits to the surface of the screen, keeping the metre rules straight and level beside the beam (not in it). Record $D$ in the table.
  2. Partner A marks the centre of each bright fringe from the 5th on the left to the 5th on the right with a short, sharp pencil line on the paper. (Mark from the side, so that your hand and head stay out of the beam.) If the 5th fringes are too faint to see clearly, use the 4th on each side and record $N$ = 8. If a fringe is missing, still count its place.
  3. Switch the laser off, remove the paper, label it with $D$, and tape on a fresh sheet. Partner B repeats step 6 on the new sheet without looking at A's marks.
  4. With the room lights on, measure the width $W$ between the two end marks on each sheet with the 30 cm ruler. Record both values of $W$ and the number of spacings $N$ in the table.
  5. Move the screen so that $D$ changes by about 0.4 m, keeping the screen perpendicular to the beam and the beam still centred on the slits. Repeat steps 5–8 until you have six distances between 1.0 m and 3.0 m. (If the screen is a fixed wall, move the laser and slit plate together along the bench instead, and recheck the alignment each time.)
  6. Switch the laser off. Read its label, and record the stated wavelength and, if given, its tolerance in the panel in Part 1.

For each row, work out the mean of the two values of $W$, then $\Delta W$: the larger of your realistic estimate (about ± 2 mm) and the half range of the two partners' values. Then fill in $s = W/N$ (using the mean $W$) and $\Delta s = \Delta W/N$.

7. Your graph

The graph updates as you type. It starts with fringe separation $s$ on the vertical axis and slit-to-screen distance $D$ on the horizontal axis, so its gradient is $\lambda/d$.

The graph tool uses one uncertainty for every point on each axis. Type 0.01 (your ΔD in metres) in the horizontal axis's uncertainty box, and your largest Δs in the vertical axis's box. Then tick Steepest and shallowest lines: they pass through every uncertainty bar, and the tool gives the gradient ± its uncertainty (half the difference between the gradients of the steepest and shallowest lines) and the y-intercept ± its uncertainty. Press Add this graph to my report to keep it.

Units: with $s$ in mm and $D$ in m, the gradient is in $\text{mm m}^{-1}$. Multiply it (and its uncertainty) by $10^{-3}$ to make it a pure number (metres per metre) before using $\lambda = d \times \text{gradient}$ with $d$ in metres.
