2. Emf and internal resistance

A cell does work on the charges that pass through it. The work done per unit charge is the cell's electromotive force (emf), $\varepsilon$.

Not all of this energy reaches the external circuit. The charges also have to pass through the materials inside the cell, which behave like a small resistor in series with it. This is the cell's internal resistance, $r$.

If the cell drives a current $I$ through an external circuit of total resistance $R$, the data booklet gives

$$\varepsilon = I(R + r)$$

The pd across the cell's terminals (the terminal pd, $V$) is the pd across the external circuit, $V = IR$. Substituting gives

$$V = \varepsilon - Ir$$

So the more current the cell supplies, the more of its emf is "used up" inside it, and the lower the terminal pd.

Compare this with the equation of a straight line, $y = mx + c$. A graph of $V$ (vertical) against $I$ (horizontal) should be a straight line with gradient $= -r$ and vertical intercept $= \varepsilon$. The intercept is the terminal pd when no current flows, so you can also check it directly, by measuring the pd across the cell on open circuit.

For an AA cell, the internal resistance is small, typically a few tenths of an ohm. The terminal pd changes by only a tenth of a volt or so over the whole experiment, so careful, quick readings matter.

3. Equipment and safety

  • Vernier interface (or LabQuest) and a laptop with Logger Pro 3
  • Vernier Voltage Probe (±10 V)
  • Vernier Current Probe (±0.6 A)
  • (Instead of the probes: two digital multimeters)
  • A 1.5 V AA cell (alkaline or zinc–carbon, not rechargeable) in a cell holder
  • A fixed protective resistor, 3.3 Ω, rated at least 1 W
  • A rheostat (variable resistor), about 20–50 Ω, rated for at least 0.5 A
  • A switch (push switch or knife switch)
  • Connecting leads with crocodile clips

Safety:

  • Never connect the cell without the protective resistor in the circuit. With the rheostat at zero, a short circuit could drive several amperes: the wires and the cell get hot, the cell can leak, and the Current Probe (maximum 0.6 A) can be damaged.
  • Use ordinary alkaline or zinc–carbon cells only. Rechargeable and lithium cells can supply very large currents if they are short-circuited.
  • The protective resistor dissipates up to about 0.7 W and can become warm. Don't hold it, and open the switch between readings.
  • Ask your teacher to check the circuit before you close the switch for the first time.

4. Before the lab

Uncertainties

Terminal pd $V$ and current $I$: the sensors are very precise (Logger Pro shows several decimal places), but the readings drift and flicker by a few millivolts and milliamperes while the switch is closed. This is usually bigger than the display's resolution.

So take three readings at each rheostat setting, opening and closing the switch each time, and use half the range, $\frac{\text{max} - \text{min}}{2}$, as the uncertainty. If your three readings are identical, use the smallest change the display can show instead.

Open-circuit pd: record the steady reading with the switch open, with the same uncertainty rule.

Multimeters: if you use multimeters instead, a single reading is ± the smallest digit displayed. The ammeter must be on a range that can carry 0.5 A (often the 10 A or 2 A range), which may only show 0.01 A: note how this limits your precision.

Give each uncertainty to 1 significant figure (2 when the uncertainty itself is known precisely), and round the mean to the same decimal place, e.g. $(1.482 \pm 0.003)$ V. See Physics skills.

5. Setting up

  1. Build the circuit in the diagram, with the switch open. Clip the Voltage Probe leads directly to the two terminals of the cell holder (red to +). The Current Probe goes in series, with the current entering at its red terminal.
  2. Plug the Voltage Probe into CH 1 and the Current Probe into CH 2 of the interface, and open Logger Pro 3. Both sensors should be identified automatically.
  3. Zero the sensors: with the switch open, unclip the Voltage Probe leads from the cell and touch them together, then choose Experiment ▸ Zero. Clip the Voltage Probe back onto the cell's terminals. (Zeroing with the probe still on the cell would wrongly set the cell's emf to zero.)
  4. Choose Experiment ▸ Data Collection and set the Mode to Events with Entry. Name the entry column "Setting" (no units). Each click of Keep will now store one pair of V and I readings.
  5. Ask your teacher to check the circuit.

Using two multimeters instead: connect one, set to volts, across the cell holder's terminals in place of the Voltage Probe, and the other, set to a range that can carry 0.5 A, in series in place of the Current Probe. Skip the Logger Pro steps, and read both displays each time you close the switch.

6. Collecting data

  1. With the switch open, record the open-circuit pd of the cell (the "before" value) in the Measurements box below.
  2. Set the rheostat to its maximum resistance. Click Collect. Close the switch, wait about 2 s for the readings to settle, click Keep, enter the setting number, then open the switch.
  3. Do this twice more at the same setting, so you have three pairs of readings. Let the cell rest for about 15 s with the switch open between readings.
  4. Change the rheostat to give at least six different settings, spread fairly evenly across the current range, from about 0.05 A up to the largest current (rheostat at zero, about 0.4 A). Take the settings in a mixed order (not always increasing the current), so that any slow change in the cell doesn't quietly bend your line. Take three pairs of readings at each setting, as in steps 2 and 3.
  5. Click Stop. With the switch open, record the open-circuit pd again (the "after" value) in the Measurements box.
  6. Save your Logger Pro file. Copy your readings into the table below: one row for each setting, with its three currents and three pds.
  7. For each row, fill in Mean I and Mean V (the mean of the three readings), and ΔI and ΔV (half the range, or the smallest change the display can show if the three readings are identical). Round each mean to the same decimal place as its uncertainty.

7. Your graph

The graph updates as you type. It starts with mean terminal pd on the vertical axis and mean current on the horizontal axis, so the gradient is −r and the vertical intercept is ε.

Untick Start the axes at zero: your pds only span about a tenth of a volt, so with the vertical axis starting at zero the line looks flat and the uncertainty bars are invisible. The results under the graph still give the intercept.

The graph tool uses one uncertainty for every point on each axis. Type your largest ΔV in the vertical axis's uncertainty box and your largest ΔI in the horizontal axis's box (a cautious choice), then tick Steepest and shallowest lines. Press Add this graph to my report to keep it.

Each point has an uncertainty box (ΔI across, ΔV up and down). Because the gradient is negative, the steepest line runs from the top-right corner of the first (smallest-current) box, $(I + \Delta I,\ V + \Delta V)$, to the bottom-left corner of the last box, $(I - \Delta I,\ V - \Delta V)$: the biggest drop in $V$ over the smallest change in $I$. The shallowest line runs from the bottom-left corner of the first box to the top-right corner of the last. Both must pass through every uncertainty bar.

The uncertainty in $r$ is half the difference between the two lines' gradients, and the uncertainty in $\varepsilon$ is half the difference between their intercepts: the tool works out both.
