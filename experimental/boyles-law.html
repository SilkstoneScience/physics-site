Boyle's law

A lab with Vernier sensors: trap some air in a syringe connected to a gas pressure sensor, squash it and let it expand, and see how its pressure depends on its volume at constant temperature. B.3 Gas laws Vernier

Aim: to test Boyle's law for a fixed amount of air at constant temperature, to find and correct the hidden "dead volume" of the apparatus, and to estimate how much air was trapped.

Tools and inquiry: measuring volume, pressure and temperature (Tool 1); collecting data with sensors, and processing it with graphs (Tool 2); linearising a relationship and propagating uncertainties (Tool 3); collecting and processing data, and concluding and evaluating (Inquiry 2 and 3).

2. Theory: Boyle's law and the dead volume

Air is made of an enormous number of molecules moving randomly. Each time a molecule hits a wall, its momentum changes, so it pushes on the wall. The pressure of the gas is the total perpendicular force per unit area:

$$P = \frac{F}{A}$$

Squash the same air into a smaller volume at the same temperature, and the molecules move just as fast on average, but each one reaches the walls more often. So the pressure rises.

For a fixed amount of gas at constant temperature, this gives Boyle's law: the pressure is inversely proportional to the volume,

$$P \propto \frac{1}{V} \qquad\text{or}\qquad PV = \text{constant}$$

Boyle's law is one of the empirical gas laws that combine into the ideal gas equation in the data booklet (see B.3 Gas laws):

$$PV = nRT \qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac{PV}{T} = \text{constant}$$

Here $n$ is the amount of gas in moles, $R = 8.31$ $\text{J K}^{-1}\,\text{mol}^{-1}$ and $T$ is the temperature in kelvin.

A graph of $P$ against $V$ is a curve, so it is hard to judge by eye whether it really is "inverse". A graph of $P$ against $1/V$ should be a straight line through the origin, with a gradient equal to $nRT$. If you measure $T$, the gradient tells you how much air you trapped.

There is a catch. The volume you read from the syringe scale is not the whole volume of the trapped air: a little extra air sits in the connector and inside the sensor, out of sight. This dead volume $V_0$ is always there, so the true volume is $V + V_0$. In this lab you will find $V_0$ from your own data and correct for it.

3. Equipment and safety

  • Vernier interface and a laptop with Logger Pro 3
  • Vernier Gas Pressure Sensor (0–210 kPa) with its 20 mL plastic syringe
  • Vernier Temperature Probe, or a thermometer, for the room (air) temperature

Safety: the sensor is only rated to 210 kPa. Never push the plunger below the 5.0 mL mark when you start at 10.0 mL: at 5.0 mL the pressure is already close to 190 kPa. Push and pull slowly and steadily.

Hold the syringe by the plunger end and the finger flange only. At small volumes the plunger needs a firm push (over 20 N): keep the syringe pointing away from faces, and don't let the plunger spring back suddenly when you let go.

4. Before the lab

Uncertainties

  • Volume $V$: record the smallest division on your syringe. You need one reading of the plunger's ring, so the scale uncertainty is ± half a division. Judging the front edge of the ring may be harder than this: decide whether a larger value is more realistic, and say why.
  • Pressure $P$: Logger Pro shows the pressure to 0.1 kPa, but the reading drifts a little as it settles. You will take each volume twice (in two runs), so use half the range of the two readings, or the flicker of the reading if that is larger.
  • Temperature $T$: read the room (air) temperature with the Temperature Probe or thermometer at the start and at the end. Use the change, or the instrument uncertainty if that is larger. Convert to kelvin.

Quote each uncertainty to 1 significant figure, with the value rounded to the same decimal place, for example $P = (124 \pm 2)$ kPa. See Physics skills.

5. Setting up

  1. Connect the Gas Pressure Sensor to the interface and open Logger Pro 3. Check that the pressure units are kPa. With nothing attached, the sensor reads the atmospheric pressure (about 100 kPa): record it below.
  2. Choose Experiment ▸ Data Collection, set the Mode to Events with Entry, and enter Volume as the column name and mL as the units.
  3. With the syringe disconnected, set the front edge of the plunger's black ring to the 10.0 mL mark. Then screw the syringe firmly onto the sensor (finger-tight, a gentle twist). It now holds a fixed amount of air at atmospheric pressure.
  4. Record the room temperature, the smallest division on the syringe and your uncertainty in each volume reading below.

Before you start

6. Collecting data

Run 1

  1. Click Collect. Wait for the pressure to settle, then click Keep and type 10.0 for the volume.
  2. Push the plunger slowly in to 8.0 mL. Hold it steady (at the plunger, not the barrel), wait about 15 s until the pressure stops changing, then click Keep and type the volume.
  3. Repeat at 7.0, 6.0 and 5.0 mL. Don't go below 5.0 mL.
  4. Let the plunger out slowly, past 10 mL without recording, and record the pressure at 12.0, 14.0, 16.0, 18.0 and 20.0 mL, waiting for each reading to settle.

Run 2

  1. Push the plunger in to about 17 mL, then let it back out and set it to 20.0 mL again, so that the repeat is a fresh setting. Wait about 15 s, then click Keep (your second reading at 20.0 mL).
  2. Push the plunger slowly back in, recording at 18.0, 16.0, 14.0, 12.0, 10.0, 8.0, 7.0, 6.0 and 5.0 mL. Click Stop.
  3. Check for a leak: compare your two readings at 10.0 mL. They should agree within about 1 kPa. If the second is clearly lower, air has escaped: tighten the connector and repeat both runs.
  4. Record the room temperature again below. Unscrew the syringe and save the file.

At the end

A first look in Logger Pro

  1. Look at the graph of pressure against volume. Then choose Analyze ▸ Curve Fit and try the inverse fit, P = A/V. Note how well it fits the points at small volume.
  2. Choose Data ▸ New Calculated Column, name it Inverse volume (units mL−1) with the equation 1/"Volume", and plot pressure against it. Use Analyze ▸ Linear Fit and note the intercept.

Now copy both runs into the table: one row for each volume, with run 1 and run 2 side by side.

7. Your graphs

All four graphs come from the same table. The graph updates as you type, and starts with mean pressure on the vertical axis against syringe volume on the horizontal axis. For each graph, choose the Variable on each axis, and use Plot to plot 1/x where you need it.

The graph tool uses one uncertainty for every point on each axis, so type the largest one (a cautious choice). For an axis that shows 1/x, the uncertainty is different for every point, but its percentage is the same as the quantity's: $\Delta(1/V) = \dfrac{\Delta V}{V} \times \dfrac{1}{V}$ and $\Delta(1/P) = \dfrac{\Delta P}{P} \times \dfrac{1}{P}$. So type the largest percentage uncertainty with a % sign, for example 2%. Press Add this graph to my report after each graph.

  • Graph 1, P against V: keep the starting choice. Type your largest ΔP as the vertical uncertainty and ΔV as the horizontal one. This graph should be a curve, so untick Line of best fit (the straight-line numbers printed under the graph mean nothing for a curve: ignore them).
  • Graph 2, P against 1/V: on the horizontal axis, set Plot to 1/x, and type the largest percentage uncertainty in V (at 5.0 mL) as a percentage. Tick Line of best fit again. Look carefully: do the points lie on a straight line through the origin, or do they bend away from the line at the smallest volumes?
  • Graph 3, V against 1/P: put syringe volume on the vertical axis (plot x, uncertainty ΔV) and mean pressure on the horizontal axis (plot 1/x, uncertainty = your largest percentage uncertainty in P). Tick Steepest and shallowest lines. By question P4, the gradient is k = nRT and the intercept is −V0. The tool gives the gradient with its uncertainty, Δk = (kmax − kmin)/2, and the intercept with its uncertainty, which is your ΔV0. (Its unit for the gradient, mL / kPa−1, is the same as kPa mL.)
  • Graph 4, P against 1/(V + V0): first fill in the last column of the table, V + V0, using your value of V0. Then put mean pressure on the vertical axis and corrected volume on the horizontal axis with Plot set to 1/x. For the horizontal uncertainty use ΔV only (type the largest percentage, at 5.0 mL): ΔV0 shifts every point by the same amount, so it doesn't belong in each point's bar.
