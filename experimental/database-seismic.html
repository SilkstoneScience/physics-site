The speed of seismic waves Database

A database practical: the measurements were made by others, and your job is to analyse them. C.2 Wave model

Aim: to find the speeds of P waves and S waves through the Earth's crust, using the times at which waves from a real earthquake reached 92 seismometers.

Skills practised: working with a large real data set, gradients and what they mean, choosing which data to include, outliers, interpreting an intercept, and judging the uncertainty of data you didn't collect.

Theory

An earthquake sends out two kinds of wave through the rock:

  • P waves (primary) are longitudinal. They are faster, so they arrive first.
  • S waves (secondary) are transverse. They are slower, so they arrive later.

If a wave travels at a constant speed $v$, the time it takes to reach a seismometer a distance $d$ away is $t = \dfrac{d}{v}$. So a graph of travel time $t$ (on the $y$-axis) against distance $d$ (on the $x$-axis) is a straight line with gradient $\dfrac{1}{v}$:

$$v = \frac{1}{\text{gradient}}$$

The S wave arrives after the P wave, and the gap grows with distance:

$$t_S - t_P = d\left(\frac{1}{v_S} - \frac{1}{v_P}\right)$$

A single seismometer can use this gap to work out how far away an earthquake was, without knowing when it happened. Three seismometers can then locate it.

The data

On 14 April 2025 at 17:08:28 UTC, a magnitude 5.2 earthquake struck 5 km south of Julian, California, 14.3 km below the surface. The Southern California Seismic Network recorded it on hundreds of seismometers, and its analysts marked (or "picked") the moment each wave arrived.

The data set lists the 92 stations, from 15 km to 110 km away, where both the P and S arrivals were picked:

  • station: the network and station code;
  • epicentral distance $d$: the distance along the surface from the epicentre, the point directly above the earthquake (we converted it from degrees to km);
  • $t_P$ and $t_S$: the travel times of the P and S waves, from the time of the earthquake to the picked arrival;
  • S minus P time: $t_S - t_P$, which we calculated.

Data: Southern California Seismic Network (Caltech and USGS), from the USGS earthquake catalogue (event ci40925991). USGS data are in the public domain.

Tasks

  1. The P wave. Plot $t_P$ against $d$. Is it a straight line? Find the gradient, and the speed of P waves in km s−1.
  2. The S wave. Do the same for $t_S$. How do the two speeds compare? Work out $v_P / v_S$.
  3. Outliers. Some points lie off the line. Decide which to leave out, and justify each one. Does leaving them out change the speeds much?
  4. The intercept. At $d = 0$ the line doesn't pass through the origin. Why not? (Think about where the earthquake actually happened.)
  5. Locating an earthquake. Plot $t_S - t_P$ against $d$. Use your gradient to estimate how far away an earthquake was if a seismometer records an S–P time of 10.0 s.
  6. Uncertainties. No uncertainties are given. Estimate them from the scatter of the points, then find the uncertainty in each speed from the steepest and shallowest lines (see uncertainty in the gradient).

Worked example: from a gradient to a speed

For a different earthquake, a student's graph of P-wave travel time against distance has a gradient of 0.168 s km−1. The steepest and shallowest lines give 0.174 and 0.162 s km−1. Find the speed of the P waves with its uncertainty.

$v = \dfrac{1}{0.168} = 5.95$ km s−1.

Gradient uncertainty: $(0.174 - 0.162) \div 2 = 0.006$ s km−1, a fractional uncertainty of $\dfrac{0.006}{0.168} = 0.036$.

Taking the reciprocal keeps the same fractional uncertainty, so $\Delta v = 0.036 \times 5.95 = 0.2$ km s−1, and $v = (6.0 \pm 0.2)$ km s−1.

Worked example: how far away was the earthquake?

In a region where P waves travel at 6.0 km s−1 and S waves at 3.5 km s−1, a seismometer records the S wave 14.0 s after the P wave. How far away was the earthquake?

$\dfrac{1}{v_S} - \dfrac{1}{v_P} = \dfrac{1}{3.5} - \dfrac{1}{6.0} = 0.286 - 0.167 = 0.119$ s km−1.

$d = \dfrac{14.0}{0.119} = 118$ km, so about 120 km.

Evaluating

  • Picking arrivals: an analyst judges each arrival time from a wiggly trace. Why is the S arrival harder to pick than the P arrival? (It arrives while the ground is still shaking from the P wave.)
  • Distance: the earthquake happened 14.3 km underground, but $d$ is measured along the surface. For which stations does this matter most? How could you calculate the true straight-line distance?
  • The rock: the waves travel through different rocks on the way to different stations, and waves speed up deeper in the crust. How would this show up in your graph?
  • The time of the earthquake: nobody saw the earthquake start: its time and position were calculated from these same arrival times. Why does the S–P method avoid this problem?
  • The source: how reliable are data that have been checked by professional analysts? What would you need to know to judge them?

Check your understanding

Why is the gradient of a time–distance graph equal to $1/v$, not $v$?

Time is on the $y$-axis and distance on the $x$-axis, so the gradient is $\dfrac{\Delta t}{\Delta d}$, the time taken per kilometre. Speed is distance per unit time, $\dfrac{\Delta d}{\Delta t}$, which is the reciprocal.

S waves cannot travel through liquids. Why does this make sense for a transverse wave?

A transverse wave needs the medium to spring back when it is pushed sideways. A liquid flows instead of resisting a sideways (shear) push, so it cannot carry a transverse wave. P waves compress and stretch the medium, which liquids do resist, so P waves can pass through liquids. This is how seismologists showed that the Earth's outer core is liquid.
