Pressure, temperature and absolute zero

A lab with Vernier sensors: a sealed flask of air sits in a water bath at constant volume, a pressure sensor and a temperature probe record its pressure and temperature, and a long extrapolation of your graph estimates absolute zero. B.3 Gas laws Vernier

Aim: to measure how the pressure of a fixed volume of air changes with temperature, to estimate absolute zero (with its uncertainty) by extrapolating the graph, and to test whether pressure is directly proportional to kelvin temperature.

2. Theory: pressure, temperature and absolute zero

Air trapped in a rigid, sealed flask can't expand, so its volume and the amount of gas inside stay fixed. If you warm it, the molecules move faster on average. They hit the walls more often, and with a larger change in momentum each time, so the force on each square metre of wall (the pressure) increases.

For an ideal gas the data booklet gives

$$PV = nRT \qquad \text{and} \qquad \frac{PV}{T} = \text{constant}$$

where $P$ is the pressure, $V$ the volume, $n$ the amount of gas in moles, $R = 8.31\ \text{J K}^{-1}\,\text{mol}^{-1}$ and $T$ the absolute (kelvin) temperature (see B.3 Gas laws). With $V$ and $n$ constant,

$$P = \frac{nR}{V}\,T$$

so the pressure is directly proportional to the kelvin temperature, and the gradient of a graph of $P$ against $T$ is $nR/V$.

In the lab you measure temperature in degrees Celsius, $\theta$. Since $T/\text{K} = \theta/{}^\circ\text{C} + 273$, a graph of $P$ against $\theta$ should still be a straight line, but it will not pass through the origin.

If you extend (extrapolate) the line back to the temperature at which the pressure would fall to zero, you get an estimate of absolute zero: the lowest possible temperature, 0 K, which is −273 °C (more precisely −273.15 °C). Real gases condense into liquids long before this, so nobody can measure a gas there. The straight-line model lets you predict it from data taken between about 0 °C and 85 °C.

For a line $P = m\theta + c$, the pressure is zero when

$$\theta_0 = -\frac{c}{m}$$

This point lies about 270 °C beyond your nearest reading, so a small uncertainty in the gradient turns into a large uncertainty in the intercept. Finding out how large is a key part of this lab.

3. Equipment and safety

  • Vernier interface and a laptop with Logger Pro 3
  • Vernier Gas Pressure Sensor (absolute pressure, 0–210 kPa) with its short plastic tubing and Luer-lock connectors
  • 125 mL conical (Erlenmeyer) flask, clean and completely dry inside, with a rubber stopper fitted with a Luer-lock connector
  • Vernier Temperature Probe
  • 1 L beaker (deep enough to cover the flask up to its stopper) as the water bath
  • Clamp stand, boss and clamp to hold the flask under water (a flask full of air floats)
  • Kettle or hot plate, crushed ice, cold tap water, a spare beaker for pouring, and heat-proof gloves or tongs
  • Stirring rod and paper towels

Safety

  • Hot water: use heat-proof gloves or tongs when pouring water above about 50 °C. Pour slowly, and keep the bath on a stable, dry bench away from the edge. Report any scald to your teacher straight away and cool it under running water.
  • Glassware: check the flask for cracks before you use it. Never put a cold flask straight into very hot water: warm it in steps, as the method does.
  • Stopper: at 80 °C the pressure inside is about 20 kPa above atmospheric, enough to push out a loose stopper. Twist the stopper in firmly, and keep the clamp just below the neck so that the flask can't tip.
  • Electrical: keep the interface, the pressure sensor and the computer well away from the water. Only the flask and the temperature probe go in the bath.

4. Before the lab

Uncertainties

  • Temperature $\theta$: the probe displays to 0.1 °C, but that isn't a realistic uncertainty. The bath is warmer at the top than at the bottom, and it cools while you read it. Stir, then watch the reading for about 20 s: half the range it wanders over is a sensible uncertainty, usually ±0.3 °C to ±0.5 °C, and larger for the hottest baths.
  • Pressure $P$: the sensor displays to 0.1 kPa. Once the reading has settled, note how much it still fluctuates and use half of that range (often about ±0.2 kPa).

Give each uncertainty to 1 significant figure, and round the value to the same decimal place, for example $\theta = (48.6 \pm 0.4)$ °C and $P = (112.3 \pm 0.2)$ kPa. Adding 273 to a temperature doesn't change its absolute uncertainty. See Physics skills.

Worked example: how extrapolating magnifies uncertainty

A group's data are centred on 45 °C and 109.6 kPa, and the gradient of their line is 0.344 $\text{kPa °C}^{-1}$. The steepest and shallowest lines have gradients about 3% larger and smaller: 0.354 and 0.334 $\text{kPa °C}^{-1}$. Estimate absolute zero and its uncertainty. (Example values, not real data.)

Every line passes through (or very close to) the centre of the data. From there, the pressure has to fall by 109.6 kPa to reach zero, so the line meets the temperature axis at $45 - 109.6 / m$.

Best-fit line: $45 - 109.6 / 0.344 \approx -274$ °C.

Steepest line: $45 - 109.6 / 0.354 \approx -265$ °C. Shallowest line: $45 - 109.6 / 0.334 \approx -283$ °C.

Uncertainty: $(283 - 265) / 2 = 9$ °C, so $\theta_0 = (-274 \pm 9)$ °C. A 3% uncertainty in the gradient has become about ±9 °C in absolute zero, because the intercept is so far from the data.

5. Setting up

  1. Make sure the inside of the flask is completely dry. (Water left inside evaporates when it is heated and adds its own vapour pressure, which spoils the results.)
  2. Connect the Gas Pressure Sensor to CH 1 and the Temperature Probe to CH 2 of the interface, and open Logger Pro 3.
  3. Choose Experiment ▸ Data Collection… and, on the Collection tab, set Mode to Selected Events. Each time you click Keep, Logger Pro will record one pressure and one temperature.
  4. Click the horizontal axis label and choose Temperature; click the vertical axis label and choose Pressure.
  5. Connect the tubing between the sensor and the stopper, keeping it as short as possible. With the flask still open to the room, check that the pressure reads atmospheric pressure (about 100 kPa) and record it below. Then twist the stopper firmly into the flask. Don't use Experiment ▸ Zero: you need the absolute pressure.
  6. Clamp the flask upright in the empty 1 L beaker, with the clamp just below the neck. Put the Temperature Probe in the beaker beside the flask, level with its middle and not touching the glass.

Measurements

6. Collecting data

  1. Fill the beaker with ice and a little cold water, so that the flask is covered up to the base of the stopper. Stir gently and wait until both readings have settled: no steady drift for about 30 s (this can take 2–3 minutes). Click ▶ Collect, then Keep. Record θ and P, with their uncertainties, in the table.
  2. Remove the ice and replace the water with room-temperature water. Stir, wait for equilibrium, click Keep and record.
  3. Pour out some water and add hot water from the kettle to raise the bath by about 15 °C. Keep the flask covered up to the stopper. Stir, wait for equilibrium, click Keep and record.
  4. Repeat step 9 until you reach about 80–85 °C, giving at least six temperatures in total, spread as evenly as you can. Click ■ Stop when you have finished.
  5. Choose Analyze ▸ Linear Fit to see a first estimate of the line on screen. Save your file.
  6. Optional check (equilibrium and leaks): let the bath cool, recording two or three more points on the way down. If they lie on the same line as your heating points, the readings were at equilibrium and the flask didn't leak.

7. Your graphs

The graph updates as you type. The graph tool uses one uncertainty for every point on each axis: type your largest uncertainty for each quantity (a cautious choice). The "Start the axes at zero" box only changes what you see, not the numbers in the results. The tool doesn't extend the lines as far as the temperature axis, so you will find absolute zero from the numbers it gives you, as below. Make three graphs, and press Add this graph to my report after each one.

Graph 1: pressure against Celsius temperature

  1. The graph starts with pressure P on the vertical axis and temperature θ on the horizontal axis. Untick Start the axes at zero, so that your points spread across the graph and you can judge whether they lie on a straight line.
  2. Type your largest ΔP in the vertical axis's uncertainty box and your largest Δθ in the horizontal axis's box, then tick Steepest and shallowest lines.
  3. The results give the gradient $m \pm \Delta m$ (the tool works out $\Delta m$ as half the difference between the steepest and shallowest gradients) and the y-intercept $c$, the pressure at 0 °C. Your best estimate of absolute zero is $\theta_0 = -c/m$.

Graph 2: temperature against pressure, for absolute zero and its uncertainty

  1. Swap the axes: choose temperature θ for the vertical axis and pressure P for the horizontal axis. Swap the uncertainties too (Δθ now goes in the vertical box).
  2. Now the y-intercept is the temperature at which the pressure is zero: your value of $\theta_0$. The tool gives it with an uncertainty, half the difference between the intercepts of the steepest and shallowest lines. That is your $\Delta\theta_0$.

Why this works: a line that passes through every uncertainty bar still does when you swap the axes, so the steepest and shallowest lines are the same lines as on graph 1, just drawn the other way round. Reading their intercepts here gives the same answer as calculating $-c/m$ for each of them. Graph 1's steepest line becomes this graph's shallowest line (its gradient is 1/m), so the labels swap, but the ± is unaffected. The best-fit value may differ very slightly from graph 1's $-c/m$ (usually by much less than a degree), because the computer's least-squares line is worked out slightly differently when the axes are swapped; quote this graph's value and uncertainty. Check: for the steepest and shallowest gradients from graph 1, work out $\bar\theta - \bar P / m$, where $\bar\theta$ and $\bar P$ are the means of your readings, as in the worked example. You should get close to the two ends of your range.

Graph 3: pressure against kelvin temperature

  1. Fill in the T column of the table ($T = \theta + 273$). Choose pressure P for the vertical axis and kelvin temperature T for the horizontal axis, with ΔP and ΔT (the same as Δθ) as the uncertainties.
  2. Tick Start the axes at zero, so that you can see the origin, and tick Steepest and shallowest lines. Look at the y-intercept and its uncertainty: is zero inside that range?
