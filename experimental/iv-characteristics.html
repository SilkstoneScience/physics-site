1. Skills you will practise

  • Connecting a current sensor in series and a voltage sensor in parallel, with the correct polarity.
  • Using a potential divider to vary the potential difference across a component from zero.
  • Estimating realistic uncertainties from fluctuating sensor readings, and propagating them through R = V/I.
  • Plotting graphs with uncertainty bars, and finding a gradient and its uncertainty from the steepest and shallowest lines.
  • Deciding from a graph whether a relationship is proportional, and explaining a curve with a physical model.

Tools and inquiry: measuring current and potential difference with sensors, and resistance with a multimeter (Tool 1); collecting data with sensors, and processing it with graphs (Tool 2); collecting and processing data, and concluding and evaluating (Inquiry 2 and 3).

2. Background

The resistance of a component is defined as the potential difference across it divided by the current through it:

$$R = \frac{V}{I}$$

This definition works for any component at any instant. Ohm's law is a stronger claim: for some conductors the current is directly proportional to the potential difference, so $R$ stays the same whatever the p.d. A component that behaves like this is called ohmic. Its I–V graph is a straight line through the origin, for both directions of current.

A component whose resistance changes with the p.d. is non-ohmic: its I–V graph is not a straight line through the origin.

The power dissipated in a component is

$$P = IV = I^2 R$$

and this energy heats the component. The syllabus treats a metal conductor at constant temperature as ohmic. (Both equations are in the data booklet: see B.5 Ohm's law: ohmic and non-ohmic conductors.)

In this lab you will measure the I–V characteristic of a fixed resistor (Part 1) and of a filament lamp (Part 2), in both directions, and use your graphs to decide which is ohmic.

3. Equipment and safety

  • Vernier interface (LabQuest or LabQuest Mini) and a laptop with Logger Pro 3
  • Vernier Current Probe (range ±0.6 A) and Vernier Voltage Probe (range ±10 V)
  • A variable d.c. power supply, set to 6 V
  • A rheostat, about 50–100 Ω (used as a potential divider)
  • A fixed resistor, 47 Ω (any value from 33 Ω to 50 Ω will do), with a power rating of 1 W or more
  • A 6 V filament lamp in a holder. Check the current rating on its base: use a lamp rated 0.3 A or less, so that the current at 6 V is well below the Current Probe's 0.6 A. Always start at 0 V: a cold filament switched straight on draws a surge many times its rated current.
  • A multimeter (ohmmeter setting)
  • Connecting leads with crocodile clips
  • Extension only: a coloured LED and a 220 Ω resistor

Safety:

  • The Current Probe must never carry more than 0.6 A. Always start each run with the potential divider at 0 V and increase the p.d. slowly. A cold filament has a much lower resistance than a hot one, so switching a lamp straight on at 6 V causes a brief surge of current.
  • The Current Probe goes in series with the component. Never connect it across the component or across the supply: it has almost no resistance, so it would short-circuit the supply.
  • Don't exceed 6 V across any component, or the lamp's rated voltage. The lamp, the resistor and the rheostat can become hot: switch off between runs, and let the lamp cool before you touch it.
  • Ask your teacher to check your circuit before you switch on the power supply.

4. Before the lab

Uncertainties

The sensors display many digits, but the last ones flicker. A realistic uncertainty comes from how much a reading fluctuates when nothing is being changed, not just from the resolution. You will measure this fluctuation once the circuit is built (see Finding your uncertainties).

For each calculated resistance, add the fractional uncertainties:

$$\frac{\Delta R}{R} = \frac{\Delta V}{V} + \frac{\Delta I}{I}$$

For example, $V = (3.50 \pm 0.01)$ V and $I = (0.0745 \pm 0.0006)$ A give $R = 46.98\ \Omega$ and $\dfrac{\Delta R}{R} = 0.0029 + 0.0081 = 0.0110$, so $R = (47.0 \pm 0.5)\ \Omega$. Notice that small readings have the largest fractional uncertainties.

Quote each uncertainty to 1 significant figure (2 when the uncertainty itself is known precisely), with the best estimate rounded to the same decimal place. See Physics skills.

5. Setting up

Multimeter reading for the resistor

  1. Before you build the circuit, measure the resistance of the resistor with the multimeter on its ohmmeter setting. Record it, with its uncertainty, above.
  2. Connect the Current Probe to CH 1 and the Voltage Probe to CH 2 of the interface, and open a new file in Logger Pro 3.
  3. Build the circuit in the diagram with the resistor as the component. Connect the red terminal of the Current Probe and the red lead of the Voltage Probe on the side nearer the positive terminal of the supply. Set the potential divider to 0 V.
  4. Ask your teacher to check your circuit.
  5. With the power supply switched off (no current, no p.d.), choose Experiment ▸ Zero and zero both sensors.

Finding your uncertainties

  1. Switch on and set the potential divider to give about 3 V across the resistor.
  2. Switch Logger Pro to time-based mode (Experiment ▸ Data Collection ▸ Mode: Time Based), click Collect and collect data for 10 s.
  3. Select the whole run and choose Analyze ▸ Statistics for each sensor. Record the minimum and maximum of V and of I below.
  4. Your uncertainties are half the range: $\Delta V = \dfrac{V_{\max} - V_{\min}}{2}$ and $\Delta I = \dfrac{I_{\max} - I_{\min}}{2}$. If half the range is smaller than the sensor's resolution (typically about 0.3 mA for the Current Probe and about 5 mV for the Voltage Probe on a LabQuest: check the values in each sensor's user manual), use the resolution instead. You will repeat this check for the lamp at a low p.d. in Part 2, where the currents are smallest.

Uncertainties for the resistor (at about 3 V)

Getting Logger Pro ready to record

  1. Choose Experiment ▸ Data Collection, set Mode to Selected Events, and click Done. In this mode Logger Pro records one pair of readings each time you click Keep.
  2. Click the vertical-axis label of the graph and choose Potential; click the horizontal-axis label and choose Current.
  3. Set the potential divider back to 0 V.

6. Part 1: fixed resistor

  1. Click Collect. With the p.d. at 0 V, wait for the readings to settle and click Keep.
  2. Increase the p.d. by about 0.5 V, wait for the readings to settle, and click Keep. Repeat up to about 6 V (about 12 readings). Click Stop and set the p.d. back to 0 V. Don't leave the resistor at the highest p.d. for longer than you need.
  3. Choose Analyze ▸ Linear Fit and record the gradient and intercept below. Copy your readings into the table.
  4. Switch off. Choose Experiment ▸ Store Latest Run first, so the next run doesn't overwrite this one. Swap the two leads at the power-supply terminals, so the current flows the other way through the whole circuit.
  5. Repeat steps 1–3 from 0 V to about −6 V. Both readings should now be negative: record them in the table with their signs.

Logger Pro linear fits (V against I)

Your Part 1 graph

The graph updates as you type. It starts with potential difference on the vertical axis and current on the horizontal axis, so the gradient is the resistance. Use the readings from both directions.

The graph tool uses one uncertainty for every point on each axis. Type your ΔV in the vertical axis's uncertainty box and your ΔI in the horizontal axis's box (if they differ between readings, type the largest), then tick Steepest and shallowest lines. The uncertainty in the resistance is half the difference between their gradients, ΔR = (gradientmax − gradientmin) / 2, so quote R = (gradient ± ΔR) Ω. Check whether the intercept is zero within its uncertainty. Press Add this graph to my report to keep it.

7. Part 2: filament lamp

  1. With the lamp cold and disconnected, measure its resistance with the multimeter on its ohmmeter setting. Record it below.
  2. Replace the resistor with the lamp, with the leads back the right way round. Set the potential divider to 0 V before switching on.
  3. First choose Experiment ▸ Store Latest Run, so that your Part 1 reversed run is kept. Then repeat the uncertainty check (Finding your uncertainties) at a low p.d., about 0.5 V, and record ΔV and ΔI for the lamp below. Then set Selected Events mode again and the p.d. back to 0 V.
  4. Choose Experiment ▸ Store Latest Run again, so the uncertainty run is kept too. Click Collect and take readings as in Part 1, but in steps of about 0.1 V up to 1.0 V (the graph bends most here), then in steps of about 0.5 V up to 6 V. After each change, wait until the current stops drifting (the filament needs a few seconds to reach a steady temperature) before clicking Keep. Note the p.d. at which the filament first glows.
  5. Click Stop and set the p.d. back to 0 V. Choose Data ▸ New Calculated Column, name it Resistance (unit Ω), and enter the expression "Potential"/"Current". (Logger Pro may show an error for the 0 V row: leave that row out.) Copy your readings into the table.
  6. Switch off. Choose Experiment ▸ Store Latest Run first. Swap the leads at the power-supply terminals and repeat steps 4–5 from 0 V to −6 V, using bigger steps (about 1 V). Let the lamp cool before you take it apart.

Lamp measurements

Your Part 2 graphs

The graph starts with current on the vertical axis and potential difference on the horizontal axis: the I–V characteristic.

Type your ΔI and ΔV in the uncertainty boxes (every point gets uncertainty bars). The lamp's graph isn't a straight line, so untick Line of best fit and don't use the steepest and shallowest lines (ignore the straight-line numbers printed under the graph: they mean nothing for a curve). The tool doesn't draw curves: look at the shape the points make, and describe the smooth curve through them in your analysis. Press Add this graph to my report. Then make a second graph: choose resistance for the vertical axis and current for the horizontal axis, type your largest ΔR in the vertical axis's uncertainty box (or a percentage, such as 5%), and add this graph to your report too.
