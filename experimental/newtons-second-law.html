Newton's second law

A lab with Vernier sensors: a falling mass pulls a cart along a level track, a force sensor on the cart measures the pull, and a motion encoder tracks the cart's motion. A.2 Forces and momentum Vernier

Aim: to find how the acceleration of a cart depends on the resultant force on it, and to find the cart's mass from a graph.

1. Skills you will practise

  • Writing every measurement with its uncertainty, and the result to a sensible number of significant figures.
  • Using repeated trials, with half the range as the uncertainty of the mean.
  • Plotting a graph with uncertainty bars and a line of best fit.
  • Explaining what a gradient means physically, and finding its uncertainty from the steepest and shallowest lines.
  • Comparing a value from a graph with one measured directly.

Tools and inquiry: measuring mass and force (Tool 1); collecting data with sensors, and processing it with graphs (Tool 2); collecting and processing data, and concluding and evaluating (Inquiry 2 and 3).

2. Equipment and safety

  • Vernier interface and a laptop with Logger Pro 3
  • Vernier Dynamics Track with a pulley and pulley bracket, and a dynamics cart
  • Vernier Dual-Range Force Sensor, fixed to the cart
  • Vernier Motion Encoder (or a Motion Detector)
  • String, a mass hanger and slotted masses, and a balance

Safety: put something soft on the floor under the hanging mass. Catch the cart before it reaches the pulley, and before the hanging mass hits the floor. Keep fingers and hair away from the pulley while the string is moving.

3. Before the lab

Your teacher will show you the apparatus: a mass hangs from a string that runs over a pulley and pulls the cart along the track. The force sensor rides on the cart, so it measures the pull of the string on the cart.

Uncertainties

The measurement you make by hand is the mass of the cart. Write it with the balance's uncertainty: for a digital balance, ± the smallest division (see Physics skills). For example, $m = (0.523 \pm 0.001)$ kg. Each trial gives a slightly different force and acceleration, and this spread is usually bigger than the sensors' resolution, so the uncertainty of each mean is half the range of your three trials.

4. Setting up

  1. Fix the pulley and its bracket to one end of the track. Level the track: a cart given a gentle push should roll at a steady speed in both directions, without speeding up or slowing down.
  2. Fix the force sensor to the cart so that its hook points along the track towards the pulley. Set its switch to ±10 N.
  3. Tie a string from the sensor's hook, over the pulley, to the mass hanger. The string between the cart and the pulley should be horizontal. Let the force sensor's cable hang loosely, so that it doesn't pull on the cart.
  4. Put the motion encoder at the other end of the track, with nothing in the way between it and the cart. (If you use a Motion Detector instead, keep the cart more than 0.15 m from it.)
  5. Plug the force sensor into CH 1, and connect the motion encoder as its instructions say. Open Logger Pro 3: you should see readings from both sensors at the bottom of the window. You need two graphs, force against time and velocity against time: if only one shows, choose Insert ▸ Graph, then click each vertical-axis label to choose what it shows.
  6. Weigh the cart with the force sensor on it (and anything else that will ride on it), and record its mass below.
  7. Unhook the string, so that nothing pulls on the sensor, and choose Experiment ▸ Zero. Then hook the string back on.

Measurements

5. Collecting data

  1. Put a small mass (for example 10 g) on the hanger. Pull the cart back so that the hanging mass can fall 40–50 cm before it reaches the floor.
  2. Click Collect, then let go of the cart. Catch it before it reaches the pulley.
  3. Force: on the force–time graph, drag across the part where the cart was speeding up smoothly (not the flat part before you let go). Choose Analyze ▸ Statistics and read the mean force.
  4. Acceleration: drag across the same time interval on the velocity–time graph, and choose Analyze ▸ Linear Fit. The gradient is the acceleration.
  5. Record both values in the table. Repeat twice more with the same hanging mass, so you have three trials.
  6. Increase the hanging mass and repeat, until you have at least five different hanging masses.

6. Your graph

The graph updates as you type. It starts with mean force on the vertical axis and mean acceleration on the horizontal axis, so the gradient is a mass.

The graph tool uses one uncertainty for every point on each axis. Type your largest ΔF in the vertical axis's uncertainty box and your largest Δa in the horizontal axis's box (a cautious choice), then tick Steepest and shallowest lines. The uncertainty in the gradient is half the difference between their gradients. Press Add this graph to my report to keep it.
