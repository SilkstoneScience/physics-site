Gravitational field lines and equipotentials + HL extra

The Earth's field lines point towards its centre and spread out with distance, so the field strength falls. Close to the surface the field is almost uniform. Between the Earth and the Moon the two fields add as vectors and cancel at one point. The last part, for HL, adds equipotentials and the potential gradient. D.1 Gravitational fields

What the animation shows

The values are those used in the D.1 notes: $G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{N m}^2\,\text{kg}^{-2}$, the Earth's mass $M_E = 5.97 \times 10^{24}$ kg and radius $R = 6.37 \times 10^6$ m, the Moon's mass $M_M = 7.35 \times 10^{22}$ kg, and the distance between their centres $d = 3.84 \times 10^8$ m. So $GM_E = 3.98 \times 10^{14}\ \text{N m}^2\,\text{kg}^{-1}$. Field lines are violet, with arrows showing which way a small test mass is pulled.

  1. The Earth pulls every mass towards its centre. The field lines show the direction of the force on a small test mass: always towards the centre.
  2. The field strength is the force per unit mass, $g = \dfrac{F}{m} = \dfrac{GM}{r^2}$, where $r$ is measured from the Earth's centre. At the surface, $g = \dfrac{3.98 \times 10^{14}}{(6.37 \times 10^6)^2} = 9.8\ \text{N kg}^{-1}$.
  3. A test mass moves out along a field line, and the graph beside it plots $g$ against $r$. At $2R$ the field is a quarter as strong, $9.8 \div 4 = 2.45\ \text{N kg}^{-1}$; at $3R$ it is a ninth, $9.8 \div 9 = 1.09\ \text{N kg}^{-1}$. The orange arrow's length is proportional to $g$, so it shrinks to a quarter and then a ninth.
  4. The lines spread out with distance: they are closer together where the field is stronger.
  5. Predict: what do the field lines look like over a small patch of the surface?
  6. The view zooms in to a patch about 320 km wide (a 100 km scale bar is shown). The lines are almost parallel and equally spaced: close to the surface, over a region that is small compared with the Earth's radius of 6370 km, the field is almost uniform: $g \approx 9.8\ \text{N kg}^{-1}$ throughout the patch. (At the top of the view, about 110 km up on a wide screen or 230 km up on a phone, it is 3% to 7% smaller.)
  7. The Earth and the Moon are shown with their sizes and the distance between them to scale: the Moon is about 60 Earth radii away.
  8. Field strength is a vector, so at each point the Earth's field and the Moon's field add. A test mass on the line between them shows both: the Earth's field points towards the Earth and the Moon's towards the Moon. The graph below plots the size of each field against the distance from the Earth's centre.
  9. Predict: where do the two fields cancel? Halfway to the Moon?
  10. Not halfway: 90% of the way, at the point P. The Moon has only $1/81$ of the Earth's mass, so you must be much closer to it for its field to be as strong. Setting $\dfrac{GM_E}{x^2} = \dfrac{GM_M}{(d - x)^2}$ gives $\dfrac{x}{d - x} = \sqrt{\dfrac{M_E}{M_M}} = \sqrt{81.2} = 9.01$, so $x = \dfrac{9.01}{10.01}\,d = 0.90d = 3.5 \times 10^8$ m from the Earth's centre. There, each field has a size of $\dfrac{3.98 \times 10^{14}}{(3.46 \times 10^8)^2} = 3.3 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{N kg}^{-1}$.
  11. The view zooms in on P and the Moon. The field lines of the resultant field are calculated point by point by adding the two fields as vectors. Near the Moon, its own field takes over and the lines curve in towards it; further away they head for the Earth. At P the resultant field is zero, so a test mass placed exactly there feels no resultant gravitational force.
  12. (HL) Equipotentials join points at the same gravitational potential, $V_g = -\dfrac{GM}{r}$. Around the Earth they are spheres (circles in the picture). At the surface, $V_g = -\dfrac{3.98 \times 10^{14}}{6.37 \times 10^6} = -6.25 \times 10^7\ \text{J kg}^{-1}$.
  13. (HL) They are drawn at equal steps of $1.25 \times 10^7\ \text{J kg}^{-1}$: $V_g = -5.00$, $-3.75$, $-2.50$ and $-1.25 \times 10^7\ \text{J kg}^{-1}$, at $r = \dfrac{GM}{|V_g|} = 1.25R$, $1.67R$, $2.5R$ and $5R$. They get further apart where the field is weaker. The graph of $V_g$ against $r$ shows why: equal steps up the curve need bigger and bigger steps along $r$ as the curve flattens.
  14. (HL) Field lines always cross equipotentials at right angles.
  15. (HL) Moving a mass along an equipotential needs no work: $\Delta V_g = 0$, so $W = m\Delta V_g = 0$.
  16. (HL) The field strength is the negative of the potential gradient, $g = -\dfrac{\Delta V_g}{\Delta r}$. From the surface to the first equipotential, $\Delta V_g = +1.25 \times 10^7\ \text{J kg}^{-1}$ over $\Delta r = 0.25R = 1.6 \times 10^6$ m, so $\dfrac{\Delta V_g}{\Delta r} = 7.8\ \text{N kg}^{-1}$ and $g = -7.8\ \text{N kg}^{-1}$ (taking outwards as positive). This is an average over the gap: $g$ falls from $9.8\ \text{N kg}^{-1}$ at the surface to $9.8 \div 1.25^2 = 6.3\ \text{N kg}^{-1}$ at $1.25R$. The minus sign shows that the field points towards lower potential: inwards.
  17. Summary: field lines show the direction of the force on a small test mass and are closer together where the field is stronger; $g = \dfrac{GM}{r^2}$; fields add as vectors. (HL) $V_g = -\dfrac{GM}{r}$, $g = -\dfrac{\Delta V_g}{\Delta r}$, equipotentials are perpendicular to field lines, and no work is done moving along one.

How it is drawn: each body is treated as a point mass at its centre, and nothing else (such as the Sun) is included. The field lines in the picture are a slice through the field, which really fills three-dimensional space. In a flat slice, the spreading of the lines shows that the field weakens with distance, but their spacing doesn't follow the inverse-square law exactly. Close to the Moon they are traced step by step along the direction of the resultant field, so their shapes are exact; they start from evenly spaced points along the edges of the view, so how closely they are packed there is not a measure of the field strength. In the first part, the arrow on the test mass has a length proportional to $g$. Between the Earth and the Moon, the arrows are drawn to scale with the field strength but are capped in length where the field is very strong. Only the Earth and the Moon are included: at P, the Sun's pull (about $6 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{N kg}^{-1}$) is actually larger than either, which is why we say the Earth's and Moon's fields cancel there.
