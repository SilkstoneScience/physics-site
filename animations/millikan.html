Millikan's oil-drop experiment

A charged oil drop falls between two horizontal plates until the p.d. across them is adjusted to hold it still. The electric force then balances its weight, which gives its charge. Measuring many drops shows that charge always comes in whole-number steps of the elementary charge. D.2 Electric charge and Millikan's experiment

What the animation shows

On the left is the apparatus, drawn from the side and not to scale. On the right is the view through the microscope, which is 2.6 mm high (the scale lines are 0.5 mm apart) and shows the drops moving at about their real speed. The drops scatter the light and appear in the microscope as bright points. The orange arrows are the weight $mg$ and the electric force $qE$ on the watched drop, drawn to the same scale; the grey-blue arrow is the air resistance while the drop moves. The plate separation is $d = 6.0$ mm and $g = 9.8\ \text{m s}^{-2}$.

  1. An atomizer sprays a fine mist of oil drops into a chamber above two horizontal metal plates, 6.0 mm apart. As the drops leave the nozzle, friction charges them: many gain a few extra electrons, and others lose some. Only negative drops can be held up when the top plate is positive.
  2. Some drops fall through a small hole in the top plate into the gap. A light shines across the gap, and one drop is watched through a microscope.
  3. With no p.d. across the plates, the drop falls slowly at a steady speed, its terminal speed: the air resistance balances its weight. Millikan found the mass of each drop from this terminal speed. This drop's mass is $m = 3.2 \times 10^{-15}$ kg.
  4. The top plate is made positive and the bottom one negative. The field between the plates points downwards, so the electric force on the negative drop points upwards. When the p.d. is made too big (here 500 V), the electric force is bigger than the weight and the drop rises, at about 0.07 mm each second.
  5. The p.d. is adjusted until the drop hangs still. Then the electric force balances the weight: $qE = mg$. (The air resistance is now zero, because the drop isn't moving.) The other drops in the view keep moving up or down: their charges and masses are different, so this p.d. doesn't balance them.
  6. The p.d. that holds this drop still is 294 V, so $E = \dfrac{V}{d} = \dfrac{294}{6.0 \times 10^{-3}} = 4.9 \times 10^4\ \text{V m}^{-1}$.
  7. $q = \dfrac{mg}{E} = \dfrac{3.2 \times 10^{-15} \times 9.8}{4.9 \times 10^4} = 6.4 \times 10^{-19}$ C. Dividing by the elementary charge, $\dfrac{6.4 \times 10^{-19}}{1.60 \times 10^{-19}} = 4$, so the drop has gained 4 extra electrons.
  8. Predict: Millikan measured many drops. Could a drop carry any amount of charge at all?
  9. A dot plot shows 40 measurements, one for each drop. They are simulated, not Millikan's own data: each is a whole number of electron charges, with a small random measurement error (about 2.5%). The first dot is the drop measured above.
  10. The charges bunch at $1.6, 3.2, 4.8, 6.4, \dots \times 10^{-19}$ C: whole-number multiples of one value. No drop has $2.4 \times 10^{-19}$ C, half-way between 1.6 and 3.2.
  11. Electric charge is quantised: every charge is $q = ne$, a whole number $n$ of elementary charges, $e = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}$ C. An electron has charge $-e$ and a proton $+e$, and every free charge ever measured is a whole number of $e$. (The dot plot shows the size of each drop's charge; the drops held up here are negative.)

How it is drawn: drops this small always move at their terminal speed, which is proportional to the resultant of their weight and the electric force, so the speed is calculated from the p.d. at each moment. The drop is tiny: with oil of density about 900 $\text{kg m}^{-3}$, a mass of $3.2 \times 10^{-15}$ kg means a drop about 2 µm across. Using Stokes' law, $F_d = 6\pi\eta r v$ (A.2 viscous drag), with the viscosity of air $\eta = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$ Pa s, it falls at about 0.1 mm each second with the plates switched off. Drops are drawn hugely enlarged.
