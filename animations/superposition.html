Superposition and music

Waves that meet simply add up. Watch two pulses pass through each other on a rope, two notes of nearly the same pitch make the loudness swell and fade, and the harmonics of a string add up to the sound of an instrument. C.3 Superposition C.4 Harmonics

Hear it

These buttons play the sounds in the animation, made by your browser. Start with a low volume, especially on headphones. Turn the animation's ♪ Music off first to hear the beats clearly. On an iPhone, turn off silent mode.

Two notes: 440 Hz, then 444 Hz. They sound almost the same. Beats: both together, so the loudness swells and fades 4 times a second. First harmonic only: a pure 220 Hz note. Add harmonics: the same note with its 2nd, 3rd and 4th harmonics added (amplitudes 1, 0.50, 0.33 and 0.25). A different mix: the same four harmonics with amplitudes 1, 0.15, 0.45 and 0.10. The pitch is the same; the sound is different.

What the animation shows

  1. Two pulses travel along a rope towards each other, one from each end. Thin coloured lines show each pulse on its own; the bright line is the rope itself.
  2. Where they overlap, the displacement of the rope at every point is the sum of the two displacements: the principle of superposition. When the two crests are exactly on top of each other, the rope is twice as high (constructive interference).
  3. Then each pulse carries on unchanged, as if the other had never been there.
  4. Predict: what happens when a crest meets an equal trough?
  5. For an instant the two cancel and the rope is completely flat (destructive interference). The energy hasn't gone: the rope is still moving at that instant (the small arrows), up on one side and down on the other, and both pulses reappear.
  6. Two continuous waves: notes of $f_1 = 440$ Hz and $f_2 = 444$ Hz, as a microphone would record them. They are drawn slowed down and with far fewer waves (20 and 21 across the picture, really 110 and 111 cycles in each quarter-second beat) so that you can see each one.
  7. Adding them point by point: where the two waves are in step, the sum is large; where they are out of step, it almost vanishes. So the loudness swells and fades. These are beats (beyond the IB syllabus). The number of beats each second is $f_2 - f_1 = 444 - 440 = 4$.
  8. A musician tuning a string listens to it with a reference note and adjusts the string until the beats slow down and stop. When both are exactly 440 Hz, the sum is one steady wave.
  9. A plucked string vibrates in its first harmonic and in higher harmonics at the same time. The animation shows the first four, at $f_n = n \times 220$ Hz: 220, 440, 660 and 880 Hz, with example amplitudes 1, 0.50, 0.33 and 0.25. They are drawn slowed down.
  10. The string's real shape is the sum of the four at every point. It keeps changing shape, but the whole pattern repeats 220 times a second, the frequency of the first harmonic.
  11. So the sound wave also repeats every $\frac{1}{220}$ s $= 4.5$ ms, and we hear the pitch of the first harmonic. Its shape is not a simple sine curve: the shape comes from the mix of harmonics. (The harmonics' starting points, their phases, are chosen to make a clear drawing. Changing the phases would change the drawn shape, but the ear hardly notices: what you hear depends mainly on the amplitudes of the harmonics.)
  12. Another instrument playing the same note has a different mix of harmonics (here 1, 0.15, 0.45 and 0.10), so its waveform has a different shape and it sounds different. This quality is called timbre (beyond the IB syllabus). Both waveforms repeat 220 times a second, so both play the same note.
