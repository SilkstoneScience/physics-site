Teacher notes: The flywheel at Ravensby

1. At a glance

The storyDuring an evening talk at a museum of Victorian engines, a priceless notebook disappears from a dark archive. The door sensor and the light alarm both seem to show that nobody went in before the night guard, and the great flywheel was still turning, so the theft looks only minutes old. Four suspects give statements. Students search a drawing of the scene, test the alibis with physics and write an accusation. (The thief is Adrian Kessler, an inventor. The flywheel had been coasting for about 25 minutes.)
PhysicsAll HL: B.4 Carnot efficiency; D.4 Faraday's law; E.2 photon energy and the work function; A.4 torque, $\tau = I\alpha$ and the equations for uniform angular acceleration. Students need all four topics taught first, so it suits the end of DP2, or revision before the exams.
Group size3–4 students, one device per group. It also works for one student alone, and on phones.
TimeA strong, focused group: 30–40 minutes. A typical group sharing one device: 50–60 minutes. Plan a full 60-minute lesson with a short debrief, or two shorter sessions.
ReadingAbout 1,900 words that students must read (story, statements, evidence and clue tasks), plus about 2,100 words of feedback, interviews and the ending: about 4,000 in all, roughly a fifth more than Fenwick Hall. That's a real load for EAL students (see making it easier).
DifficultyThe hardest case so far. Clues 1–3 are one- or two-step calculations; the flywheel (clue 4) needs four steps and a careful sign. The real challenge is seeing that two "alarms" and a still-turning wheel can all be honestly misleading.
Accounts and dataNone. Progress is saved only in the browser on that device; nothing is sent anywhere.

2. Before the lesson

  • Check each device. Open the case. If it opens part-way through (another class used that computer), press Start again. Progress is saved in that browser only.
  • Pre-teach a few words if your class needs it: alibi, suspect, archive, flywheel, clutch, circuit breaker, photocell, investor, brochure, auction, unaccounted for, motive, red herring.
  • Decide on hints. They're always available. Hints and "show me the working" lower the group's detective rank; you can also ban them for strong groups.
  • Printing (optional). The Print the case file button prints the story, the scene, statements, all the evidence and the four clue tasks, without answers. Paper can't mark answers, so use the answer key as you go round.
  • On the board: Show on the board makes the case fill the screen in large text, good for showing the scene and for the debrief.

3. A lesson plan (60 minutes)

  1. 0–5 min: the hook. Show the scene on the board and read the story aloud. Ask: "The door log, the alarm and the flywheel all point at the guard. Could all three be telling the truth and still mislead us?"
  2. 5–15 min: search the scene. Groups tap the numbered markers on the picture to open the evidence, then read the statements. Ask them to tap every sentence that makes a claim they could test with physics (this pins it to their notebook).
  3. 15–40 min: the clues. Work through them together, or run a jigsaw, with each student in a group of four taking one clue and reporting back. Give the flywheel (clue 4, the hardest) to your strongest student and the Carnot efficiency (clue 1, the easiest) to someone who needs a confident start. It works best if the flywheel comes last, as the numbering suggests: it moves the time of the theft.
  4. 40–50 min: the accusation. The Accuse tab opens once all four clues are solved. Each group chooses the thief, ticks the evidence that proves it, and writes 4–6 sentences. A wrong suspect gets an explanation and costs rank.
  5. 50–60 min: debrief. Show "What really happened" on the board. Groups mark an accusation with the 6-point checklist, and compare ranks. Finish with the discussion question.

Short of time? Set the reading (steps 1–2) as homework the night before, or let groups finish the accusation at home: their progress is saved on that device.

4. How the game works

  • The scene: at the top of the Case file tab, a drawing of the Engine Hall and the archive has six numbered markers. Each one opens a piece of evidence and turns green once visited. All 12 pieces of evidence are also listed below the picture, so nothing depends on tapping the markers.
  • Each clue has four steps: the evidence for that clue; a typed calculation, marked automatically; a multiple-choice "What does this prove?"; then a short detective's note, which they can compare with our model note.
  • Interviews: solving a clue unlocks one suspect's interview (three questions each). Several answers contain extra evidence, so encourage groups to read them.
  • The accusation is locked until all four clues are solved.
  • Detective rank: the group starts on 100 points. A hint costs 8, "show me the working" 15, each wrong typed answer 3 (at most 12 per clue), each wrong "What does this prove?" choice 5, and each wrong accusation 15. Chief Inspector 90+, Inspector 75+, Sergeant 55+, otherwise Constable.
  • Free help: the Σ Equations button shows the data-booklet equations for the clue's topic.

5. Where groups get stuck, and what to say

Questions to ask, rather than answers to give:

Sticking pointTry asking
Clue 1 (engine): putting the temperatures into $1 - T_c/T_h$ in degrees Celsius (giving 82%)."What temperature scale do the thermodynamics equations need? What would 0 °C give you?"
Clue 1: thinking 35% is fine because it is less than 100%."Which law does 'less than 100%' come from? What does the second law add?"
Clue 2 (door): forgetting the 500 turns, or turning 10 mV into 10 V."Is the emf induced in one turn or in all of them?" "How many volts is 10 mV?"
Clue 2: thinking that a slow opening gives a bigger emf."Faraday's law has a $\Delta t$ in it. Is it on the top or the bottom?"
Clue 3 (red light): stopping at $3.16 \times 10^{-19}$ J, or not converting nm to m."The work function is in eV. How do you turn joules into electronvolts?" "How many metres is 630 nm?"
Clue 3: believing that a brighter red light would set the alarm off."What does making the light brighter change: the number of photons or the energy of each one?"
Clue 4 (flywheel), the hardest: not seeing why the steady pull gives the frictional torque."At a steady speed, what is the resultant torque? So how does the pull's torque compare with friction's?"
Clue 4: using turns per minute, or turns per second, instead of $\text{rad s}^{-1}$ (giving 240 or 4.0 minutes)."What units does $\tau = I\alpha$ give for $\alpha$? So what units must $\omega$ be in?"
Clue 4: the sign of $\alpha$. Groups use a positive $\alpha$ and get a negative time, or quietly swap $\omega_i$ and $\omega_f$."Is the flywheel speeding up or slowing down? So what sign should $\alpha$ have in $\omega_f = \omega_i + \alpha t$?"
Clue 4: finding the time for the flywheel to stop (16.8 or 41.9 minutes)."Had it stopped at 22:04? Which two speeds do you know?"
The accusation: naming Kessler, but only for his motive or his fake engine."A motive isn't proof. Where was each suspect at 21:39, and how do you know?"

6. Making it easier or harder

More support

  • Read the story and statements aloud, and pre-teach the vocabulary above.
  • Run the jigsaw, so each student does one calculation in depth.
  • Give a blank timeline from 20:45 to 22:30 with a column for each suspect.
  • For clue 4, give the four steps as a scaffold: torque, angular acceleration, the two angular speeds, then the time.
  • Allow hints freely, and point everyone to the writing frame for the accusation.
  • Accept a spoken accusation instead of a written one.

More challenge

  • No hints or "show me the working": aim for Chief Inspector.
  • Ask groups to spot the warning signs before clue 4: Mr Kessler's "quarter of an hour" call lasted 2 minutes 10 seconds, and he says the theft happened just before the round, a time he can't have known.
  • Ask them to check the deceleration in the video itself: does the period change noticeably between the spoke's passes 15 s apart? (Hardly: the angular speed falls by about 1.5% per turn.)
  • Ask them to find the weak points. Is the frictional torque really the same at every speed? Would a slightly larger torque change who could have done it? What is the threshold wavelength of the photocell, and could another light in the building have set it off?
  • Extension: work out the flywheel's rotational kinetic energy at 10 turns per minute, and the average power of the friction while it coasts.

7. Literacy, oracy and the nature of science

  • Close reading: pinning claims makes students read the statements as evidence. At the end the game tells them how many of the tested claims they pinned.
  • Writing: the accusation is an extended "explain" answer, and the 6-point checklist works like a mark scheme.
  • Oracy: in the debrief, ask groups to argue for the defence. What would Mr Kessler's lawyer say about the bicycle light, and about the flywheel calculation's assumptions?
  • NoS (Evidence; Models): Ms Haldane honestly believed the alarms were proof, and Mr Achebe honestly believed the flywheel showed a recent theft. Ask: "A detector records nothing. Does that prove nothing happened? What would you need to know about the detector first?" The Carnot clue makes a good second example: a claim can be ruled out by a law without testing the machine at all.

8. Answer key

Show the answer key (contains the solution)

The thief: Adrian Kessler

His alibi is that the theft happened just before the guard's 22:00 round, when he was in the café. But the flywheel's huge moment of inertia and small frictional torque mean that it took about 25 minutes to slow to one turn every 15.0 s, so the East Wing power went off at about 21:39. Then only he was unaccounted for. The door sensor and photocell stayed silent because he opened the door slowly and worked by red light.

Why the others are innocent

  • Desmond Achebe: the door log seems to show that only he went in, but a slow opening isn't logged (clue 2). The reception camera shows him at the desk from 20:45 to 22:01, including 21:39, when the power went off (clue 4). He never left the building, and the police found neither the notebook nor the tool used on the lock. His white torch set off the alarm at once, as it should.
  • Ruth Haldane: she holds the cabinet key, but the cabinet was forced. The talk recording shows her at the projector desk throughout, then she was in the foyer.
  • Dr Mei-Lin Zhou: she opposed the sale, but the recording shows her asking a question from 21:38 to 21:43, then she was in the foyer. She wanted the notebook scanned and public.

Extra evidence in the interviews

  • Dr Zhou (after clue 1): before the talk Kessler asked whether she had photographed any pages yet; she wanted the notebook scanned, not hidden; Ms Haldane explains the alarms on staff tours.
  • Mr Achebe (after clue 2): he can't see the back corridor from the desk; only Kessler went out during the talk; he assumed a flywheel stops quickly.
  • Ms Haldane (after clue 3): Kessler asked her last month what light the photocell reacts to and whether a gentle opening would be noticed; only the lights and the motor are on the East Wing circuit; the cabinet key was in her pocket.
  • Mr Kessler (after clue 4): he can't account for 21:37 to 21:50 or for his bike light, and won't discuss his engine's efficiency.

The claims the clues test (for the pinning score)

  • Mr Kessler: "…it turns 35% of the heat it takes in into useful work."; "…a long phone call: a quarter of an hour at least."; "The flywheel was still turning when Desmond found it, so the theft happened just before his ten o'clock round…"
  • Mr Achebe: "…the great flywheel was still turning, so the power can only have gone off a minute or two before."
  • Ms Haldane: "That door sensor records every single opening…"; "…the photocell goes off at the slightest glimmer of light…"
  • Dr Zhou: none.

Evidence cards in the accusation

  • Key evidence: the power went off at about 21:39; only Kessler was unaccounted for then; the red light fits his bike and can't set off the photocell.
  • Supporting: a slow door opening isn't logged; his impossible 35% claim and the coming scans (motive); his "long" call lasted 2 minutes 10 seconds.
  • Red herrings: the door log showing no opening before Mr Achebe's; Ms Haldane's key; Dr Zhou's wish to keep the notebook in the country.

The accusation checklist (6 points)

  1. Names Adrian Kessler as the thief.
  2. Uses the frictional torque and moment of inertia to show that the power went off about 25 minutes before the video, at about 21:39.
  3. Uses the talk recording and the reception camera to show that only Mr Kessler was unaccounted for at that time.
  4. Explains why the door sensor and the photocell stayed silent: a slow change in flux gives a small emf, and red photons have less energy than the work function.
  5. Explains why at least one other suspect is cleared, with evidence.
  6. Gives a physics reason each time, not just "it doesn't add up".
