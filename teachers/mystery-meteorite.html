Teacher notes: The vanishing meteorite

How to run the physics mystery The vanishing meteorite with a class: timing, set-up, where groups get stuck and what to say, ways to make it easier or harder, and the full answer key.

1. At a glance

The storyA 4 kg iron meteorite is stolen from a museum's first-floor gallery at 20:14:00 on a stormy evening. Four suspects each give a statement. Students test the alibis with physics and write an accusation. (The thief is Victor Hale, the deputy director.)
PhysicsAll SL: A.1 free fall ($v^2 = u^2 + 2as$), B.1 specific heat capacity ($Q = mc\Delta T$) and power, B.5 current as rate of flow of charge ($I = \Delta q / \Delta t$), C.2 speed of sound. Students need all four topics taught first: usually the end of DP1 or early DP2.
Group size3–4 students, one device per group. It also works for one student alone, and on phones.
TimeA strong, focused group: 30–35 minutes. A typical group sharing one device: 45–55 minutes. Plan a full 60-minute lesson with a debrief, or two shorter sessions.
ReadingAbout 1,550 words that students must read (story, statements, evidence and clue tasks), plus about 1,500 words of feedback, interviews and the ending: about 3,000 in all. That's a real load for EAL students (see making it easier).
DifficultyEvery group can finish: hints, worked solutions and feedback on wrong answers mean nobody gets stuck for good. The challenge is the reading, keeping the timeline straight, and above all writing an accusation that links each piece of evidence to the physics.
Accounts and dataNone. Progress is saved only in the browser on that device; nothing is sent anywhere.

2. Before the lesson

  • Check each device. Open the case. If it opens part-way through (another class used that computer), press Start again. Progress is saved in that browser only, so a group that changes device starts from scratch, and the next class on a shared computer will find this group's progress unless someone presses Start again at the end.
  • Pre-teach a few words if your class needs it: alibi, suspect, statement, rating plate (on the kettle), window sill, frames per second, unaccounted for, motive, red herring.
  • Decide on hints. They're always available. Hints and "show me the working" lower the group's detective rank, which most classes find motivating; you can also ban them for strong groups.
  • Printing (optional). The Print the case file button prints the story, statements, all the evidence and the four clue tasks, without answers. Paper copies help groups spread the evidence out and argue, but paper can't mark answers: use the answer key as you go round.
  • On the board: Show on the board makes the case fill the screen in large text, good for reading the story aloud and for the debrief.

3. A lesson plan (60 minutes)

  1. 0–5 min: the hook. Show the case on the board and read the story aloud. Ask: "How could physics prove that someone is lying?"
  2. 5–15 min: read the case. Groups read the evidence (Case file tab) and the statements (Suspects tab). Ask them to tap every sentence that makes a claim they could test with physics (this pins it to their notebook).
  3. 15–40 min: the clues. Either work through them together, or run a jigsaw: each student in a group of four takes one clue on their own device (or paper), then reports back. The jigsaw fits the time much better. If you assign clues, give the falling key (clue 3, the hardest) to your strongest student and the thunder or phone (the easiest) to someone who needs a confident start. In a jigsaw, one device should then hold the group's game, where the reports are entered.
  4. 40–50 min: the accusation. The Accuse tab opens once all four clues are solved. Each group chooses the thief, ticks the evidence that proves it, and writes 4–6 sentences (the "Add the writing frame" button gives sentence starters). A wrong suspect gets an explanation and costs rank.
  5. 50–60 min: debrief. Show "What really happened" on the board. Groups mark their own or another group's accusation with the 6-point checklist, and compare ranks. Finish with the discussion question.

Short of time? Set the reading (steps 1–2) as homework the night before, or let groups finish the accusation at home: their progress is saved on that device.

4. How the game works

  • Five tabs: Case file (10 pieces of evidence), Suspects (statements, and interviews once unlocked), Clues, Notebook (their pinned claims, notes and interview answers) and Accuse.
  • Each clue has four steps: the evidence for that clue; a typed calculation, marked automatically (the unit is given; a common wrong answer gets specific feedback); a multiple-choice "What does this prove?"; then a short detective's note, which they can compare with our model note.
  • Interviews: solving a clue unlocks one suspect's interview (three questions each). Several answers contain extra evidence (see the key), so encourage groups to read them.
  • The accusation is locked until all four clues are solved, so a group that guesses the thief early still has to do the physics.
  • Detective rank: the group starts on 100 points. A hint costs 8, "show me the working" 15, each wrong typed answer 3 (at most 12 per clue), each wrong "What does this prove?" choice 5, and each wrong accusation 15. Chief Inspector 90+, Inspector 75+, Sergeant 55+, otherwise Constable. Unreadable answers (e.g. letters) cost nothing.
  • Free help: the Σ Equations button shows the data-booklet equations for the clue's topic, and each clue's topic tag opens the right section of the notes in a new tab.

5. Where groups get stuck, and what to say

Questions to ask, rather than answers to give:

Sticking pointTry asking
Clue 1 (thunder): using 7.7 s, the clock reading, instead of the 2.7 s gap."When did the light arrive? When did the sound arrive? How long did the sound take?"
Clue 2 (kettle): ΔT = 100 K; using kW as W; not knowing what to do with 1.5 litres."What temperature does the water start at?" "How many joules does a 3.0 kW kettle supply every second?"
Clue 2: "Kettles lose heat, so the calculation is useless.""Do losses make the kettle faster or slower? So is 189 s a longest or a shortest time?"
Clue 3 (key), the hardest: not seeing how to get a speed from the camera."How much time passes between two frames of a 50-frames-per-second video?" Then: "What do you know about an object falling from rest?"
Clue 3: "It might have been thrown, so we can't tell.""Does throwing it sideways change how fast it falls?" (The case says the gallery windows are sealed, and the task says "dropped, or thrown sideways".)
Clue 3 makes Lena look guilty (her workshop is a second-floor west room)."Where was Lena at 20:14:40? Who says so?" (Sam's statement, and hers.)
Clue 4 (phone): using 10 instead of 600 s; multiplying by 3.85 V."What is current? What units does charge have?"
Clue 4: "Charge gets used up in the torch.""Is the current smaller after the LED than before it? So what does the LED take from the charge?" (Energy, not charge.)
The accusation: naming Victor but saying only "his story doesn't add up"."Take each thing Victor claimed. Which clue breaks it, and what number proves it?"

6. Making it easier or harder

More support

  • Read the story and statements aloud, and pre-teach the vocabulary above.
  • Run the jigsaw, so each student does one calculation in depth.
  • Allow hints freely, and point everyone to the writing frame for the accusation.
  • Give out printed case files, so the evidence can be laid out on a table.
  • Accept a spoken accusation instead of a written one, recorded or presented to the class.

More challenge

  • No hints or "show me the working": aim for Chief Inspector.
  • Ask each group to write down who they suspect, and why, before solving any clue, then see whether the physics agrees.
  • Ask them to find the weak points in the evidence. Good ones: the speed from the camera is an average over the last frame, so is 6.7 m an under- or over-estimate? Could Sam and Lena be covering for each other? How much would air resistance on the key change the answer?
  • Before the reveal, ask each group to write its own "What really happened" timeline, from 20:13 to 20:25.
  • Extension: groups design a fifth clue from another topic (e.g. Victor's coffee cooling, or a reflection in a window) that would also expose him.

7. Literacy, oracy and the nature of science

  • Close reading: pinning claims makes students read the statements as evidence. At the end the game tells them how many of the 6 tested claims they pinned (listed in the key). Other reasonable pins aren't penalised.
  • Command terms: the clue tasks say "calculate"; the notes and accusation ask students to deduce and justify. See Exam technique.
  • Writing: the accusation is an extended "explain" answer. The 6-point checklist works like a mark scheme, so it's good for peer marking.
  • Oracy: in the debrief, ask groups to argue the case for the defence: what would Victor's lawyer say about each piece of evidence?
  • NoS (Evidence; Measurement): the witnesses' statements and the physical measurements disagree. Which should we trust, and why? Ask: "Is a calculation from a measurement more reliable than a person's memory? When might it not be?" (The clocks had to be checked; the camera's frame rate is assumed; the kettle gives only a shortest time.)

8. Answer key

Show the answer key (contains the solution)

The thief: Victor Hale

His alibi is that a power cut at 20:13 trapped him in the lift until 20:25 and his phone went flat, so he couldn't call for help. Both parts are broken by physics: the kettle shows the power was on after 20:14 (and the lift is on the same supply), and ten minutes of torch can't flatten a phone on 20 %. The key places the thief on the second floor, west side (his office or Lena's workshop), at 20:14:40, when Lena was with Sam reaching the gallery.

Common wrong answers with their own feedback: clue 1: 126, 0.0079, 2618; clue 2: 210 (ΔT = 100 K), 214 (1.7 L), 3.15 (minutes), 189 000 (W for kW); clue 3: 11.5 (the speed), 13.5 (no ½), 0.59 (not squared); clue 4: 4 (10 s), 924 (× 3.85 V gives energy), 2880 (20 % of the battery).

Why the others are innocent

  • Dr Navarro: clue 1 puts her camera, hand-held with her voice on the soundtrack, about 920 m away 5 s after the alarm. Her motive (the refused loan) is a red herring.
  • Sam Okafor: his kettle story fits the physics (clue 2), Lena saw him come out of the café just after the alarm, and at 20:14:40 he was with Lena reaching the gallery.
  • Lena Fischer: clue 3 makes her look guilty, but at 20:14:40 she was with Sam reaching the gallery, and (her interview) her workshop window is painted shut.

Extra evidence in the interviews

  • Dr Navarro (after clue 1): the hut's gate log; a second-floor light came on about 20 s after the strike.
  • Sam (after clue 2): the café lights never flickered; the lift hummed about a minute before the alarm; Victor walked in at 20:25 looking very calm.
  • Lena (after clue 3): her workshop window is painted shut; she heard the lift doors on the second floor about a minute before the alarm.
  • Victor (after clue 4): the phone is "at home, charging"; he says he got in the lift on the ground floor going to his office; he brushes off the dealer's letter.

The six claims the clues test (for the pinning score)

  • Dr Navarro: "…about 900 m from the museum" and "…my camera caught it, the flash and the thunder."
  • Sam: "…I filled the big kettle to the 1.5 litre line…" and "The alarm went off a few seconds before the kettle clicked off."
  • Victor: "At about 20:13 the power went off…" and "The phone was only on about 20 %…"

Evidence cards in the accusation

  • Key evidence: the kettle (power on after 20:14); the phone charge (under 2 %); the key's fall (second floor at 20:14:40); Sam and Lena together at 20:14:40.
  • Supporting: the dealer's letter (a motive, not proof); the thunder (clears Dr Navarro but doesn't point to the thief).
  • Red herring: the museum refusing Dr Navarro the loan.

The accusation checklist (6 points)

  1. Names Victor Hale as the thief.
  2. Uses the kettle to show the power was still on after 20:14.
  3. Uses the charge calculation to show the phone couldn't have gone flat.
  4. Uses the key's fall to place the thief on the second floor at 20:14:40.
  5. Explains why Lena is cleared, even though she works on the second floor.
  6. Gives a physics reason each time, not just "it doesn't add up".

Expect most groups to reach 3 or 4 of the 6 at first. The points most often missed are 5 (clearing Lena) and 6 (a physics reason each time).
