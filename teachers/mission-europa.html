Teacher notes: Mission to Europa

How to run the review game Mission log: Mission to Europa with a class: timing, set-up, how the gauges and endings work, where students get stuck and what to say, ways to make it easier or harder, and every answer.

1. At a glance

The storyStudents command the Kittiwake, the first crewed ship to Europa (2071). In four chapters they leave Earth, reach Jupiter, land on Europa, sample its ocean and dock for home. Twelve decisions each need a calculation and then an order. Mistakes cost fuel, power or shielding, but nobody is ever stranded and nobody dies.
PhysicsAll SL on the main path: A.1, A.2, A.3, B.1, B.5, C.2, D.1, D.2, D.3, E.3 and E.4 (the full list is in the key). Three optional HL extras use D.1 HL (orbital speed, escape speed) and E.3 HL (decay constant). Because it needs Themes D and E, it suits DP2 revision best.
RoutesTwo forks with no wrong answer (where to land; how to reach the ocean), so there are four routes. Decisions 7 and 10 differ by route; everything else is shared. Students who replay on another route meet new physics.
Group sizeBest alone or in pairs, one device each. It works on phones.
Time60–75 minutes for the whole mission; a quick student about 45. Each chapter takes 15–20 minutes. Progress is saved, so it can run over two lessons, or as lesson plus homework.
ReadingAbout 3,100 words on any one route (about 3,800 with both routes and every detour) that students must read (story, data, tasks and options), plus feedback and the ending. Each decision is short, but the total is a real load for EAL students (see making it easier).
Accounts and dataNone. Progress is saved only in the browser on that device; nothing is sent anywhere.

2. Before the lesson

  • Check each device. Open the mission. If it opens part-way through (another class used that computer), press Start again. Progress is saved in that browser only, so a student who changes device starts from scratch.
  • Check what's been taught. Chapter 1 needs A.2, B.1 and E.4; Chapter 2 needs C.2, D.1 and D.3; Chapter 3 needs A.1, A.2, A.3, B.5 and C.2; Chapter 4 needs A.1, A.2, B.1, D.2 and E.3. The game can't skip a chapter, so if Themes D and E aren't done yet, save it for later.
  • Pre-teach a few words if your class needs it: burn (of an engine), thrust, reactor, control rods, orbit, crevasse, plume, latch, background count.
  • Data booklet and calculators. Students need a calculator. The Σ Equations button shows each topic's data-booklet equations, and each decision's topic tag opens the right section of the notes.
  • On the board: Show on the board makes the game fill the screen in large text, good for playing the first decision together.

3. Lesson plans

Option A: two 60-minute lessons

  1. Lesson 1, 0–10 min: play Chapter 1's introduction and decision 1 together on the board. Ask everyone to commit to a burn time on mini-whiteboards before anyone types.
  2. 10–50 min: students play on through Chapters 1 and 2 at their own pace. Circulate with the sticking points. Fast finishers do the HL extra at decision 5, or start Chapter 3.
  3. 50–60 min: share captain's logs 1 and 2: read two aloud and compare them with the model. Which physics reason did each one give?
  4. Lesson 2, 0–5 min: quick recap: show of hands on gauges and on the landing site each student plans to choose.
  5. 5–45 min: Chapters 3 and 4.
  6. 45–60 min: debrief. Who reached which ending? Collect each student's "What to revise" list (a photo or a copy) as an exit ticket: it's a ready-made revision plan. Finish with the discussion question.

Option B: one lesson plus homework

Play Chapters 1 and 2 in class (with the board start above), set Chapters 3 and 4 for homework, and use the endings and "What to revise" lists to start the next lesson.

4. How the game works

  • A decision has three steps: read the log and the data; type the answer to one calculation (marked automatically: the unit is given, an answer inside the accepted range listed in the answer key is right (the range always includes the answer rounded to 2 significant figures; the three answers with no range are marked to within 2%), and common wrong answers get specific feedback); then choose an order. The order always follows from the calculation.
  • Mistakes: a wrong number gets one free retry. A second wrong number, pressing "show me the working", or a wrong order counts as a mistake and costs the decision's gauge: 10 points, or 15 for a decision with a detour. A detour is a short recovery calculation; solving it wins 5 points back. A hint costs 2 points.
  • Gauges: fuel, power and shielding start at 100 and never fall below 10, so nobody is ever stranded. The two forks cost 10 and 15 points of the gauge the student chooses.
  • Captain's logs: at the end of each chapter, students write two sentences explaining their decisions, compare them with a model, and tick up to 2 checklist points. It's self-marked and honest ticking matters, because the 8 log points count towards the best ending. (The model can't be seen until a student has written at least 12 words.)
  • HL extras: three optional tasks after decisions 5, 11 and 12. They have free hints and don't change the gauges, so SL students lose nothing by skipping them. The final report shows how many were solved.
  • Endings (the first one whose conditions are met):
    EndingNeeds
    The deep signalGauges total 250 or more (of 300), at most 1 decision went wrong, and 6 or more of the 8 log points
    Mission accomplishedGauges total 215 or more
    Limping homeGauges total 165 or more
    Mission abortedAnything lower (everyone still gets home safely)
    A perfect run ends with a total of 275 (the forks always cost 25). In a simulation, a student who gets about 85% of steps right reaches the best ending about 40% of the time; one who gets 55% right usually reaches "Mission accomplished" or "Limping home". Every ending gives a final report, a list of topics to revise (linked to the notes) and a "Fact or fiction?" note.

5. Where students get stuck, and what to say

Questions to ask, rather than answers to give:

Sticking pointTry asking
1, the burn: no idea how to get a force from "8.0 kg every second at 30 km s⁻¹"."How much momentum does the propellant carry away each second? What is force, in terms of momentum?"
2, sunlight: dividing by 5.2 rather than 5.2²."If you were twice as far from the Sun, over how much more area would the same light be spread?"
3, the reactor: leaving the energy in MeV, or forgetting that only 20% becomes electrical."Is the 30 kW the reactor's thermal power or the ship's electrical power?"
4, the message: giving the round trip, or the answer in seconds."What does the task ask for: there, or there and back? In what unit?"
5, the orbit: doubling the period because the radius doubles."What does Kepler's third law relate: $T$ and $r$, or $T^2$ and $r^3$?"
6, the shield: "the field bends the protons, so they'll miss.""How deep does a proton get before it turns round? Is that more or less than 0.50 m?"
7A, braking: leaving out the weight."Draw the free-body diagram while the engine is braking. What two forces act?"
7B, the crevasse: "the rover is lighter, so it falls more slowly", or using Earth's $g$."Does mass appear anywhere in the equations of motion for free fall? Where are we?"
8, the echo: forgetting to halve."How far does the sound travel between the charge and the detector?"
9, the heaters: ignoring the internal resistance; "series has more resistance, so more heat"."What happens to the current when the total resistance goes up? Which matters here, $I$ or $R$?"
10A, the melt probe: forgetting either the warming or the latent heat."What two things must happen to ice at 150 K before it's water?"
10B, the plume: "halfway up means half the speed.""Does the speed fall evenly with height, or with time?"
11, the sample: forgetting the background."How much of the 1800 counts per minute comes from the sample itself?"
12, docking: assuming the lander stops completely."Is the momentum conserved? Does the ship move at all after the latches lock?"
Captain's logs: "the panels weren't enough" with no physics."Why weren't they? Which law, which number?"

6. Making it easier or harder

More support

  • Play in pairs, with one reader and one calculator, swapping each chapter.
  • Play the first decision together on the board, modelling how to read the data list and how to set out the working.
  • Allow hints freely: they cost only 2 points. Encourage "show me the working" after a real attempt, then talk it through.
  • Pre-teach the vocabulary above, and read each chapter's introduction aloud.
  • Accept spoken captain's logs, said to a partner, instead of written ones.

More challenge

  • No hints or "show me the working": aim for "The deep signal".
  • HL students do all three HL extras; SL students can try them too, after a quick introduction to the equation.
  • Replay on the other route (different decisions 7 and 10) and compare which route is harder.
  • Find the simplifications: what has the game ignored? Good ones: the lander's changing mass while braking; air resistance in Europa's thin atmosphere; heat lost by the melt probe; meltwater refreezing; the size of the proton storm. Which would change an order?
  • Extension: design a thirteenth decision, with a calculation, an order, three tempting wrong orders based on real misconceptions, and a detour.

7. Literacy, oracy and the nature of science

  • Command terms: every task says "calculate"; the captain's logs ask students to explain, linking the situation to the law and giving a number, which is exactly what examiners want in an "explain" answer. See Exam technique.
  • Writing: the four captain's logs are short extended-response answers with a 2-point checklist each, good for peer marking.
  • Oracy: the forks are good for an opinion line. "Plains or chaos terrain? Melt probe or plume?" Students stand on a line, then justify their position with physics and the cost to the gauges.
  • NoS (Evidence; Science as a shared endeavour): the best ending finds chemistry that could come from life but proves nothing, and Amara says so. Ask: "What would count as convincing evidence of life on Europa? Who should check it?"
  • NoS (Models): every ending has a "Fact or fiction?" note. Ask which simplifications made the physics possible, and which would matter for a real mission.

The HL extras (optional)

  • After 5 (D.1 HL): orbital speed at twice Europa's orbital radius: $\sqrt{GM/r}$ with $r = 1.342 \times 10^9$ m gives 9720 m s⁻¹ (9600–9800), which agrees with $2\pi r/T$.
  • After 11 (E.3 HL): $\lambda = \ln 2 / 15 = 0.0462$ h⁻¹; count rate from the sample after 40 h: $1600 e^{-0.0462 \times 40} = 252$ counts per minute (248–256).
  • After 12 (D.1 HL): escape speed from Europa: $\sqrt{2GM/r} = 2030$ m s⁻¹ (2000–2050).

The captain's log checklists

  1. Chapter 1: uses the inverse-square law to explain why the panels aren't enough; explains that control rods keep the reaction steady by absorbing neutrons.
  2. Chapter 2: the reply takes 105 min (radio travels at $c$), more than the 90 available; a stronger field gives a smaller radius ($r = mv/qB$), so protons turn round inside the 0.50 m layer.
  3. Chapter 3: thickness = speed × time ÷ 2 for an echo; parallel heaters have less total resistance, so more current and more power in the heaters.
  4. Chapter 4: subtract the background, then four half-lives (1600 → 100) is 60 h; conservation of momentum and force = change of momentum ÷ contact time explain the slower approach.

Fact or fiction

Real: Europa's mass, radius and orbit, its likely salty ocean, chaos terrain, Jupiter's radiation belts, the radio delay, sodium-24's half-life (about 15 h), and the Europa Clipper and JUICE missions. Invented or simplified: the crewed ship and its fusion drive; ice only 300 m thick (estimates range from a few km to about 30 km or more); the plumes (tentative, debated evidence); the uniform shield field; the activated sample; and any sign of life.
