Organisation Release: Ghostly Cat’s Head Nebula lurks within sight of Hubble and Webb (opens ESA/Hubble’s article in a new tab)

Hubble and Webb have imaged a dusty pillar nicknamed the Cat's Head Nebula inside the Carina Nebula, about 7,500 light-years from Earth. In visible light the dust looks opaque, but Webb's infrared images see through much of it, exposing embedded young stars and warm dust. Ultraviolet radiation from nearby massive stars ionises and heats the pillar's edges, slowly evaporating gas and shaping where new stars can form.

New converter could bridge light wavelength gap in quantum communications (opens Phys.org’s article in a new tab)

Researchers have built a new converter that could help overcome a mismatch in light wavelengths within quantum networks. These networks link stationary qubits, such as quantum information stored in trapped ions, by sending single photons along optical fibres. Matching wavelengths between the parts of the network could make secure quantum links more practical. It also shows the photon model of light, where each particle carries energy set by its wavelength, being used in real technology.

From spin wave to superconducting circuit: A one-way street for quantum information (opens Phys.org’s article in a new tab)

Scientists at Argonne National Laboratory and the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign have shown a way for information to travel in only one direction, from a spin wave in a magnetic material to a superconducting circuit. Spin is a quantum property of particles that can store and process information. Controlling the direction of this signal flow could help magnetic materials become useful parts of future quantum computers, sensors and secure communication systems.

Physics

Optical skyrmions can control the motion of magnetic skyrmions (opens Phys.org’s article in a new tab)

Researchers report that patterns of light called optical skyrmions can be used to steer magnetic skyrmions, which are tiny swirls of magnetisation inside some magnetic materials that act much like particles. Because these swirls are very small and need little energy to move, they could carry information in low-power memory and logic devices. Precise control of their motion has been a major goal, so this approach links light and magnetic fields in a potentially useful way.

Energy

Scientists may have found a shortcut to fusion energy (opens ScienceDaily’s article in a new tab)

Researchers at Princeton Plasma Physics Laboratory have calculated that a fusion reactor might reach ignition more easily if its plasma is heated first and only then made denser, rather than the usual order. This reversed approach could let future reactors become self-sustaining while using much less energy and avoiding problems that make ignition harder. Fusion requires high temperature and density so that nuclei can overcome electrostatic repulsion and fuse.

Webb measures distance to farthest fast radio burst, suggesting origin (opens ESA/Webb’s article in a new tab)

Using the Webb telescope, astronomers located the host galaxy of FRB 20240304B, the most distant fast radio burst yet found, and measured its redshift as 2.148. The galaxy turned out to be a small, young dwarf that formed most of its stars quickly. This favours the idea that these brief radio flashes come from magnetars rather than from slow neutron star mergers, and the burst's signal also carried imprints of matter it passed through.

Astronomy

Webb captures commotion from nebula’s stellar jets (opens ESA/Webb’s article in a new tab)

The James Webb Space Telescope has imaged NGC 7129, a star-forming nebula about 3,300 light-years away, using infrared light that passes through dust which blocks visible light. Its brightest star, a pre-main-sequence star of 5 to 8 solar masses, is shrinking under gravity and heating up before hydrogen fusion begins. Outflows and radiation from young stars have carved a cavity spanning about 3.5 light-years, compressing gas that may form new stars.

Space

Solar Orbiter tracks origin of mysterious magnetic switchbacks (opens ESA’s article in a new tab)

ESA's Solar Orbiter passed through a large S-shaped fold in the solar wind's magnetic field and measured the charged oxygen and carbon particles inside it. Their composition showed the plasma came from hot magnetic loops on the Sun, released when open and closed field lines reconnected. Waves and turbulence then appear to shape the fold as it travels outward, helping scientists understand how charged particles follow magnetic field lines and improving space weather forecasts.
