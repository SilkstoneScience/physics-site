Extended essay in physics

The extended essay (EE) is a 4,000-word academic essay that answers a focused research question using physics, with evidence you have gathered and analysed yourself. It is the longest piece of work in the Diploma, and the only one you design yourself. This page helps you decide whether physics is the right subject for your EE, and then takes you through the essay from first idea to final submission. It is for essays first assessed in 2027.

3. What makes it a physics essay

A physics EE has four parts. Take any one of them away and the essay drifts towards engineering, technology or general science writing, and your knowledge mark (Criterion B) suffers.

  1. A model. You state a physical theory and the assumptions it rests on.
  2. A prediction. The model gives you a number before you measure anything.
  3. A measurement. You test the prediction, with uncertainties propagated properly.
  4. A judgement. You decide whether prediction and measurement agree, by comparing the difference with the uncertainty, not by eye.

If you can't write down an equation that predicts your dependent variable, you don't have a physics EE yet. Step 4 is where the best essays stand out: most students report their numbers and never judge whether they agree.

It is an essay, not a long lab report

This is the most common structural mistake. Students bring their IA and lab-report habits with them, and the essay reads like a worksheet instead of an argument.

A lab reportYour extended essay
An aim, then numbered method stepsAn introduction that sets up a question
Equipment list in the bodyMethod described in continuous prose; equipment lists go in an appendix
Results reported, then discussedAnalysis and discussion together, building an argument
An evaluation section after the conclusionEvaluation woven into the discussion; the conclusion always comes last
Written for your teacherWritten for a physicist who has never met you

It is also not an IA with more words. The EE rewards a sustained line of argument, use of published literature and critical evaluation. An essay that reads like a 4,000-word IA usually ends up around a grade C.

And it does not need the "right" answer. Results that contradict your prediction are just as valuable as results that confirm it, provided you analyse them honestly. You lose marks for claiming agreement you haven't shown, or for explaining away a difference without investigating it.

4. Should you choose physics?

Students who do well in a physics EE usually have these four things:

QualityWhat it looks like
Genuine curiosityYou have a question you actually want answered. You will live with it for a year, and interest is what keeps you going. It is also the one thing on this list you can't learn in a term.
Analytical strengthYou are comfortable rearranging equations, propagating uncertainties and reading a graph critically.
Practical patienceApparatus fails and data is noisy. You are willing to repeat, troubleshoot and run a pilot before committing.
Research skillYou can find and read sources beyond revision websites, and build on what others have published.

Independence: the requirement nobody mentions

The essay is yours. As your supervisor, I guide you; I don't carry you.

Ask questions that make you thinkProofread or correct your spelling

The students who thrive are the ones who turn up to a meeting having already tried something, even if it failed. If you need to be chased to make progress, a physics EE will be hard work.

Choosing physics just for the points? Think again. Vague analysis shows up immediately in physics, in a way it doesn't in an essay-based subject. The marks are in the uncertainty treatment and the evaluation, which take the longest and can't be bluffed. And a topic you find dull becomes unbearable by month eight. Choose the subject where your question lives: the grade follows the interest.

Four questions to ask yourself

  1. Is there a physics question I genuinely want answered?
  2. Can I write an equation that predicts what I would measure?
  3. Am I willing to work on my own for a year without being chased?
  4. Am I choosing physics, or just avoiding another subject?

Come and talk to me before you decide, and bring a question, not just a subject.

5. Your timeline

The times below are approximate; the school sets the actual deadlines. The three reflection sessions are compulsory and are recorded on your Reflection and Progress Form (RPF).

1. Exploration (1–2 months)

Read widely, narrow down an area, and test possible questions for feasibility (see Choosing a topic).

2. Reflection session 1

Working RQ needed A refined proposal: you need a working research question and some grounding in the physics behind it.

3. Pilot work (2–4 weeks)

Try the real apparatus. Find out what actually works before you commit. This is the stage students skip, and the one that saves them.

4. Research (2–3 months)

Collect and process your data, and keep reading.

5. Reflection session 2

An interim check: your progress, the obstacles, and whether your research question still holds.

6. Writing (4–6 weeks)

Write a full draft.

7. Draft feedback, then revision (2–3 weeks)

One draft only I comment on one complete draft. I can't proofread or edit it. You act on the comments yourself.

8. Reflection session 3: the viva voce

A short final conversation about your essay and what you learned, then you complete your RPF (see Reflection and the RPF).

Build in pilot time. More physics EEs are damaged by finding out, at the writing stage, that the apparatus wasn't good enough than by anything else. Two hours of trial work early in the year prevents a crisis later.

6. Choosing a topic

Start from a mechanism, not a phenomenon

"Bridges" is a phenomenon. "How the resonant frequency of a beam depends on its length" is a mechanism. Topics fail when you can describe what happens but have no model that predicts by how much. Ask yourself: can I write down an equation that predicts my dependent variable? If not, you can't analyse quantitatively, and Criteria C and D become very hard to reach.

Good ideas often come from the course itself: a lab that you wanted to take further, a demonstration that made you ask "what if?", or a topic where you were left with a nagging question. Everyday physics (music, sport, medical technology) works too, as long as the physics stays at the centre.

Be honest about your apparatus

You have a school lab, a phone camera and a few months. That is genuinely enough: excellent essays have been written with a metre rule and a stopwatch. Phone sensors and video analysis open up a lot more. What doesn't work is a topic that needs equipment you don't have, or more precision than your instruments can give.

Do the arithmetic in your first fortnight. Estimate how big your effect will be, and compare it with your measurement uncertainty. If you predict a 2% change and your timing is only good to 5%, the essay is over before it starts.

Kinds of physics EE

KindStrengthWhat to watch
Experimental (primary data)You control the variables and the method, which supports Criterion A.Equipment limitations. Pilot early.
Secondary data (published datasets, archives, astronomical or satellite catalogues)Precision and scale you couldn't reach in a school lab.The processing, fitting, uncertainties and interpretation must all be yours. State which datasets you chose, why, and what you left out.
Simulation or numerical modelLets you explore systems you can't build.It needs clear physics principles and variables, and an anchor: experimental data, observations or an analytical result to compare with.

Databases and simulations each have their own rules: see Databases and simulations.

Topics that tend to work

  • Testing a standard model outside its comfort zone: where does the simple theory start to fail, and why?
  • Isolating one variable that textbook comparisons mix up with others (see the example below).
  • Comparing two competing models against the same data.
  • Analysing published or archival data yourself.

Topics that tend to struggle

  • "Which material is best for X?" This is usually materials testing with no physical model.
  • Anything that needs a wind tunnel, a vacuum chamber or a particle source you don't have.
  • Simulations with no experimental, observational or analytical comparison.
  • Measuring a single known constant you could look up.
  • Broad surveys ("the physics of black holes") with no research question inside them.
  • Theory far beyond the course, such as quantum computing.

Worked example: narrowing a topic

Interest: rolling objects, and why some reach the bottom of a slope faster than others.

Too broad: "The physics of rolling motion."

Still too broad: "How does an object's shape affect how fast it rolls down a slope?"

Better, but confounded: "How does the moment of inertia affect the energy distribution of a rolling object?" Comparing a hoop, a solid cylinder and a sphere changes the mass, radius, surface and material all at once, so any effect can't be put down to the moment of inertia alone.

Good: "To what extent does the moment of inertia, varied by the radial position of attached point masses, affect the partition of kinetic energy in a cylinder rolling without slipping down an inclined plane?"

The final version uses one object, with its mass, radius, surface and drop height all held constant, and changes only how the mass is distributed. That isolation is a real improvement on the textbook comparison, and the introduction should say so.

7. The research question

Everything rests on the question. Your research question (RQ) must be:

  • a question, ending in a question mark;
  • focused: one question, not two joined by "and";
  • specific: a reader can tell exactly what is varied and what is measured;
  • analytical, not descriptive: the answer isn't obvious;
  • answerable in 4,000 words with the resources you have.

Question stems

Use stems that demand a judgement: "To what extent…", "How does … depend on …", "How accurately does [model] predict …", "Under what conditions does … break down?"

Avoid stems that invite description: "What is…", "How does … work?", "Why is…"

Test your question

TestAsk yourself
SpecificCan a reader name my independent and dependent variables from the RQ alone?
RangeDoes the RQ state, or clearly imply, the range I will cover?
ModelIs there an equation that predicts the answer before I measure it?
FeasibleCan my equipment resolve the effect I expect?
DepthDoes answering it need analysis, or just reporting numbers?

Weak and strong questions

  • Weak: "How does mass affect the period of a pendulum?" Theory says it doesn't, so there is nothing to analyse. If you like pendulums, ask about amplitude instead, where the small-angle approximation fails.
  • Weak: "What is the acceleration due to gravity?" You are measuring a known constant, so there is no argument to make.
  • Strong: "To what extent does the small-angle approximation limit the accuracy of the simple pendulum period equation for amplitudes between 5° and 60°?"
  • Strong: "How does the terminal velocity of a sphere falling through glycerol depend on its radius, and to what extent does the measured relationship agree with Stokes' law over the range of Reynolds numbers reachable in a school laboratory?"

Notice the pattern: both strong questions name the variables, give the range, and name a specific model that is tested, not just used.

Word for word, three times. Your RQ must be identical on the title page, in the introduction and in the conclusion. Examiners check. It is one of the easiest marks to lose, and one of the easiest to keep.

8. Designing the investigation

Variables

  • Independent variable: what you physically set. If you move masses to five positions, the independent variable is the position; the moment of inertia is calculated from it. Getting this the wrong way round is common and looks careless.
  • Dependent variable: what you measure.
  • Controlled variables: for each one, say why it must be controlled and how you controlled it.

Aim for at least five values of the independent variable, over as wide a range as your apparatus allows, with at least five repeats of each. With fewer than five points it is very hard to claim a trend.

Make the method replicable

The test: could a competent physics student in another school repeat your experiment from your description alone? Write it in prose, not as numbered recipe steps, and leave out exhaustive equipment lists.

Be most precise at the point that matters most. If the whole essay depends on how you repositioned the masses, say exactly how they were attached, how many there were, how you kept the arrangement symmetric and how you measured their positions. Vagueness at the central manipulation is a serious weakness, and a very common one.

Include a labelled diagram of the apparatus, and make sure every value on it matches your data tables. If the diagram says 10° and the table says 15°, the examiner won't know which to believe, and will trust neither.

Check that your values are consistent

If you record a ramp's angle $\theta$, its length $L$ and its height $h$, they must satisfy $\sin\theta = \dfrac{h}{L}$. If they don't, one of them is wrong. Check before you leave the lab, not after you have written the analysis.

Safety and ethics

Include a short safety paragraph where it is relevant: lasers, high voltages, radioactive sources and projectiles all need explicit risk control. If your investigation involves people (reaction times, sport, the body), you need their informed consent; follow the IB's ethical guidelines for experiments and ask me first.

9. Databases and simulations

You don't have to collect every number yourself. A physics EE can be built on published data or on a simulation you write, or on either of these alongside your own measurements. Both are allowed and both can score well, but each has its own rules. In both, the examiner is asking: which part of this is your work?

Essays using databases (secondary data)

Research institutions publish enormous datasets, far more precise than anything a school lab can produce. You can download them and do the physics yourself. Some places to start:

SourceWhat it holdsA model you could test
NASA Exoplanet ArchiveOrbital periods, orbit sizes and masses of thousands of planets around other starsKepler's third law: find a star's mass from its planets' orbits
JPL HorizonsPositions and velocities of planets, moons, comets and spacecraft at any timeThe inverse-square law of gravitation; conservation of energy and angular momentum in an orbit
Gaia archive (ESA)Distances, brightnesses and colours of over a billion starsLuminosity and the Stefan–Boltzmann law; the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram
NIST Atomic Spectra DatabaseWavelengths of spectral lines for every elementEnergy levels; the Rydberg formula and where it stops working
CERN Open DataReal particle-collision events, including datasets prepared for studentsConservation of energy and momentum; the invariant mass of a particle
Gravitational Wave Open Science CenterSignals from black-hole and neutron-star mergersHow the frequency of the signal changes as two objects spiral together

Phone sensors and video recordings that other people have published count as databases too, as long as you do the analysis.

What a database essay must show:

  • Your own analysis. You may reproduce a published graph to compare with, but the processing, fitting, uncertainties and interpretation must be yours. Downloading a result and discussing it is not an investigation.
  • Selection criteria, stated openly. Which data you used, why, and what you left out and why. If you picked the points that fit best, the argument collapses. Decide your rule before you look at how well the points fit, and say what it was.
  • The database's own uncertainties. Most datasets give an uncertainty with each value. Use them in your error bars and propagation; don't invent new ones or ignore them.
  • Where the data came from. Say briefly how the values were measured (a space telescope, a spectrometer, a detector), because that tells the reader what limits them. Cite the database, with the date you downloaded the data.
  • Careful units and conventions. Databases use astronomical units, days, electronvolts and solar masses. Convert them carefully, and say how you did it.

Worked example: from a database to a research question

Idea: the seven planets of the TRAPPIST-1 system all orbit the same star, so Kepler's third law says they should all lie on one line.

Model: for circular orbits, $T^2 = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{GM}\,a^3$, where $T$ is the orbital period, $a$ the orbit's radius and $M$ the star's mass.

Analysis: plot $T^2$ against $a^3$ using the archive's values and their uncertainties. The gradient is $\dfrac{4\pi^2}{GM}$, so it gives the star's mass. Compare that with the star's mass that astronomers found by a different method, from the star's brightness.

The check that matters: find out how the archive got each orbit radius $a$. Some values are calculated from the period and the star's mass using Kepler's law itself. Testing Kepler's law with those would be circular. Use only values measured independently (for example, from the shape of the dip in starlight as the planet crosses the star), and say in the essay that you checked.

Research question: "To what extent does Kepler's third law, applied to the orbits of the seven TRAPPIST-1 planets, give a stellar mass consistent with the published mass found from the star's brightness?"

The data are published, but the choice of data, the linearisation, the fit, the uncertainty in the gradient and the comparison are all the student's own. Two independent routes to the same quantity give the essay something to argue about.

Essays using simulations

There are two very different things people mean by a simulation:

  • Using someone else's simulation (a ready-made app or website). This is usually weak: the simulation already contains the model, so its "data" can only give you back the equations someone else put in. It can't test anything. Use these for exploring an idea, not as the evidence in your essay.
  • Building your own numerical model. You write the equations, turn them into a step-by-step calculation in a spreadsheet or a short program, and use it to make predictions that the simple textbook formula can't. This can be an excellent EE.

A numerical model is most useful where the equations can't be solved with algebra alone: air resistance that depends on $v^2$, a pendulum at large amplitudes, a rocket losing mass, alpha particles scattering off a nucleus, a planet pulled by two stars.

Worked example: a falling ball with air resistance

A ball falls from rest, with drag proportional to $v^2$. Its acceleration is

$a = g - \frac{k}{m}v^2$

Take $g = 9.8\ \text{m s}^{-2}$ and $k/m = 0.020\ \text{m}^{-1}$. The acceleration changes as the speed changes, so the equations of motion for constant acceleration don't apply.

Instead, split the motion into short time steps $\Delta t$ and assume the acceleration is constant during each step:

$$v_{n+1} = v_n + a_n\,\Delta t$$

With $\Delta t = 0.1$ s:

Step$t$ / s$v$ / $\text{m s}^{-1}$$a$ / $\text{m s}^{-2}$
00.00.0009.800
10.10.9809.781
20.21.9589.723
30.32.9309.628

A spreadsheet repeats this for thousands of rows in a second. The speed should level off at the terminal velocity, where the acceleration is zero: $v_\text{t} = \sqrt{\dfrac{g}{k/m}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{9.8}{0.020}} = 22\ \text{m s}^{-1}$.

What a simulation essay must show:

  • The physics. Derive the equations your model uses and state every assumption (no wind, constant drag coefficient, a point mass…).
  • That the model works. Test it where you already know the answer. Set the drag to zero: does it give $v = gt$ exactly? Does the speed level off at the terminal velocity you can calculate by hand? Is energy conserved when it should be?
  • That the time step is small enough. Halve $\Delta t$ and run it again. If the result barely changes, the step was small enough; if it changes a lot, it wasn't. The size of that change is part of your model's uncertainty.
  • An anchor in reality. Compare the model with something real: your own measurements (a video of a falling ball analysed frame by frame in a free tool such as Tracker), published data, or an analytical result. A model that is never compared with anything is just arithmetic. The best simulation essays use the model to make a prediction and then test it.
  • Sensitivity. How much does the prediction change if a parameter (the drag constant, the mass) is 5% out? That tells you which measurements matter most.

Tools: a spreadsheet is enough for most essays. Python (free to use in a browser with Google Colab) or GlowScript VPython are good if you want to go further. Describe the method in prose in the essay and put the code or a sample of the spreadsheet in an appendix. Write the code yourself; if any tool, including AI, helped with it, acknowledge it as you would any other source.

The common trap in both kinds of essay is circularity. If your data come from a model, or you choose the data that fit a model, you can't use them to test that same model. Ask: could my results have come out differently from my prediction? If not, there is no test, and so no argument.

10. Measurement and uncertainty

This is where physics EEs are won and lost. Examiners look for uncertainties handled properly, and most candidates handle them badly. The Physics skills page has the basics; this section is about how to use them in an essay.

Measure the quantity your equation needs

Before you use a measured quantity, ask: is this the quantity in my equation? Average and instantaneous, peak and RMS, apparent and true: these mix-ups are silent, and they can wreck an otherwise excellent essay.

Worked example: average or final velocity?

A cylinder rolls 0.54 m from rest in 0.65 s. A student calculates $v = \dfrac{d}{t} = 0.83\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ and uses it as the speed at the bottom of the ramp.

That is the average velocity. For uniform acceleration from rest, the average velocity is half the final velocity, so the final velocity is $v = \dfrac{2d}{t} = 1.66\ \text{m s}^{-1}$.

Kinetic energy depends on $v^2$, so every energy value is out by a factor of four. The student concluded that 70% of the energy was lost to friction. In fact the analysis was wrong.

If you need an instantaneous velocity, measure a displacement over a short interval at that point, or fit a curve to the position–time data and find its gradient there.

Check against conservation laws

Any experiment involving energy, momentum or charge gives you a free consistency check: work out the total before and after. No rolling object loses 70% of its energy over half a metre. A difference of more than about 20% is almost always a mistake in the analysis, not a physical effect. Investigate it before you write the discussion.

Uncertainty in each measurement

MeasurementUncertainty to use
Analogue scaleHalf the smallest division for each reading. A length between two marks needs two readings, so double it.
Digital displayThe resolution of the last digit, unless the manufacturer gives a better figure.
Hand timingYour reaction time dominates: typically ±0.1 s to ±0.2 s, not the stopwatch's ±0.01 s.
Video analysisAt least one frame period: at 240 frames per second, ±0.004 s.
Repeated readingsUse the spread of your data if it is bigger than the instrument's resolution: half the range, $\dfrac{\text{largest} - \text{smallest}}{2}$.

For example, five trials of 0.63 s, 0.63 s, 0.65 s, 0.67 s and 0.66 s have a half-range of 0.02 s, twice the stopwatch resolution. Quoting ±0.01 s would understate your uncertainty and misrepresent your data.

Propagating uncertainties

Adding or subtracting: add the absolute uncertainties. If $z = x \pm y$, then $\Delta z = \Delta x + \Delta y$.

Multiplying, dividing and powers: add the fractional uncertainties, multiplied by the powers. If $z = x^a y^b$, then

$$\frac{\Delta z}{z} = |a|\frac{\Delta x}{x} + |b|\frac{\Delta y}{y}$$

Worked example: uncertainty in kinetic energy

$m = (0.274 \pm 0.001)$ kg and $v = (1.67 \pm 0.03)\ \text{m s}^{-1}$. Find $E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ with its uncertainty.

$E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.274 \times 1.67^2 = 0.382$ J.

$$\frac{\Delta E_\text{k}}{E_\text{k}} = \frac{\Delta m}{m} + 2\frac{\Delta v}{v}$$

$= 0.0036 + 2 \times 0.018 = 0.040$, so $\Delta E_\text{k} = 0.040 \times 0.382 = 0.015$ J, and $E_\text{k} = (0.382 \pm 0.015)$ J.

The velocity term is doubled and dominates. Say so in your essay: it tells the reader what to improve, and it shows you understand your data.

Gradient uncertainty

For a straight-line graph, plot the points with error bars and draw the steepest and shallowest lines that still pass through all of them. Then

$$\Delta m = \frac{m_\text{max} - m_\text{min}}{2}$$

Check the gradient's units: a graph of energy in J against moment of inertia in kg m² has a gradient in J kg⁻¹ m⁻².

Then use the uncertainty

An uncertainty you calculate and never mention again is wasted. Use it to make judgements:

  • Does the gradient's range include zero? If so, you haven't shown a trend, and you must say so.
  • Does your value agree with theory within the combined uncertainty? That is the test of agreement, not whether the numbers look close.
  • Which measurement contributes most to the total uncertainty, and what would reduce it?

These sentences are where Criterion D marks are earned.

11. Processing and presenting data

Units everywhere

Every column heading, every axis and every quoted value needs a unit. Use SI units and a slash on axes and headings: kinetic energy / J, moment of inertia / kg m². Missing units on graph axes are one of the most common and most avoidable faults in physics EEs.

Significant figures

Round each value so its last digit is in the same decimal place as its uncertainty: (0.376 ± 0.004) J, not 0.3758134766 ± 0.004 J. Spreadsheets give ten digits by default. Fixing this takes five minutes and makes the whole essay look more professional.

Graphs: always a scatter plot

In a spreadsheet's line or bar chart, the x-values are treated as text labels and spaced evenly, whatever their real values. Any trendline is then fitted against 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 instead of your data, and its gradient is physically meaningless. Students have quoted gradients wrong by a factor of 10,000 this way. Use a scatter (XY) chart. Also:

  • add error bars on both axes where they are meaningful;
  • label every axis with the quantity and its unit;
  • number and caption every graph, and refer to it in the text: a figure you never mention doesn't support your argument;
  • label each series clearly when there are two on one graph, so no one has to guess which line is theory and which is measurement.

Linearise where you can

If theory predicts $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{L}{g}}$, don't plot $T$ against $L$ and fit a curve. Plot $T^2$ against $L$: the gradient is $\dfrac{4\pi^2}{g}$, so you can find $g$ with its uncertainty. Linearising makes the model testable, not just illustrated, and it is one of the clearest signs of a strong physics essay.

Watch out for circular analysis

If you calculate a quantity from your measurements using the very relationship you claim to be testing, you haven't tested anything. For example, if you find $\omega$ from $v$ using $v = \omega r$ and take $I$ from theory, the ratio of rotational to translational kinetic energy comes out as $\dfrac{I}{mr^2}$ automatically: it contains no measurement at all.

That doesn't make the work invalid, but you must be open about it: say what you measured, what you deduced, and what the comparison with theory actually tests. Examiners spot a deduction presented as a result and mark it down under Criterion D. Being upfront about it does the opposite.

13. Finding your argument

Criterion C asks for "a clear, sustained line of argument", and most students find this the hardest idea in the whole EE. It sounds like something from a history essay. In physics, it is simpler than it sounds.

Your argument is your answer to the research question, plus the reasons a sceptical physicist should believe it, plus the limits on how far it can be trusted. It is not a debate or an opinion. It is a claim, backed by evidence, with its conditions stated.

Description is not an argument

"I measured the period of a pendulum at amplitudes from 5° to 60°, and it increased" is a description. Nobody could disagree with it, and it doesn't answer a question. An argument makes a claim that could have turned out to be wrong, and then shows why it isn't:

"The small-angle equation predicts the period to within 1% only up to about 23°. Above that, the period grows with amplitude as the first correction term predicts, reaching about 7% longer at 60°."

That claim is specific, it has numbers, someone could test it, and the rest of the essay exists to support it.

The one-sentence test

Before you write the essay, finish this sentence:

My results show that ___, because ___, although ___.

  • "show that" is your claim: the direct answer to the RQ, with numbers.
  • "because" is your strongest evidence: the graph, the gradient, the comparison with uncertainty.
  • "although" is the limit: the assumption that may fail, the range it holds over, the biggest uncertainty. This is where your evaluation comes from.

If you can't fill in the blanks, you aren't ready to write yet: go back to your analysis. If your sentence would be true whatever your data had shown, it is a description, not a claim.

The shapes a physics argument usually takes

ShapeThe claim sounds likeThe key evidence
Where a model breaks down"Model X works within 2% up to Y, but beyond that it fails because assumption Z no longer holds."The measured value against the model's prediction over the whole range, with the point where they stop agreeing within uncertainty
Which of two models fits"Drag proportional to $v^2$ describes the data better than drag proportional to $v$, over this range of speeds."Each model's linearised graph, and which one is straight within the error bars
A relationship"Q depends on P as $Q \propto P^{n}$, with $n = 1.48 \pm 0.06$, consistent with the predicted 1.5."A log–log or linearised graph and its gradient with uncertainty
Two routes to one quantity"Method A gives (a ± Δa) and method B gives (b ± Δb); they agree / disagree, because…"The overlap (or not) of two independent results

Choosing a shape early helps: it tells you what your key graph has to be, and so what data you need.

How to find it

  1. Start with a predicted argument. Once you have a research question and a model, write the claim you expect to make: "I expect the small-angle equation to fail above about 20°." That is your hypothesis, and it shapes your method.
  2. Revise it when the data arrive. Your final argument is whatever your analysis supports, even if it is the opposite of what you expected. An argument that the model failed, explained well, is just as strong.
  3. Ask "so what?" twice. "The gradient of my graph was 0.064 ± 0.002." So what? "The first correction term predicts 1/16 = 0.0625, which is inside that range." So what? "So the longer period at large amplitudes is explained by the term the small-angle equation leaves out, not by friction or damping." The second "so what?" usually reaches the argument.
  4. Argue with yourself. Write down what a sceptical examiner would say ("Couldn't that just be damping?"), then answer it with evidence ("the amplitude fell by less than 2° over ten swings; using the average amplitude instead of the starting one changes the predicted period by less than 0.3%, far smaller than the 7% effect"). Each answered objection strengthens the argument and earns Criterion D marks.
  5. Draft the conclusion first. Write a rough conclusion paragraph before the rest of the essay. Then every section has a clear job: to get the reader to that paragraph.

Every section has a job in the argument

SectionIts job
IntroductionShows there is a real question: what is known, what isn't, and what your essay will test.
TheoryTurns the model into a prediction, and names the assumptions that might fail.
MethodConvinces the reader that the test is fair and the measurements can be trusted.
AnalysisProvides the evidence: the key graph and the comparison with uncertainty.
Discussion and evaluationConsiders other explanations, tests the assumptions, and sets the limits of the claim.
ConclusionStates the claim, with numbers and conditions. Nothing new.

Anything that doesn't do one of these jobs (a page of history, a theory section that is never used, a graph you never mention) weakens the argument by distracting from it. Cut it.

Check that the line holds

  • Read only the first and last sentence of each section, in order. Do they tell a coherent story that ends at your answer? If not, the line of argument is broken, and no amount of good data will repair it.
  • Every graph and table should be referred to in the text, with a sentence saying what it shows for the argument.
  • Use signposts that show the reasoning: "This suggests…", "However…", "To test whether…", "This rules out…", "Therefore…".
  • Your conclusion's first sentence should be your one-sentence argument, now with final numbers.

14. Writing analytically

Descriptive writing answers what, where and who. Analytical writing answers why, how and so what.

DescriptiveAnalytical
Presents the dataUses the data to build an argument
States that the gradient was 0.42Explains what the gradient means physically, and what its value says about the model
Reports that the results differed from theoryGives the size of the difference, compares it with the uncertainty, and identifies which assumption failed
Explains a theoryApplies the theory, and judges where it holds and where it breaks down

Build each point with PEELL

  • Point: state it.
  • Evidence: data, a calculation or a citation.
  • Explain how the evidence supports the point, physically.
  • Link back to the research question.
  • Link forward to your next point.

Points add up to an argument

Each PEELL point is one step in your argument. See Finding your argument for how to work out what the argument is and check that it holds together.

Phrases that cost marks

  • "This proves…" Physics doesn't prove: results support, are consistent with, or fail to support a model.
  • "Human error" means nothing. Name the cause: parallax, reaction time, an inconsistent release.
  • "The results were quite accurate." Accurate to what, within what uncertainty? Give numbers.
  • "Due to friction" as an explanation for everything. If friction is your explanation, estimate its size and show that it accounts for the difference.

15. Discussion, evaluation and conclusion

Discussion

A balanced discussion considers more than one interpretation. For each finding: what does it mean physically? Does it match the prediction? If not, what could explain the difference, and can you tell the explanations apart with the evidence you have? Go back to the literature here: how do your results compare with published values? Agreement is a finding worth stating; disagreement is a finding worth investigating.

Evaluation: weave it in, don't bolt it on

A separate evaluation section after the conclusion is a lab-report habit that doesn't belong in an essay. Evaluate your sources, methods and results at the point in the discussion where each one comes up. Evaluate three things:

  • Strengths. Most students leave these out, and lose marks, because the criterion asks for strengths and limitations. What did your design do well? Which variable did you control especially well? Was your uncertainty small compared with the effect?
  • Limitations, with their size. Not everything that could have gone wrong, but a ranked account of what actually mattered. For each one: is it systematic or random, which way does it push your result, roughly how big is it, and what specific change would reduce it? "Use better equipment" is not an improvement; "replace hand timing with a light gate, reducing the timing uncertainty from ±0.02 s to ±0.001 s" is.
  • Your assumptions. You listed them in the introduction; now test them. Did the cylinder really roll without slipping? Was the approximation valid over your whole range?

Conclusion

  • Answer the RQ directly, in the first sentence.
  • Give a quantitative answer with its uncertainty wherever you can.
  • State the conditions under which the answer holds.
  • Say what remains unresolved, and suggest a specific next step.
  • Introduce nothing new.

Don't overclaim. Writing that the data "closely followed the theoretical model" is fatal when the graph shows a large gap. Examiners read the graph.

17. Twenty ways to lose marks

Every one of these turns up in real essays, and most take less than an hour to fix.

Structure

  1. The essay is set out as a lab report, with the evaluation after the conclusion.
  2. There is no introduction: the RQ appears only on the title page.
  3. The RQ is worded differently on the title page, in the introduction and in the conclusion.
  4. The candidate code is missing, or the word count on the title page is wrong.
  5. The contents page has no page numbers.
  6. A personal engagement section uses up words that earn nothing.

Physics and analysis

  1. An average velocity is used where an instantaneous one is needed, or another slightly different quantity is measured from the one in the equation.
  2. A quantity is defined differently, or counted twice, between the theoretical and experimental calculations.
  3. A line chart is used, so the trendline gradient is calculated against category labels.
  4. Graph axes or table headings have no units.
  5. Values are given to ten significant figures beside a one- or two-figure uncertainty.
  6. An uncertainty is calculated and then never used to test anything.
  7. The instrument resolution is quoted as the uncertainty when the spread of the repeats is bigger.
  8. A large difference is blamed on friction, with no estimate of how big the friction is.
  9. A circular calculation is presented as an experimental result.

Method

  1. The method can't be repeated, especially at the key manipulation.
  2. The apparatus diagram doesn't match the data tables (a different angle or height).
  3. The geometry doesn't add up: the angle, length and height don't satisfy $\sin\theta = \dfrac{h}{L}$.

Sources

  1. The bibliography is mostly revision sites, YouTube and Reddit, not textbooks and papers.
  2. Citations in the text don't appear in the reference list, or sources are listed but never used.

18. Sources, referencing and AI

Choosing sources

Criterion B judges whether your research materials are relevant and comprehensive. A bibliography of revision websites won't support a high mark, however accurate they are. A rough order of quality for physics:

  1. Peer-reviewed journals. American Journal of Physics, European Journal of Physics and Physics Education are written at a level you can read, and are full of ideas for EEs.
  2. University textbooks, such as OpenStax University Physics (free online).
  3. Data from institutions and observatories, such as NASA, NIST, CERN Open Data and JPL Horizons.
  4. University lecture notes and course pages.
  5. Only to get your bearings: HyperPhysics, Khan Academy and revision sites. Use them to orient yourself, then find and cite what they summarise.

Never cite Reddit, Stack Exchange or YouTube as the authority for a physics result. Remove sources you didn't use: an off-topic entry in your reference list counts against the relevance judgement in Criterion B.

Referencing

Choose one style (MLA, APA, IEEE or Chicago) and use it consistently. Cite at the point of use. Every citation in the text must appear in the list, and every entry in the list must be cited somewhere. Give access dates for web sources. References don't count towards the word limit, so there is no reason to skimp.

Artificial intelligence

  • You may use AI tools to support your research, but you must not use generative AI to write any part of your essay.
  • Anything you take from an AI tool (copied, paraphrased, or an image you changed) must be cited where you use it and listed in your references, with the prompt and the date.
  • Keep a record of your prompts. It protects you.
  • AI output contains errors and biases. Check everything against a real source, and cite that source, not the AI.
  • Follow the school's AI policy and the IB's academic integrity policy.

19. Reflection and the RPF

Criterion E (4 marks) is marked only on the reflective statement on your Reflection and Progress Form (RPF), up to 500 words. It is not marked on your essay. It has two strands: whether your reflection is evaluative rather than descriptive, and whether it shows your growth and transfer of learning.

Scores badlyScores well
A story of what you did: "First I chose my topic, then I collected data, then I wrote the essay." This is description, and it keeps you in the bottom band.Specific examples: the moment, the decision, the change of mind.
Evaluation: not just what happened, but what it was worth. What did a setback teach you? Why did you change approach?
Transfer: how a skill you developed applies elsewhere: your IA, another subject, university, work. This is the strand most often missed completely.

This is where your personal engagement belongs. The 500-word limit is strict (examiners stop reading there), and the statement must be in the same language as your essay. Make notes after each reflection session, while it is fresh.

Title page

  • Candidate code (no name, no school)
  • Research question, written as a question
  • Subject: Physics
  • Word count, correct and under 4,000

Structure

  • Contents page with accurate page numbers
  • An introduction containing the RQ word for word
  • The RQ identical on the title page, in the introduction and in the conclusion
  • The conclusion is the last section of text
  • Evaluation inside the discussion, not after the conclusion
  • A reference list or bibliography
  • No personal engagement section

Physics

  • Theory derived, with its assumptions stated
  • Method repeatable from the description alone
  • Apparatus diagram consistent with the data tables
  • Geometry and stated values consistent with each other
  • SI units throughout

  • Significant figures matched to the uncertainties
  • Every graph a scatter plot, with labelled axes, units and error bars
  • Every figure and table numbered, captioned and referred to in the text

  • Every citation in the list, and every entry cited
  • No unused or off-topic sources
  • One referencing style throughout
  • Any AI use acknowledged and cited

  • Proofread by you. Nobody else may proofread, correct or edit it.

  • RPF complete, under 500 words, in the same language as the essay
  • All three reflection sessions attended and recorded

21. Check your understanding

A student's research question is "How do the length and the diameter of a wire affect its resistance?" What is wrong with it?

It is two questions joined by "and", so it isn't focused. It also gives no range and names no model to test. Choose one variable (say, diameter), give its range, and make the question test something: for example, how well $R = \dfrac{\rho L}{A}$ predicts the resistance for diameters from 0.2 mm to 1.0 mm.

A trolley starts from rest and travels 1.20 m down a ramp in 1.50 s. What speed should you use for its kinetic energy at the bottom?

$\dfrac{d}{t} = 0.80\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ is the average speed. For uniform acceleration from rest, the final speed is twice that: $v = \dfrac{2d}{t} = 1.60\ \text{m s}^{-1}$. Using 0.80 would make the kinetic energy four times too small.

$m = (0.500 \pm 0.002)$ kg and $v = (2.40 \pm 0.06)\ \text{m s}^{-1}$. Find $E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ with its uncertainty, and say which measurement to improve.

$E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.500 \times 2.40^2 = 1.44$ J. Fractional uncertainty $= \dfrac{0.002}{0.500} + 2 \times \dfrac{0.06}{2.40} = 0.004 + 0.050 = 0.054$, so $\Delta E_\text{k} = 0.08$ J and $E_\text{k} = (1.44 \pm 0.08)$ J. The velocity contributes almost all of it, so improve the velocity measurement (for example, with light gates or video analysis).

Your gradient is $(2.45 \pm 0.08)$ in suitable units. Theory predicts 2.31. What should you conclude?

Your range is 2.37 to 2.53, which doesn't include 2.31, so the result does not agree with the model within uncertainty. Don't call it "close". Say that there is a significant difference (about 6%), look for a systematic error or an assumption that failed, and discuss the possible causes with evidence.

Your energy calculation shows that 65% of the energy was lost as a ball rolled down a short ramp. What should you do first?

Check your analysis before writing about friction. A loss that large over a short distance is almost certainly a mistake: an average speed used as a final speed, a wrong unit, or the wrong height. A difference of more than about 20% is rarely a physical effect.

The steepest line through the error bars has a gradient of 0.52 and the shallowest 0.44. What are the gradient and its uncertainty?

Use the best-fit gradient, which should lie close to the middle of 0.44 and 0.52, about 0.48. Its uncertainty is $\dfrac{0.52 - 0.44}{2} = 0.04$, so the gradient is $0.48 \pm 0.04$, with its units.

A student writes a spreadsheet model of a projectile with air resistance. Name two checks that show the model can be trusted.

Any two of: set the drag to zero and check that it gives the exact no-drag results (for example, the range $\dfrac{u^2\sin 2\theta}{g}$); check that a falling object levels off at the terminal velocity calculated by hand; halve the time step and check that the result hardly changes; check that energy is conserved when there is no drag. Then compare the model with real data, such as a video of a launched ball.

Which of these is an argument? (a) "I measured how the terminal velocity of steel balls in glycerol changed with their radius." (b) "Terminal velocity was proportional to $r^2$, as Stokes' law predicts, up to a radius of 3 mm; above that it rose more slowly, because the flow around the ball was no longer smooth."

(b). It makes a specific claim that could have been wrong, gives the evidence (the $r^2$ relationship), and states its limit (3 mm) with a physical reason. (a) only describes what was done: nobody could disagree with it.

Your finished essay is 2,400 words long. Is that a good thing?

Not necessarily. Equations, tables and graphs don't count, so physics essays often come in short, and a short essay has usually skimped on the literature or the discussion. Check that your discussion and evaluation (Criterion D, 8 marks) are developed fully.

You want to explain that you chose your topic because of a holiday visit to a hydroelectric dam. Where should this go?

In your RPF reflection, not in the essay. No essay criterion rewards personal engagement, and it uses up your 4,000 words. In the RPF, make it evaluative: say how that interest shaped your decisions and what you learned from them.
