    Two ways to calculate

    Orbits. Each planet follows the exact ellipse that the star's gravity alone gives it, and each moon the exact ellipse around its planet. This is always stable, however fast time runs. What it leaves out: the planets do not pull on each other.

    Full gravity. Every planet and the star pull on every other with $F = G\dfrac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}$, and the motion is worked out step by step. Orbits slowly change, as real ones do. Moons still follow exact ellipses around their moving planet. Step-by-step calculation is never perfect, so the total energy is checked live: the "energy drift" should stay tiny.

    Changing a mass or giving a planet a push switches to Full gravity, starting from wherever everything is at that moment, because the old ellipses no longer apply.

    What is real here

    Every number comes from the physics, in SI units, calculated as you watch. The constants are the data booklet's: $G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{N m}^2\,\text{kg}^{-2}$.

    • Sizes. Far-away bodies are drawn enlarged so you can see them. The label at the bottom left always says by how much. Distances between bodies are to scale. Asteroids are drawn about 2500 times too big.
    • Stars. Brightness, size and colour come from the star's mass using simple rules for ordinary stars ($L \propto M^4$, then $L = \sigma A T^4$ for the temperature).
    • Brightness. Outer planets are shown brighter than they would look: the exposure is adjusted, as a camera would.
    • Test mass. Only the planet's gravity acts on it: no air, no spin, no star.
    • Potential well. A model: height stands for gravitational potential. Space is not really shaped like this.
