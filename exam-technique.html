Command terms and exam technique

Knowing the physics is only half of doing well in an exam. You also need to know what each question is asking for, how much to write, and how to set out your working so that every mark you deserve can be awarded. This page brings together the advice in the IB examiners' reports from the first exams on the current course (2025 and 2026) and the habits that make the biggest difference. All the examples are new.

2. Multiple-choice questions (Paper 1A)

You have about 2 minutes per question on average, but some take 30 seconds and a few take longer. The aim is to find the correct option, not to work everything out in full as you would in Paper 2. Examiners recommend these strategies:

  • Read the whole stem, carefully. Questions are kept short, so every word matters. A question that looks like one you have seen before may differ in one important detail.
  • Picture the situation, or sketch it. A quick diagram or sketch graph often shows the answer straight away.
  • Eliminate the options that are clearly wrong. Often two can be ruled out quickly, doubling your chance even if you have to guess.
  • Check the units of each option. An expression with the wrong units can't be right.
  • Try extreme values. Make an angle 0° or 90°, or a mass very large or zero, and see which option still makes sense.
  • Use ratios instead of full calculations when a question asks what happens when something is doubled or halved (see section 3).
  • Don't work out numbers too early. Set up the expression first: factors often cancel, and powers of ten are quicker to combine by hand than on a calculator.
  • Use the graph's axes: the units of the gradient and of the area tell you what they represent.
  • $g \approx 10$ $\text{m s}^{-2}$ is fine here. The examiners' keys allow it; with 9.8 the correct option is still the closest one.
  • Choose the best answer. Sometimes no option is perfect, or all four are true statements and only one actually answers the question asked.
  • Never leave a blank. There is no penalty for a wrong answer.

Worked example: try an extreme value

A block slides down a smooth slope at an angle $\theta$ to the horizontal. Which expression gives its acceleration? A: $g\cos\theta$   B: $g\sin\theta$   C: $g\tan\theta$   D: $\dfrac{g}{\sin\theta}$

Make the slope flat: $\theta = 0$. The block shouldn't accelerate at all, so the answer must be 0. A gives $g$, C gives 0, D is infinite, and B gives 0. Now make the slope vertical: $\theta = 90^{\circ}$. The block falls freely, so the answer is $g$: B gives $g$, but C is infinite. The answer is B, with no working needed.

Worked example: use a ratio

A satellite's circular orbit is changed so that its radius is four times larger. What happens to its orbital speed?

Write the relationship, then the new situation: $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{GM}{r}}$, so $v_\text{new} = \sqrt{\dfrac{GM}{4r}} = \dfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\dfrac{GM}{r}} = \dfrac{1}{2}v_\text{old}$. The speed halves. There's no need for any numbers, and leaving the 4 alone until the end avoids mistakes.

3. Practice: ratio questions

Examiners note that ratio questions appear often and that students need plenty of practice with them. Try each before you open it.

1. The length of a simple pendulum is made 9 times longer. What happens to its period?

$T = 2\pi\sqrt{l/g}$, so $T \propto \sqrt{l}$: the period becomes $\sqrt{9} = 3$ times longer.

2. You move three times further from a small lamp that radiates equally in all directions. What happens to the intensity?

$I = \dfrac{P}{4\pi r^2}$, so $I \propto 1/r^2$: the intensity becomes $1/9$ of its value.

3. A wire is replaced by one of the same metal that is twice as long and has twice the diameter. What happens to the resistance?

$R = \dfrac{\rho L}{A}$ and $A \propto d^2$, so $R \propto L/d^2$: the resistance becomes $2/2^2 = 1/2$ of its value.

4. A fixed amount of ideal gas has its kelvin temperature doubled and its volume made four times larger. What happens to its pressure?

$P = \dfrac{nRT}{V}$, so $P \propto T/V$: the pressure becomes $2/4 = 1/2$ of its value.

5. The kinetic energy of a cyclist increases by a factor of 4. What happens to the cyclist's speed?

$E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, so $v \propto \sqrt{E_\text{k}}$: the speed doubles.

6. Both charges in a pair are doubled, and so is the distance between them. What happens to the electric force between them?

$F = \dfrac{kq_1q_2}{r^2}$: the force is multiplied by $\dfrac{2\times2}{2^2} = 1$. It stays the same.

7. The mass on a spring is made four times larger. What happens to the frequency of oscillation?

$T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$, so $T$ doubles, and the frequency $f = 1/T$ halves.

4. Data-based questions (Paper 1B)

Paper 1B tests the skills you use in practical work and the Internal Assessment, often with an experiment you haven't seen before. Examiners' advice:

  • Find a gradient from the line of best fit, not from data points. Use a large triangle across most of the line. In 2026 fewer than 20% of students did this.
  • A line of best fit may be a curve. It should be a straight line or smooth curve through the error bars, chosen with the physics in mind.
  • Read the axes carefully: units, powers of ten, and whether the graph starts at the origin. The point where the axes meet is not always (0, 0).
  • Watch for radius against diameter, and for converting units together with their uncertainties.
  • Know the difference between accuracy and precision, and between the uncertainty of one reading and the spread of repeated readings.
  • Random and systematic errors are different. A straight line that should pass through the origin but doesn't points to a systematic error.
  • A conclusion must answer the purpose of the experiment, with evidence: for example, whether the accepted value lies within the uncertainty range.
  • Follow the design of the question. If parts (a) and (b) find an uncertainty, part (c) probably needs it.
  • Practise with logarithms and log scales, which examiners found difficult, especially at SL.

5. Command terms: what the question wants

Every exam question starts with a command term: a verb such as state, outline or explain. It tells you exactly what kind of answer is wanted and how deep to go. The IB groups them by assessment objective (AO): AO1 is recalling, AO2 is applying, AO3 is analysing and evaluating. Examiners report that many lost marks come from answering a different command term from the one asked, for example describing when the question said explain.

Other verbs (such as compare, comment or justify) can appear too; read them in their everyday meaning. Examiners report that outline and suggest are often answered too briefly: an outline still needs every key point, and a suggestion still needs a reason.

A question asks you to "Outline why the trolley slows down" for 2 marks. A student writes "friction". How many marks?

Probably none. "Friction" is a single word, not an outline, and it doesn't say why the force slows the trolley. A 2-mark answer: "Friction acts on the trolley opposite to its motion, so there is a resultant force backwards. By Newton's second law this gives an acceleration opposite to the velocity, so the trolley slows down."

6. "Show that" questions

A "show that" question gives you the answer. The marks are for proving it, so the examiner needs to see every step. The stated value also lets you carry on with later parts if you get stuck: always use it in the next part if you weren't sure of your own answer.

The rules

  1. Write the equation you are using.
  2. Substitute every value, with powers of ten, before working out the answer.
  3. Give your final answer to at least one more significant figure than the value in the question. If the question says "about 2.4 kW", write 2.42 kW, not 2.4 kW.
  4. Never use the value you are asked to show in your working. That is a circular argument and scores nothing.
  5. If your answer is far from the stated value, check for an error rather than forcing it.

Why the extra significant figure? Writing the same rounded value that the question gives doesn't prove that you calculated it: you could have copied it. One more figure shows the examiner that the number came from your working. Examiners state this explicitly in their reports.

Worked example: a "show that" answer

A car of mass 1200 kg travels at 15 $\text{m s}^{-1}$. Show that its kinetic energy is about $1.4\times10^{5}$ J. [2]

A full-mark answer: $E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}\times1200\times15^2$ $= 1.35\times10^{5}$ J. The equation and the substitution earn the first mark; the answer, to 3 significant figures, earns the second.

Answers that lose marks:

  • "$E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 = 1.4\times10^{5}$ J": no substitution, and no more precise than the question.
  • "$\tfrac{1}{2}\times1200\times v^2 = 1.4\times10^{5}$, so $v = 15.3$, which is about 15 $\text{m s}^{-1}$": this works backwards from the answer, so it proves nothing.

Some "show that" questions ask for an equation rather than a number, for example "Show that the period is independent of the radius". Then every algebraic step must be visible, so that the examiner can follow each substitution and rearrangement. Examiners regularly report derivations where steps are missing or out of order.

7. Writing explanations

"Explain" questions are where most marks are lost. An explanation is not a definition, and it is not a restatement of the question. Examiners want you to link the situation in the question to a physics law or relationship, and then use that law, in correct scientific language, to reach the answer. In 2026, for example, many students quoted Faraday's law correctly but didn't apply it to the situation described, and so didn't earn the marks.

A structure that works

  1. Name the physics: which law, principle or relationship applies here?
  2. Apply it to this situation: what changes, and what stays the same, in the question's own context?
  3. Follow the chain: cause → effect → effect, using precise terms, one link per step.
  4. Answer the question: finish with the conclusion it asked for.

Aim for one clear, separate idea per mark. Use "so", "therefore" and "because" to make the chain visible.

Worked example: a lamp's I–V graph

The current in a filament lamp increases less and less as the potential difference across it increases. Explain why. [3]

Weak answer: "Because the lamp is non-ohmic." This only names the behaviour; it gives no cause, so it scores nothing.

Strong answer:

  • A larger current transfers energy to the filament at a greater rate, so its temperature rises. (applies the physics to this lamp)
  • At a higher temperature the metal ions vibrate with greater amplitude, so the electrons collide with them more often. (the mechanism, in precise terms)
  • So the resistance ($R = V/I$) increases, and each further increase in potential difference produces a smaller increase in current. (answers the question)

Worked example: a magnet falling through a copper pipe

A strong magnet is dropped down a vertical copper pipe. It falls much more slowly than it does outside the pipe. Explain why. [3]

Weak answer: "Because of Lenz's law." A law with no link to the pipe earns nothing.

Strong answer:

  • As the magnet falls, the magnetic flux through each part of the pipe changes, so by Faraday's law an emf is induced in the copper.
  • Copper is a good conductor, so the emf drives currents around the pipe.
  • By Lenz's law these currents produce a magnetic field that opposes the change causing them, so there is an upward force on the magnet. This reduces its acceleration, and it soon falls at a low, steady speed.

Say exactly what you mean. Vague words lose marks, because the examiner can't be sure you know the correct physics:

Instead of…write…
"gravity""gravitational force", "weight" or "gravitational field strength" (whichever you mean)
"energy is lost" or "used up""energy is transferred to the surroundings as thermal energy"
"heat" (for what a hot object contains)"internal energy"; heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference
"the voltage through the resistor""the potential difference across the resistor"

  • Don't copy the question into your answer. Marks are only for what you add.
  • Make microscopic links when asked about mechanisms: conduction, resistance, pressure and entropy are all explained by what particles do.
  • Use the data given. If the stem gives a graph, a value or a diagram, your explanation usually needs to refer to it.
"Explain why a gas exerts a pressure on the walls of its container" [3]. Write the three marking points before you look.

(1) The molecules move randomly and collide with the walls. (2) In each collision a molecule's momentum changes, so the wall exerts a force on it, and it exerts an equal and opposite force on the wall (Newton's third law). (3) Many collisions per second over the wall's area give a steady average force per unit area, which is the pressure.

8. Setting out calculations

A correct final answer usually earns full marks, but an incorrect final answer with no working earns nothing. With clear working you keep the method marks, and if a later part uses your earlier wrong answer you can still get full credit there ("error carried forward", ECF). Examiners report students losing ECF marks because their working was too disorganised to follow.

  • Write the equation, then a complete substitution, then the answer, ideally one under the other. Copying an equation from the data booklet without using it earns nothing.
  • Convert to SI units first: cm to m, g to kg, minutes to s, eV to J. Watch the powers of ten; examiners call out power-of-ten errors in every session.
  • Read the question again for the quantity it asks for. Radius or diameter? The speed at the top or at the bottom? Has the electron's mass been included?
  • Don't round in the middle of a calculation. Keep your calculator's value and round only the final answer.
  • Give the final answer with a unit and sensible significant figures, usually 2 or 3, matching the data. Give decimals rather than fractions or surds.
  • Check your calculator mode (degrees or radians) before any question with angles.
  • Ask: is this answer sensible? Examiners see stars closer than a kilometre and photons with the energy of a car. Know some typical sizes:
QuantityTypical size
diameter of an atom$10^{-10}$ m
diameter of a nucleus$10^{-15}$ m

density of water; of a gas at room conditions$1000$ $\text{kg m}^{-3}$; about $1$ $\text{kg m}^{-3}$

9. One problem, three methods

Many problems can be solved in more than one way. The first method that comes to mind isn't always the quickest, and in an exam the quickest correct method saves time. Energy methods are often fastest when a question asks for a speed after a change of height or position.

Worked example: a crate sliding down a ramp

A crate of mass 2.0 kg is released from rest at the top of a smooth ramp. The ramp is 3.0 m long and makes an angle of 30° with the horizontal. Friction is negligible. Calculate the speed of the crate at the bottom.

Method 1: Newton's second law, then the equations of motion. The component of the weight down the slope is $mg\sin30^{\circ}$, so $a = g\sin30^{\circ} = 4.9$ $\text{m s}^{-2}$. Then $v^2 = u^2 + 2as$ $= 0 + 2\times4.9\times3.0 = 29.4$, so $v = 5.4$ $\text{m s}^{-1}$.

Method 2: work done by the resultant force = change in kinetic energy. The resultant force is $mg\sin30^{\circ} = 9.8$ N along the slope, and it acts over 3.0 m: $W = 9.8\times3.0 = 29.4$ J $= \tfrac{1}{2}\times2.0\times v^2$, so $v = 5.4$ $\text{m s}^{-1}$. The normal force does no work, because it is perpendicular to the motion.

Method 3: conservation of energy. The crate drops a height $h = 3.0\sin30^{\circ} = 1.5$ m. With no friction, $mgh = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, so $v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2\times9.8\times1.5} = 5.4$ $\text{m s}^{-1}$.

Method 3 is the quickest and shows that the mass cancels: any crate reaches the same speed. But energy methods can't tell you the time taken; for that you need method 1.

10. Presenting your answers

  • Write inside the answer boxes. Papers are scanned, and examiners don't have to look for work outside the box. Do rough work neatly in the space around the question.
  • If you need more space, use the extra pages and write the question number clearly, then note in the box that the answer continues there.
  • Make it legible. Illegible work can't be credited, and digits such as 4, 7 and 9, and powers of ten, must be unambiguous.
  • Make the final answer obvious, for example by underlining it.
  • Cross out, don't scribble over, anything you want ignored. Examiners mark what isn't crossed out.
  • Practise with real answer boxes. Examiners noticed that some students seemed to be using them for the first time in the exam.

11. What examiners found difficult

The reports on the first exams on this course (May and November 2025, May 2026) list these as areas where many students lost marks. Each is worth extra revision:

  • Graphs: acceleration–time graphs, the meaning of areas and gradients, and log–log graphs (A.1, Skills).
  • Rotational energy: problems needing both translational and rotational kinetic energy (A.4).
  • Simple harmonic motion: energy changes, and the SHM equations at HL (C.1).
  • Thermal physics: conduction explained in terms of particles, energy transfers during melting, and adiabatic changes (B.1, B.4).
  • The greenhouse effect: the mechanism, and energy-balance calculations (B.2).
  • Fields: precise definitions (the test charge!), the superposition of fields, the magnetic field of a wire, and gravitational potential graphs (D.1, D.2, D.3).
  • Induction: applying Faraday's law to the situation described, not just quoting it (D.4).
  • Waves: coherence, harmonics and boundary conditions, and the Doppler effect for light (C.3, C.4, C.5).
  • Relativity: the space–time interval (A.5).
  • Nuclear physics: linking the shape of the binding energy per nucleon curve to the short range of the strong force; the continuous beta spectrum as evidence for the neutrino (E.3).
  • Stars: fusion at the level of particles, and mass defect and energy calculations (E.5).
  • Circuits: internal resistance (B.5).

12. Learning from your mistakes

When you get a test back, sort every lost mark into one of these types. Each type has a different fix, and the pattern tells you what to do before the next test.

Type of mistakeWhat happenedHow to fix it
MissingYou had no idea how to start: a topic you hadn't revised.Make your own notes on the topic, then practise questions on it.
MisappliedYou knew the theory but couldn't use it in this question. Very common in physics.Redo hard questions until you can get them fully right without help.
MisinformedYou were confident, but your physics was wrong. The most dangerous type, because you won't think to revise it.Find out what is actually correct, ask what would happen if your idea were true, and test yourself on it again later.
MisrememberedYou swapped two facts or two symbols in an equation.Find a new way to remember it, and check equations against the data booklet.
Not enough detailYou made two points for a 4-mark question.Match the number of points to the marks.
MisunderstoodYou answered a different question, such as describing when asked to explain.Underline the command term and the key words before you start.
MisreadYou answered for "larger" when it said "smaller", or used the diameter as the radius.Underline key words, and reread the question before writing the final answer.
Wrong wordThe mark needed a precise term, or you used a vague one ("gravity", "energy lost").Study mark schemes and learn the exact terms (see section 7).
SlipYou knew it, but wrote a number down wrongly or had the calculator in the wrong mode.Check answers by working backwards or estimating the size.
Ran out of timeYou didn't finish, or turned over two pages at once.Practise single questions and whole papers against the clock.
