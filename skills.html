In the IB guide they are the "Tools", and this page covers Tool 3 (Mathematics) in full. Exam questions test these skills throughout Papers 1 and 2, so it's worth getting them right early. Knowledge and science

Nature of science

MeasurementEvidenceScience as a shared endeavour

Measurement. Every measurement has an uncertainty. A result without one can't be compared fairly with a prediction or with anyone else's result.

Evidence. Two results only disagree if their uncertainty ranges don't overlap. Uncertainties tell scientists whether a surprising result is real evidence or just a measurement that happened to come out high.

Shared endeavour. The SI units are agreed internationally. Since 2019, all seven base units have been defined from fixed constants of nature, such as the speed of light and the Planck constant, so any well-equipped laboratory in the world can reproduce them.

ToK: questions to think about

  • Can we ever know the "true value"? We only ever have measurements with uncertainties. Is the true value something we discover, or an idea we use to make sense of our data?
  • How certain is certain enough? A bridge engineer and a particle physicist need very different levels of certainty. Who decides what counts as "precise enough"?
  • Are units invented or discovered? The metre was once a metal bar in Paris and is now defined using the speed of light. Did the metre change?
  • Why draw graphs? A best-fit line asks us to ignore some of the scatter in our data. When is it right to trust the pattern over the individual points?

Units and how to convert them, estimating, and the mathematics physics uses all the time.

1. SI units, prefixes and scientific notation

Physics uses the International System of Units (SI). Every other unit can be built from the seven base units:

Derived units are combinations of base units. For example, the newton comes from $F = ma$: $1\ \text{N} = 1\ \text{kg m s}^{-2}$. The joule comes from $W = Fs$: $1\ \text{J} = 1\ \text{N m} = 1\ \text{kg m}^{2}\,\text{s}^{-2}$. You can work out any derived unit the same way, from an equation that defines it:

Checking an equation with its units

Every term in a correct equation has the same units. Checking this catches many mistakes, such as a formula remembered upside down. You only need this simple check; the formal method (dimensional analysis) isn't assessed.

Worked example: is the formula possible?

A student remembers the period of a pendulum as $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{g}{l}}$. Check the units.

Right-hand side: $\sqrt{\dfrac{\text{m s}^{-2}}{\text{m}}} = \sqrt{\text{s}^{-2}} = \text{s}^{-1}$. A period is in seconds, so the formula is wrong. With $\dfrac{l}{g}$ instead, the units come out as $\sqrt{\text{s}^{2}} = \text{s}$, as they should.

A units check can't find a missing number, though: $T = \pi\sqrt{\dfrac{l}{g}}$ has the right units but is still wrong.

2. Orders of magnitude, estimates and assumptions

The order of magnitude of a value is the power of ten closest to it. Write the value in scientific notation, $a\times10^{n}$. If $a$ is less than about 3 (strictly $\sqrt{10} \approx 3.16$), the order of magnitude is $10^{n}$; otherwise round up to $10^{n+1}$.

  • A person's mass, 70 kg $= 7\times10^{1}$ kg, is of order $10^{2}$ kg.
  • The diameter of an atom, about $1\times10^{-10}$ m, is of order $10^{-10}$ m.

Two values that differ by $n$ orders of magnitude have a ratio of about $10^{n}$. An atom ($10^{-10}$ m) is five orders of magnitude bigger than its nucleus ($10^{-15}$ m): about 100 000 times bigger. Comparing orders of magnitude is a quick way to check whether an answer is sensible.

Making an estimate

An estimate uses rounded values and simple physics to get an answer to within a factor of about ten. State each value you assume, round it to 1 significant figure, and give the answer as an order of magnitude.

Worked example: the air in a classroom

Estimate the mass of air in your classroom.

Assume the room is 8 m × 7 m × 3 m, so its volume is about $170\ \text{m}^3$. The density of air is about $1.2\ \text{kg m}^{-3}$.

$m = \rho V \approx 1.2 \times 170 \approx 200$ kg, so the order of magnitude is $10^{2}$ kg. That's about the mass of three students, which surprises most people.

Assumptions: when can an effect be neglected?

Real situations are complicated, so physicists leave out effects that are too small to change the answer much. This makes the maths possible and shows the main physics clearly. A good answer says which effect is neglected and why it is small, and checks afterwards whether that was reasonable.

3. The maths you need

Physics uses mathematics as a language. Most of it is algebra you already know; this section collects the parts you will use again and again.

Rearranging equations

Rearrange the symbols to make the quantity you want the subject before you put in numbers: it's faster, you make fewer slips, and you can check the units. Do the same thing to both sides each time.

Worked example: making $r$ the subject

Rearrange $F = \dfrac{GMm}{r^{2}}$ to find $r$.

Multiply both sides by $r^{2}$: $\ Fr^{2} = GMm$.   Divide by $F$: $\ r^{2} = \dfrac{GMm}{F}$.   Take the square root: $\ r = \sqrt{\dfrac{GMm}{F}}$.

Areas and volumes

ShapeArea or volume
rectangle; trianglearea $= lw$; area $= \frac{1}{2}bh$
circle of radius $r$area $= \pi r^{2}$; circumference $= 2\pi r$
cuboidvolume $= lwh$
cylinder of radius $r$, height $h$volume $= \pi r^{2}h$; curved surface area $= 2\pi rh$
sphere of radius $r$volume $= \frac{4}{3}\pi r^{3}$; surface area $= 4\pi r^{2}$

The sphere's surface area, $4\pi r^{2}$, is behind every inverse-square law in the course: light, sound and gravitational fields spread their effect over a sphere whose area grows as $r^{2}$.

Percentages

Four percentages turn up often. Make sure you know which one a question is asking for.

PercentageCalculationUse it for
percentage change$\dfrac{\text{new} - \text{old}}{\text{old}}\times100\%$how much a quantity has changed (negative for a decrease)
percentage difference$\dfrac{|a - b|}{\text{mean of } a \text{ and } b}\times100\%$comparing two values when neither is the "right" one, such as two groups' results
percentage error$\dfrac{|\text{experimental} - \text{accepted}|}{\text{accepted}}\times100\%$comparing your result with an accepted value
percentage uncertainty$\dfrac{\Delta x}{x}\times100\%$how precise a measurement is (section 6)

For example, if a resistor's resistance rises from 12.0 Ω to 13.5 Ω as it warms up, the percentage change is $\dfrac{13.5 - 12.0}{12.0}\times100\% = +12.5\%$.

Relationships between variables

  • Continuous variables can take any value in a range: length, time, temperature. Discrete variables can only take certain values, usually counts: the number of coils on a spring, the number of sheets of paper, the number of slits in a grating.
  • Directly proportional, $y \propto x$, means $y = kx$: doubling $x$ doubles $y$, and the graph is a straight line through the origin. A straight line that misses the origin, $y = mx + c$, is linear but not proportional.
  • Inversely proportional, $y \propto \frac{1}{x}$, means $y = \frac{k}{x}$: doubling $x$ halves $y$, and $xy$ stays constant. A graph of $y$ against $\frac{1}{x}$ is a straight line through the origin.
  • A positive correlation means $y$ tends to increase as $x$ increases; a negative correlation means $y$ tends to decrease. A correlation on its own doesn't prove that one variable causes the change in the other, and it doesn't tell you the relationship is proportional.

Worked example: what happens if a variable changes?

The intensity of light from a small lamp is inversely proportional to the square of the distance, $I \propto \dfrac{1}{d^{2}}$. A light sensor is moved from 2.0 m to 5.0 m from the lamp. By what factor does the intensity change?

The distance is multiplied by $\dfrac{5.0}{2.0} = 2.5$, so the intensity is divided by $2.5^{2} = 6.25$. The new intensity is about $\dfrac{1}{6}$ of the old one. There's no need to know the lamp's power.

Rates of change

A rate of change tells you how fast one quantity changes compared with another, usually time. The average rate over an interval is $\dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta t}$. For example, a cup of coffee cooling from 80 °C to 62 °C in 6.0 minutes cools at an average rate of $\dfrac{62 - 80}{6.0} = -3.0$ °C per minute. The instantaneous rate at one moment is the gradient of the tangent to the graph at that moment (section 10).

Angles in radians

An angle in radians is the arc length divided by the radius, $\theta = \dfrac{s}{r}$. A full circle is $\dfrac{2\pi r}{r} = 2\pi$ rad, so $2\pi\ \text{rad} = 360°$.

To convert:   radians $=$ degrees $\times\dfrac{\pi}{180}$   and   degrees $=$ radians $\times\dfrac{180}{\pi}$.

For example, $45° = \dfrac{\pi}{4} = 0.785$ rad, and $1$ rad $= 57.3°$.

Circular motion (A.2), simple harmonic motion (C.1) and diffraction (C.3) use radians. For small angles in radians, $\sin\theta \approx \tan\theta \approx \theta$: for 0.10 rad, $\sin\theta = 0.0998$. Check your calculator's mode before every calculation with angles.

Logarithms and exponentials

A logarithm answers the question "what power?". $\log_{10} x$ is the power of 10 that gives $x$: $\log_{10} 1000 = 3$. The natural logarithm $\ln x$ is the power of $e$ $(= 2.718\ldots)$ that gives $x$: if $x = e^{n}$, then $\ln x = n$. Natural logs appear wherever something changes at a rate proportional to its size, such as radioactive decay (E.3).

The rules work for any base:

$$\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b \qquad \ln\left(\frac{a}{b}\right) = \ln a - \ln b$$ $$\ln\left(a^{n}\right) = n\ln a \qquad \ln\left(e^{x}\right) = x$$

Worked example: solving an exponential equation

The activity of a sample falls as $A = A_0 e^{-0.35t}$, with $t$ in minutes. How long does it take to fall to a quarter of its starting value?

$\dfrac{A}{A_0} = 0.25 = e^{-0.35t}$.   Take natural logs: $\ \ln 0.25 = -0.35t$.

$t = \dfrac{\ln 0.25}{-0.35} = \dfrac{-1.386}{-0.35} = 4.0$ minutes.

You can only take the logarithm of a pure number. On a graph, label the axis with the quantity divided by its unit, such as $\ln(A / \text{Bq})$; the log itself has no unit.

Part 2 Measurement and uncertainty

How good is a measurement, how to record its uncertainty, and how uncertainties carry through a calculation.

4. Accuracy, precision and errors

  • A measurement is accurate if it is close to the true (or accepted) value.
  • A set of measurements is precise if the readings are close to each other, whether or not they are close to the true value.

Random errors

  • Scatter readings above and below the true value, by a different amount each time. They reduce precision.
  • Examples: reaction time with a stopwatch; reading a meter whose value flickers; small changes in conditions.
  • Reduce them by repeating readings and taking the mean, or by using a more precise instrument.

Systematic errors

  • Shift every reading in the same direction, by the same amount (or the same fraction). They reduce accuracy.
  • Examples: a zero error (a balance reading 0.2 g with nothing on it); a stretched tape measure; heat lost to the surroundings in every trial.
  • Reduce them by zeroing or recalibrating the instrument, or by improving the method. Repeating doesn't help.

On a graph, a systematic error often shows up as an unexpected intercept: a straight line that should pass through the origin but doesn't. Random errors show up as scatter of the points about the line.

Reliability and validity

  • A result is reliable if you get similar values when you repeat it (repeatable) and when someone else repeats it with different equipment (reproducible).
  • A method is valid if it really measures what the question asks, with the other variables controlled. A precise measurement of the wrong thing isn't valid.

Outliers

An outlier is a reading that lies far from the pattern of the others. Look for a reason first: a misread scale, a wrong setting, a trial that went wrong. If you find one, repeat the reading if you can. Leave out an outlier only when you can justify it, say that you did, and keep it in your table. Never remove a point just because it spoils your line. On a graph, plot it, label it, and draw the best-fit line without it.

5. Significant figures

Counting significant figures

  • Non-zero digits always count: 472 has 3 s.f.
  • Zeros between non-zero digits count: 5.006 has 4 s.f.
  • Zeros at the start of a number never count: 0.0420 has 3 s.f. (the 4, the 2 and the final 0).
  • Zeros at the end of a decimal count: 2.50 has 3 s.f. They show the measurement was made to the nearest 0.01.
  • Zeros at the end of a whole number are ambiguous: 3600 could have 2, 3 or 4 s.f. Scientific notation removes the doubt: $3.6\times10^{3}$ (2 s.f.) or $3.600\times10^{3}$ (4 s.f.).

Significant figures in calculations

Multiplying, dividing, powers and roots: give the answer to the same number of significant figures as the least precise value used.   $4.8 \times 3.162 = 15.1776$, which rounds to $15$ (2 s.f.).

Adding and subtracting: give the answer to the same number of decimal places as the value with the fewest.   $12.45 + 3.1 = 15.55$, which rounds to $15.6$ (1 d.p.).

Keep extra digits in your calculator during a calculation, and round only at the end. Rounding at every step builds up errors. A long string of digits copied from the calculator claims a precision your data doesn't have.

6. Recording uncertainties

Every measurement is written as a best value ± absolute uncertainty, with a unit: for example $l = (24.5 \pm 0.1)$ cm.

A single reading

  • Analogue scale (a ruler, a thermometer, a measuring cylinder, a meter with a pointer): ± half the smallest division. A thermometer marked every 1 °C gives ±0.5 °C; a measuring cylinder marked every 2 cm³ gives ±1 cm³.
  • Two readings on an analogue scale: some measurements need you to judge two positions. A length on a ruler needs the zero end and the far end, each ±0.5 mm on a millimetre ruler. The two half-divisions add, so the length is ± one full division, ±1 mm. (This is the adding rule of section 7: a length is the difference between two positions.)
  • Digital display (a balance, a digital meter, a stopwatch): ± the smallest division, one in the last digit. A balance reading 12.34 g gives ±0.01 g.

Worked example: three single readings

A student measures a spring's length with a millimetre ruler (152 mm), the water temperature with a thermometer marked every 1 °C (24 °C), and a mass on a balance that reads to 0.1 g (48.6 g). Record each with its uncertainty.

Length: two ends, each ±0.5 mm, so $L = (152 \pm 1)$ mm.
Temperature: one reading, half a division, so $T = (24.0 \pm 0.5)$ °C. The value now has one decimal place, to match its uncertainty.
Mass: digital, so $m = (48.6 \pm 0.1)$ g.

Make the uncertainty realistic

The scale only sets the smallest uncertainty a reading can have. Always ask: how precisely could I really judge this reading? If the honest answer is "less precisely than the scale", use a bigger uncertainty and explain why.

  • A stopwatch reads to 0.01 s, but your reaction time adds an uncertainty of about 0.1–0.2 s.
  • A digital meter whose last digit keeps flickering between 4.31 and 4.36 V is uncertain by about ±0.03 V, not ±0.01 V.
  • In video analysis, the scale in the picture may read to 1 mm, but that doesn't mean you can judge a position to 1 mm. To find how high a basketball rebounds, you have to choose the frame where it is highest and judge where its blurred bottom edge is, and each pixel may cover several millimetres. A realistic uncertainty might be ±1 cm.

In your IA, say how you chose each uncertainty and why it is realistic. An uncertainty that is too small is just as wrong as one that is too big.

Repeated readings

Use the mean, $\bar{x}$, as the best value, and half the range as the uncertainty: $\Delta x = \dfrac{x_{\max} - x_{\min}}{2}$. If half the range is smaller than the uncertainty of a single reading, use the single-reading uncertainty instead: the uncertainty can never be smaller than what the instrument allows.

Worked example: repeated timings

Five students time the same falling ball: 2.31 s, 2.45 s, 2.38 s, 2.29 s and 2.42 s. Give the result with its uncertainty.

Mean: the five times add up to $11.85$ s, so $\bar{t} = \dfrac{11.85}{5} = 2.37$ s.

Half the range: $\Delta t = \dfrac{2.45 - 2.29}{2} = 0.08$ s.   So $t = (2.37 \pm 0.08)$ s.

The same uncertainty can be written three ways. For $t = (2.37 \pm 0.08)$ s:

  • absolute uncertainty: $\Delta t = 0.08$ s (same unit as the value);
  • fractional uncertainty: $\dfrac{\Delta t}{t} = \dfrac{0.08}{2.37} = 0.034$ (no unit);
  • percentage uncertainty: $3.4\%$.

How many significant figures in an uncertainty?

Round the absolute uncertainty to 1 or 2 significant figures, then give the best value to the same decimal place. Which one depends on how well you know the uncertainty itself:

  • 1 s.f. when the uncertainty is a rough estimate, which is almost always: half a scale division, half the range of a few repeats, a guess at reaction time. A second figure would claim a precision you don't have. Write $(9.7 \pm 0.3)\ \text{m s}^{-2}$. Exam mark schemes usually use 1 s.f.
  • 2 s.f. when the uncertainty itself is known well, and rounding it to 1 s.f. would change it a lot. For example, a calibrated thermometer whose certificate states ±0.15 °C: rounding to ±0.2 °C would change it by a third, so write $(21.37 \pm 0.15)$ °C.

Either way, never write $(9.748 \pm 0.278)\ \text{m s}^{-2}$ or $(9.748 \pm 0.3)\ \text{m s}^{-2}$: the value must stop at the same decimal place as its uncertainty.

Percentage error is different from percentage uncertainty. It compares your result with an accepted value: $\dfrac{|\text{experimental} - \text{accepted}|}{\text{accepted}} \times 100\%$. If the percentage error is bigger than the percentage uncertainty, there is probably a systematic error.

7. Propagating uncertainties

When you calculate a result from measured values, their uncertainties carry through to the result. The data booklet gives three rules:

Adding or subtracting: add the absolute uncertainties.

$$y = a \pm b \quad\Rightarrow\quad \Delta y = \Delta a + \Delta b$$

Multiplying or dividing: add the fractional (or percentage) uncertainties.

$$y = \frac{ab}{c} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{\Delta y}{y} = \frac{\Delta a}{a} + \frac{\Delta b}{b} + \frac{\Delta c}{c}$$

Powers: multiply the fractional uncertainty by the size of the power.

$$y = a^{n} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{\Delta y}{y} = \left|n\,\frac{\Delta a}{a}\right|$$
  • Uncertainties always add, even when you subtract the quantities. Subtracting two similar values can give a result with a very large percentage uncertainty.
  • Exact numbers, such as the 2 in $2\pi r$ or the $\frac{4}{3}$ in $\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$, have no uncertainty, so they don't appear in the uncertainty calculation.
  • A square root is a power of $\frac{1}{2}$, so it halves the fractional uncertainty.

Worked example: a temperature rise (subtracting)

Water starts at $(21.5 \pm 0.5)$ °C and ends at $(34.0 \pm 0.5)$ °C. Find the temperature rise.

$\Delta T = 34.0 - 21.5 = 12.5$ °C, with uncertainty $0.5 + 0.5 = 1.0$ °C. So the rise is $(12.5 \pm 1.0)$ °C, which is an 8% uncertainty, even though each reading was only about 2% uncertain.

Worked example: density (dividing)

A stone has mass $(240 \pm 2)$ g and volume $(88 \pm 3)\ \text{cm}^3$. Find its density.

$\rho = \dfrac{m}{V} = \dfrac{240}{88} = 2.73\ \text{g cm}^{-3}$.

$\dfrac{\Delta\rho}{\rho} = \dfrac{2}{240} + \dfrac{3}{88} = 0.008 + 0.034 = 0.042$, so $\Delta\rho = 0.042 \times 2.73 = 0.1\ \text{g cm}^{-3}$.

$\rho = (2.7 \pm 0.1)\ \text{g cm}^{-3}$. Notice that the volume uncertainty dominates: improving the volume measurement would help much more than a better balance.

Worked example: g from a pendulum (powers)

A pendulum has length $l = (0.800 \pm 0.005)$ m and period $T = (1.80 \pm 0.02)$ s. Find $g$ using $g = \dfrac{4\pi^2 l}{T^2}$.

$g = \dfrac{4\pi^2 \times 0.800}{1.80^2} = 9.75\ \text{m s}^{-2}$.

$\dfrac{\Delta g}{g} = \dfrac{\Delta l}{l} + 2\,\dfrac{\Delta T}{T} = \dfrac{0.005}{0.800} + 2 \times \dfrac{0.02}{1.80} = 0.006 + 0.022 = 0.028$. The $T$ term is doubled because $T$ is squared; $4\pi^2$ is exact.

$\Delta g = 0.028 \times 9.75 = 0.3\ \text{m s}^{-2}$, so $g = (9.7 \pm 0.3)\ \text{m s}^{-2}$. The accepted value, 9.8, lies inside this range, so the result agrees with it.

Other functions: use the highest and lowest values

The three rules don't cover functions such as $\sin\theta$ or $\ln x$. For these, work out the result for the largest and smallest possible input, and take half the difference as the uncertainty. This method works for any calculation, so you can also use it to check the rules.

Worked example: the sine of an angle

An angle is measured as $\theta = (30 \pm 2)°$. Find $\sin\theta$ with its uncertainty.

Best value: $\sin 30° = 0.500$.   Largest: $\sin 32° = 0.530$.   Smallest: $\sin 28° = 0.469$.

$\Delta(\sin\theta) = \dfrac{0.530 - 0.469}{2} = 0.03$, so $\sin\theta = 0.50 \pm 0.03$.

8. Tables and charts

Recording data in a table

  • Each column heading gives the quantity, its unit and its uncertainty, for example "Extension $x$ / mm ±1". The numbers in the column are then just numbers.
  • Every value in a column has the same number of decimal places, matching its uncertainty.
  • Record every reading, not just the mean. Keep raw data (what you read) and processed data (what you calculated, such as means) in separate, clearly headed columns.

Sketching or plotting?

A sketch shows the shape of a relationship: labelled axes but no scale, with the important features clear (through the origin or not, curving up or down, levelling off). When a question says "sketch", that's all it wants. A plot uses real data on scaled axes, and is what you draw in a practical.

9. Graphs and best-fit lines

  • Put the independent variable (the one you change) on the $x$-axis and the dependent variable (the one you measure) on the $y$-axis.
  • Label each axis with the quantity and its unit, for example "extension / cm".
  • Choose scales so that the points fill most of the graph area, and use easy steps (1, 2 or 5 per division).
  • Draw uncertainty bars on every point, showing $\pm\Delta y$ (and $\pm\Delta x$ if it is big enough to see).
  • Draw a single smooth line or curve of best fit, with the points scattered evenly either side. It should pass through as many uncertainty bars as possible. Don't join the dots, and don't force it through the origin.
  • Find the gradient from a large triangle drawn on the line, using two points far apart. Don't use two data points.

Maths link: straight lines AA SL 2.1

The gradient of a straight line is $m = \dfrac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$. In physics, the gradient has units: the units of $y$ divided by the units of $x$. For a graph of force in N against extension in m, the gradient is in $\text{N m}^{-1}$.

10. Reading a graph: gradient, area and intercepts

  • Gradient: $\dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$, with units. It is a rate only when $x$ is time. On a curve, the gradient at a point is the gradient of the tangent: a straight line that just touches the curve there.
  • Changes in gradient: a curve that gets steeper means the rate is increasing. Where the tangent is horizontal (gradient zero), the graph has a maximum or minimum.
  • Area under the graph: the $y$-quantity multiplied by the $x$-quantity, with units to match. The area under a velocity–time graph is the displacement; under a force–extension graph, the work done; under a power–time graph, the energy. Split straight-line shapes into rectangles and triangles; for a curve, count squares and multiply by what one square represents.
  • Intercepts: where the line crosses an axis. The $y$-intercept is the value of $y$ when $x = 0$, and it often has a physical meaning (section 11).
  • Interpolation means reading a value between your data points, which is usually reliable. Extrapolation means extending the line beyond your data. It is less reliable, because the relationship may change: a spring stretched past its elastic limit no longer follows Hooke's law.

Worked example: tangent and area

(a) Use the tangent on the left-hand graph to find the velocity at $t = 3.0$ s.
The tangent passes through $(1.5\ \text{s}, 0)$ and $(5.0\ \text{s}, 8.4\ \text{m})$, so $v = \dfrac{8.4 - 0}{5.0 - 1.5} = 2.4\ \text{m s}^{-1}$.

(b) Use the right-hand graph to find the displacement in the first 5.0 s.
The area is a trapezium: $\dfrac{1}{2}(2.0 + 6.0) \times 5.0 = 20$ m. The units are $\text{m s}^{-1} \times \text{s} = \text{m}$.

11. Linearising and logarithms

Straight-line graphs are the easiest to analyse: you can see at a glance whether the data fit, and the gradient and intercept are easy to measure. Compare your equation with $y = mx + c$ and choose what to plot so that you get a straight line. The gradient and intercept then give physical quantities.

RelationshipPlotGradient
$F = kx$ (spring)$F$ against $x$$k$
$s = \frac{1}{2}gt^2$ (falling from rest)$s$ against $t^2$$\frac{g}{2}$
$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{l}{g}}$ (pendulum)$T^2$ against $l$$\frac{4\pi^2}{g}$
$pV = nRT$ at constant $T$ (gas)$p$ against $\frac{1}{V}$$nRT$
$v = u + at$$v$ against $t$$a$ (intercept $u$)

In the first four, the line should pass through the origin. An intercept that should be zero but isn't usually means a systematic error (such as a zero error) or a missing effect (such as friction). That's useful information, not a failure.

Exponentials: plot the natural log

An exponential decrease, such as $A = A_0e^{-\lambda t}$ for radioactive decay, gives a curve. Taking natural logs of both sides turns it into a straight line:

$$\ln A = \ln A_0 - \lambda t$$

This matches $y = c + mx$, so a graph of $\ln A$ against $t$ is a straight line with gradient $-\lambda$ and $y$-intercept $\ln A_0$.

Worked example: the decay constant from a log graph

The straight line on the right-hand graph goes from $\ln A = 6.68$ at $t = 0$ to $\ln A = 3.89$ at $t = 8.0$ min. Find the decay constant and the starting activity.

Gradient $= \dfrac{3.89 - 6.68}{8.0 - 0} = -0.35\ \text{min}^{-1}$, so $\lambda = 0.35\ \text{min}^{-1}$.

Intercept: $\ln A_0 = 6.68$, so $A_0 = e^{6.68} = 8.0\times10^{2}$ Bq.

Power laws: plot log against log

If you suspect $y = kx^{n}$ but don't know $n$, take logs of both sides:

$$\ln y = \ln k + n\ln x$$

A graph of $\ln y$ against $\ln x$ is a straight line with gradient $n$ and intercept $\ln k$. Measuring the gradient tells you the power.

Worked example: finding a power

The period $T$ of a mass $m$ on a spring is thought to follow $T = km^{n}$. A log–log graph of the data is a straight line through $(\ln 0.10, \ln 0.63)$ and $(\ln 0.40, \ln 1.26)$, with $m$ in kg and $T$ in s. Find $n$.

$n = \dfrac{\ln 1.26 - \ln 0.63}{\ln 0.40 - \ln 0.10} = \dfrac{0.231 - (-0.462)}{-0.916 - (-2.303)} = \dfrac{0.693}{1.386} = 0.50$.

So $T \propto \sqrt{m}$, as the theory for a mass on a spring predicts (C.1).

Logarithmic scales

On a logarithmic scale, each equal step on the axis multiplies the value by the same factor, usually 10: the marks read 1, 10, 100, 1000 rather than 1, 2, 3, 4. Use one when your data cover several orders of magnitude, such as the luminosities of stars on a Hertzsprung–Russell diagram (E.5), which range from about $10^{-4}$ to $10^{6}$ times the Sun's. A power law plotted on log scales on both axes appears as a straight line.

Maths link: linearising with logs AA SL 1.5, 1.7

Taking logs uses the rules $\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b$ and $\ln(a^{n}) = n\ln a$. In maths, the same idea is used to find the parameters of exponential and power models from data.

12. Uncertainty in the gradient and intercept

To find the uncertainty in a gradient:

  1. Draw uncertainty bars on every point, then the line of best fit. Find its gradient and intercept.
  2. Draw the steepest line that still passes through every uncertainty bar, and the shallowest such line. These are the lines of maximum and minimum gradient.
  3. Find the gradient and intercept of each.
  4. The uncertainty is half the difference: $\Delta m = \dfrac{m_{\max} - m_{\min}}{2}$ and $\Delta c = \dfrac{c_{\max} - c_{\min}}{2}$.

Worked example: the spring constant

From the graph above, the best-fit line has gradient 0.51 N cm⁻¹ and an intercept of about 0. The maximum-gradient line has gradient 0.55 N cm⁻¹ and intercept −0.13 N. The minimum-gradient line has gradient 0.46 N cm⁻¹ and intercept +0.16 N.

$\Delta k = \dfrac{0.55 - 0.46}{2} = 0.05\ \text{N cm}^{-1}$, so $k = (0.51 \pm 0.05)\ \text{N cm}^{-1} = (51 \pm 5)\ \text{N m}^{-1}$.

Intercept: $\Delta c = \dfrac{0.16 - (-0.13)}{2} = 0.1$ N, so $c = (0.0 \pm 0.1)$ N. The intercept is consistent with zero, as Hooke's law predicts. There's no sign of a zero error.

Part 4 Vectors

Quantities with a direction: how to draw them, add them and split them into components.

13. Vectors

A scalar has size (magnitude) only. A vector has magnitude and direction.

VectorsScalars
displacement, velocity, accelerationdistance, speed, time
force (including weight), momentum, impulsemass, energy, work, power
electric, magnetic and gravitational field strengthtemperature, density, pressure, electric potential

A vector is drawn as an arrow: its length shows the magnitude and the arrowhead shows the direction. Two vectors are equal if they have the same magnitude and direction, wherever they are drawn. Multiplying a vector by a scalar changes its length; multiplying by a negative number also reverses it.

Adding and subtracting vectors

For two perpendicular vectors, use Pythagoras for the magnitude and trigonometry for the direction. For other angles, draw an accurate scale diagram (below), or resolve each vector into components, add the components, and recombine. Both methods work for three or more vectors: just keep adding tip to tail.

Scale diagrams

A scale diagram solves a vector problem by accurate drawing:

  1. Choose a scale that makes the diagram large, and write it down, for example "1 cm represents 10 N".
  2. Draw each vector to scale, tip to tail, using a ruler and a protractor for the angles.
  3. Draw the resultant from the start of the first vector to the end of the last. Measure its length and convert with the scale; measure its angle with the protractor.

A careful drawing is accurate to within a few per cent, which is often good enough and is a useful check on a calculation.

Resolving a vector into components

Any vector can be split into two perpendicular components. For a vector $A$ at angle $\theta$ to the $x$-axis:

$$A_x = A\cos\theta \qquad A_y = A\sin\theta$$

and to rebuild the vector from its components:

$$A = \sqrt{A_x^2 + A_y^2} \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{A_y}{A_x}$$

Signs matter. A component pointing left or down is negative. When you rebuild a vector, find the angle from $\tan\theta = \left|\frac{A_y}{A_x}\right|$, then use a quick sketch to decide which quadrant it points into. Your calculator can't tell the difference between "left and down" and "right and up".

Worked example: components and back again

(a) A rope pulls on a crate with a force of 25 N at 40° above the horizontal. Find the horizontal and vertical components.
$F_x = 25\cos 40° = 19$ N (horizontal),   $F_y = 25\sin 40° = 16$ N (vertical).

(b) A force has components $F_x = -3.0$ N and $F_y = +4.0$ N. Find its magnitude and direction.
$F = \sqrt{3.0^2 + 4.0^2} = 5.0$ N.   $\tan\theta = \dfrac{4.0}{3.0}$, so $\theta = 53°$.
$F_x$ is negative and $F_y$ is positive, so the force points up and to the left: 53° above the negative $x$-axis.

Free-body diagrams

A free-body diagram shows one object and every force acting on it, and nothing else:

  • Draw each force as an arrow starting at its point of application: weight from the centre of mass, the normal force and friction where the surfaces touch, tension where the rope is attached. For a particle, all the arrows can start from one point.
  • Make the lengths roughly show the sizes, and label each force with its name or symbol.
  • Leave out forces the object exerts on other things.

Maths link: trigonometry AA SL 3.2, 3.3

Resolving and recombining vectors is right-angled trigonometry (SOH CAH TOA) and Pythagoras. Adding two vectors that aren't at right angles means solving a non-right-angled triangle, which you can do with the cosine rule $c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C$ and the sine rule. Make sure your calculator is in degrees for angles given in degrees, and in radians for circular motion and SHM.

14. Common mistakes

  • Leaving out units, or the uncertainty, from a final answer.
  • Forgetting to convert non-SI units (eV, kWh, minutes, cm) before substituting into an equation.
  • Copying every digit from the calculator. Round the uncertainty to 1 s.f. (2 only if you know it that well) and match the value to it.
  • Quoting an uncertainty smaller than you could really judge, such as ±1 mm for a position read from blurry video, or ±0.01 s for hand timing.
  • Subtracting uncertainties when the quantities are subtracted. Absolute uncertainties always add.
  • Adding absolute uncertainties when multiplying or dividing. Use fractional or percentage uncertainties instead.
  • Forgetting to multiply by the power, for example doubling the fractional uncertainty for $T^2$.
  • Thinking repeats remove a systematic error. They only reduce random errors.
  • Calling a straight line that misses the origin "proportional". Proportional means a straight line through the origin.
  • Finding a gradient from two data points rather than from the best-fit line, or using a small triangle.
  • Forcing a best-fit line through the origin. Let the data decide; the intercept is information.
  • Extrapolating far beyond your data as if the relationship must carry on.
  • Mixing up accuracy and precision. Accurate means close to the true value; precise means close together.
  • Losing the sign of a vector component, or having the calculator in the wrong angle mode.

15. Check your understanding

How many significant figures are there in 0.003 050?

Four: 3, 0, 5 and the final 0. The leading zeros don't count. In scientific notation it's $3.050\times10^{-3}$.

Estimate the number of seconds in a human lifetime of 80 years, to the nearest order of magnitude.

$80 \times 3.2\times10^{7}\ \text{s} \approx 2.5\times10^{9}$ s. Since 2.5 is less than about 3, the order of magnitude is $10^{9}$ s.

A gamma photon has an energy of 2.5 MeV. What is its energy in joules?

$2.5\times10^{6} \times 1.60\times10^{-19} = 4.0\times10^{-13}$ J.

The current through a lamp rises from 0.20 A to 0.26 A. What is the percentage change?

$\dfrac{0.26 - 0.20}{0.20}\times100\% = +30\%$.

A measuring cylinder is marked every 2 cm³, and the liquid level is at 46 cm³. Record the volume with its uncertainty.

One reading on an analogue scale: half the smallest division, so $V = (46 \pm 1)\ \text{cm}^3$.

A length is measured as (50.0 ± 0.2) cm. A second length is (48.5 ± 0.2) cm. What is the difference between them, with its percentage uncertainty?

$50.0 - 48.5 = 1.5$ cm, with uncertainty $0.2 + 0.2 = 0.4$ cm. That's a percentage uncertainty of $\frac{0.4}{1.5} = 27\%$, even though each length was measured to 0.4%. Subtracting similar values magnifies the percentage uncertainty.

The radius of a circle has an uncertainty of 3%. What is the percentage uncertainty in its area?

$A = \pi r^2$, so the fractional uncertainty doubles: 6%. $\pi$ is exact.

Every reading on a newton-meter is 0.3 N too high. Is this a random or a systematic error, and will repeating the measurements help?

Systematic (a zero error). Repeating won't help, because every reading is shifted the same way. Zero the meter before use, or subtract 0.3 N from every reading.

A graph of $\ln A$ against $t$ (in hours) for a radioactive sample is a straight line with gradient $-0.12\ \text{h}^{-1}$. What is the decay constant?

The gradient is $-\lambda$, so $\lambda = 0.12\ \text{h}^{-1}$.

What are the units of the area under a graph of power (in W) against time (in s), and what does the area represent?

$\text{W} \times \text{s} = \text{J}$: the area is the energy transferred.

A vector has components $A_x = -6.0$ and $A_y = -8.0$. Find its magnitude, and describe its direction.

$A = \sqrt{6.0^2 + 8.0^2} = 10$.   $\tan\theta = \frac{8.0}{6.0}$, so $\theta = 53°$. Both components are negative, so it points left and down: 53° below the negative $x$-axis.
